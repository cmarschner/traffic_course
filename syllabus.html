    Syllabus

    Course syllabus

    15 modules · 4 parts plus capstone · ≈ 74 study hours · self-paced or as a 12-week semester

    The course moves from what to optimize (Part I), to how people respond (Part II), to how traffic physically behaves (Part III), to the levers a society can pull (Part IV). It ends with a capstone in which you appraise a real corridor with the full welfare objective.

    Aims

    By the end of the course you should be able to:

    1. Write down, defend and criticize an explicit objective for a transport plan, and recognize the implicit objective behind a standard, a model or a political slogan.
    2. Estimate each term of the welfare objective \(W\) with standard methods: demand and choice models, traffic-flow and capacity analysis, external-cost valuation, safety analysis and land-use effects.
    3. Read and compare the professional guidance of the US, Germany, Japan, Singapore, the Netherlands, Sweden and France, including key documents available only in their original languages.
    4. Build, calibrate and above all verify a traffic model, and say what it can and cannot tell you about \(W\).
    5. Carry out a transparent appraisal of alternatives, reporting the full vector of effects, Pareto dominance and sensitivity to contested values.

    Prerequisites

    Comfort with algebra, basic calculus and probability (expected values, the logit function), and reading a spreadsheet or short script. No prior transport background is needed. Engineers will find Parts I and IV the newest material, and economists and planners Part III.

    Six-week intensive for practising engineers

    Week 1: Modules 1–3. Week 2: Modules 4–5. Week 3: Modules 6, 11, 12. Week 4: Module 13 and 14. Week 5: skim 7–9 and focus on Module 10 (verification). Week 6: capstone.

    Economics- and policy-focused track

    Modules 1–6, 11, 13, 14, 15, reading only sections 1 and the "connection to \(W\)" parts of Modules 7–10.

    Core open resources

    If you bookmark only a handful of places, these carry much of the course:

    • TU Delft OCW — Traffic Flow Theory and Simulation Hoogendoorn and colleagues: fundamental diagrams, shockwaves, queues, capacity, simulation (Modules 7–10).
    • Martin Treiber — Traffic Flow Dynamics lecture slides and traffic-simulation.de Car-following, lane changing and stability, with interactive simulators (Module 7).
    • MIT OCW 1.201J — Transportation Systems Analysis: Demand and Economics Demand, discrete choice, economics and pricing (Modules 2–4, 13).
    • FHWA — Traffic Analysis Toolbox Volume III (2019 update) Practical microsimulation calibration (Module 10).
    • European Commission — Handbook on the External Costs of Transport Unit values for Term C (Module 3).
    • ITF/OECD — Urban Access Framework Accessibility as the organizing objective (Modules 1, 5).
    • Sources & translations English digests of key Swedish, German, Japanese, French, Dutch, Spanish and Italian originals.
