    Syllabus

    Course syllabus

    15 modules · 4 parts plus capstone · ≈ 74 study hours · self-paced or as a 12-week semester

    The course moves from what to optimize (Part I), to how people respond (Part II), to how traffic physically behaves (Part III), to the levers a society can pull (Part IV). It ends with a capstone in three variants: a decision memo, a transparent appraisal, or a citizen proposal.

    The course is written for three kinds of readers: people who decide (mayors, councillors, ministers and their staff), urban and transport planners, and informed citizens. Each has a reading path below.

    Aims

    By the end of the course you should be able to:

    1. Write down, defend and criticize an explicit objective for a transport plan, and recognize the implicit objective behind a standard, a model or a political slogan.
    2. Question a traffic study or a model: which objective it serves, which numbers are contested and how much they move the result, and whether the model was verified. Run simple what-if experiments with open tools.
    3. Estimate each term of the welfare objective \(W\) with standard methods: demand and choice models, traffic-flow and capacity analysis, external-cost valuation, safety analysis and land-use effects.
    4. Read and compare the professional guidance of the US, Germany, Japan, Singapore, the Netherlands, Sweden and France, including key documents available only in their original languages.
    5. Build, calibrate and above all verify a traffic model, and say what it can and cannot tell you about \(W\).
    6. Carry out a transparent appraisal of alternatives, reporting the full vector of effects, Pareto dominance and sensitivity to contested values.
    7. Put forward an alternative for a street, a corridor or a policy in terms of the objective, so that it can be compared fairly with the official proposal.

    Prerequisites

    No transport background is needed. Each module has three layers:

    • an In brief box at the top, for a reader with five minutes;
    • the main text, which carries the argument with stories, charts and worked numbers. It needs no more than school mathematics: percentages, averages and reading a chart;
    • collapsible Under the hood boxes with derivations and model details. These assume algebra, basic calculus and probability, and the ability to read a spreadsheet or a short script.

    Engineers will find Parts I and IV the newest material, and economists and planners Part III.

    Reading paths

    For people who decide (about 10 hours)

    1. The introduction and Module 1, main text only.
    2. The In brief box of every module, to see the whole field in an hour.
    3. The main text of the modules closest to your decisions. Most decision makers need 3 (prices of harms), 5 (access, land use and induced traffic), 10 (how to question a model), 11 (safety), 13 (pricing) and 14 (appraisal).
    4. Two country pages: one close to your own tradition, one far from it.
    5. The decision-memo variant of the capstone, on a project in front of you.

    For informed citizens (about 9 hours)

    1. The citizen toolkit: reading a study, questions for a hearing, building an alternative, and where decisions can be influenced in your country.
    2. The introduction and Module 1, main text only.
    3. The main text of 5 (access), 6 (transit, walking and cycling), 11 (safety) and 12 (street design).
    4. From Module 10, the sections on questioning a model and running your own what-if.
    5. The page for your country, and its translated sources in Sources & translations.
    6. The citizen-proposal variant of the capstone, on a street or corridor you know.

    For planners (the full course, about 74 hours)

    All modules in order, including the Under the hood boxes and the exercises, on one of the schedules below. The capstone is a transparent appraisal of a real corridor with the full welfare objective.

    Core open resources

    If you bookmark only a handful of places, these carry much of the course:

    • TU Delft OCW — Traffic Flow Theory and Simulation Hoogendoorn and colleagues: fundamental diagrams, shockwaves, queues, capacity, simulation (Modules 7–10).
    • Martin Treiber — Traffic Flow Dynamics lecture slides and traffic-simulation.de Car-following, lane changing and stability, with interactive simulators (Module 7).
    • MIT OCW 1.201J — Transportation Systems Analysis: Demand and Economics Demand, discrete choice, economics and pricing (Modules 2–4, 13).
    • FHWA — Traffic Analysis Toolbox Volume III (2019 update) Practical microsimulation calibration (Module 10).
    • European Commission — Handbook on the External Costs of Transport Unit values for Term C (Module 3).
    • ITF/OECD — Urban Access Framework Accessibility as the organizing objective (Modules 1, 5).
    • Sources & translations English digests of key Swedish, German, Japanese, French, Dutch, Spanish and Italian originals.
