    Part I · The objective · Module 3

    Externalities and their prices

    ≈ 4 hours · Prerequisites: Modules 1 and 2 · Key terms: C, D, links to B and the limits

    Term C of the welfare objective subtracts the harm that travel does to people who did not choose it: the child crossing the road, the family under the flight path of a motorway, the people who will live with a warmer climate. Module 1 wrote it as a sum, \(C=\sum_k p_k E_k\): for each kind of harm \(k\), a physical quantity \(E_k\) times a price per unit \(p_k\). This module is about the prices. Where does a number like "€3.6 million per death" or "€100 per tonne of CO₂" come from? Why do countries use such different numbers? And which of them should a decision maker refuse to take on trust?

    In brief
    The question
    What does a trip cost the people who did not make it, and how can that cost be priced fairly?
    What matters
    Crashes, climate, air pollution, noise, severance and land. Each enters as a quantity times a unit value. Unit values are built from surveys, health studies and policy targets: they are choices informed by evidence, not constants of nature. Congestion is not on the list. The delay drivers cause each other is already in everyone's travel times (Term A). It sets the right congestion charge instead.
    Numbers to question
    The value of a statistical life (€3.6 million in the EU reference, about $14 million in US guidance). The carbon value and how fast it rises. Air-pollution damage per kilogram, which differs fivefold between metropolitan and rural areas. Whether a value is marginal or average. The price year.
    Levers
    Choose between pricing a harm and setting a limit on it. Use values that depend on place and time. Charge the marginal harm where it occurs. Lower speeds and better street design reduce the quantities themselves.
    Ask your analysts
    Which unit values did you use, from which source, in which price year? Marginal or average? Did you count the harm caused by the extra traffic the project attracts? Is any delay counted twice? Does the ranking survive a low and a high carbon value?
    Learning outcomes
    • Decide whether a cost is external, and whether it is already counted in Term A.
    • Explain the three ways a unit value is built (damage, avoidance and replacement cost), and how a value of a statistical life is derived from small risks.
    • Distinguish marginal from average costs, and say which one a given decision needs.
    • Use the EU Handbook on the External Costs of Transport and national values (France, Germany, Sweden, US, Japan) critically, and explain why the values differ between countries.
    • Explain why congestion and crowding are not subtracted in Term C, and what the external delay is used for instead.
    • Name the unit values that move an appraisal most, and test a ranking against them.

    1. Who pays for a car trip? C

    Take a 10 km drive across a European city at midday, alone, in a new petrol car. The driver pays for the fuel and spends the time. Others pay too, without being asked. Pedestrians and cyclists carry a small risk of being hit. People along the route breathe a little exhaust and hear a little more noise. Everyone, now and later, inherits a little more CO₂.

    The European Commission's Handbook on the External Costs of Transport puts numbers on these shares. For a passenger-km in a new, fuel-efficient petrol car in dense city traffic by day, it lists 1.41 euro cents for crashes, 1.00 for climate, 0.5 for noise and 0.14 for air pollution. Over the 10 km trip that is about 30 cents of harm to others. The Handbook lists one more item: 41.2 cents per passenger-km, about €4 for the trip, for the delay the car causes to other drivers (Handbook, Table 125).

    This module is mostly about the 30 cents: where each number comes from, and how much it can be trusted. The €4 is real too, but it is a different kind of cost. It falls on other travelers, whose travel times the model already counts. Section 9 explains where it belongs.

    What makes a cost external

    A cost is external if the person whose decision causes it does not bear it and does not take it into account. The test is about decisions, not physics. My exhaust harms me a little and my neighbors a lot. The table sorts the harms of Term C into what the traveler already weighs and what falls on others:

    CategoryInternal part (already in Term A)External part (Term C)
    CrashesOwn risk, as far as perceived; insurance premiumsRisk imposed on others (especially pedestrians and cyclists); medical, police and other costs paid by society
    ClimateFuel cost, including fuel and carbon taxes (a transfer, not a cost to society)Damage, or avoidance cost, of each tonne of CO₂-equivalent
    Air pollutionA little own exposureHealth, crop and material damage to others
    NoiseAlmost noneAnnoyance, sleep disturbance, heart disease among residents
    SeverancePedestrians' detours and waiting, if the model includes walking tripsWhat the model misses: trips not made, children kept indoors, neighbors who no longer meet
    Land and parkingParking fees, if anyRent of land given free or below cost (Term D, section 10)
    Congestion and crowdingBoth the own delay and the delay imposed on others are in Term A, through everyone's modeled travel times. Not subtracted again in C (section 9)

    Two accounting rules from Module 1 apply throughout. First, count each harm once, in the place where the person who bears it is counted. Second, taxes are not costs. A fuel tax is a transfer from driver to government. It lowers the driver's surplus in Term A and arrives as revenue \(R\) in Term E, and the two cancel. A tax reduces harm only if it changes behavior.

    2. How a unit value is built

    Nobody sells clean air or a quiet night, so there is no market price to look up. Analysts use three approaches instead. The EU Handbook describes them as follows.

    1. Damage cost. Trace the harm to the people who suffer it, and ask what they would pay to avoid it. For air pollution, this is a chain: emissions, then the concentrations they cause, then the people exposed, then the extra illness and deaths, then a money value for each. Most economists prefer this approach. The Handbook uses it for crashes, air pollution and noise.
    2. Avoidance cost. Start from a target that society has already set, such as a cut in CO₂ emissions. Find the cheapest way to meet it. The cost of the last tonne avoided is the value of a tonne. This is the shadow price of the target (Module 1). The Handbook uses it for climate.
    3. Replacement cost. Value a harm at what it costs to repair, such as cleaning soiled buildings. Simple, but it says nothing about what people would pay.

    "What people would pay" is measured in two ways. In stated-preference surveys, people choose between hypothetical options, for example a safer and a cheaper route. In revealed-preference studies, analysts read values from real markets. House prices along noisy and quiet streets, for example, show what buyers pay for quiet.

    The value of a statistical life

    The most contested unit value is the price of a road death. It is not the value of a particular person's life. It is built from small risks, like this.

    Suppose a measure lowers each person's yearly risk of dying in a crash by 1 in 10,000, and each of 10,000 people would pay €360 a year for it. Together they would pay €3.6 million. On average, the measure saves one life among them each year. We do not know whose. So €3.6 million is the value of a statistical life (VSL): what a group is willing to pay, in total, for risk reductions that together prevent one death.

    Two methods produce such values, and they give different answers.

    • Surveys ask people directly what they would pay for safer products or roads. Most European values come from these. The EU Handbook's €3.6 million (2016 euros) comes from the OECD's 2012 review of such surveys worldwide.
    • Wage–risk studies compare jobs. If a riskier job pays more, the extra pay shows what workers demand for accepting the risk. Most US values come from these studies. The Handbook notes that they tend to give much higher values than surveys.

    Injuries are valued as fractions of the VSL. The Handbook values a serious injury at 13 % and a slight injury at 1 % of the VSL. France uses 12.5 % and 0.5 %.

    Moving values between countries

    Few countries run their own surveys for every harm. Most borrow values from studies elsewhere and adjust them for income. This is called benefit transfer. People with higher incomes are willing to pay more to avoid a risk, so a value from a richer country is scaled down, and one from a poorer country scaled up.

    The scaling is governed by one number, the income elasticity: the percentage by which the value rises when income rises by 1 %. The EU Handbook uses 0.8, following an OECD review that found 0.7 to 0.9 for health and environmental values. So a country with half the average income gets a value about 43 % lower (\(0.5^{0.8}\approx 0.57\)). That is why the Handbook's VSL is the same method everywhere in the EU but a different number in each country.

    Under the hoodThe transfer formula, and what is inside the Handbook's crash cost

    Benefit transfer. With willingness to pay \(\mathit{WTP}_s\) at the study site, income \(I_s\) there and \(I_p\) in the policy country, the transferred value is

    $$\mathit{WTP}_p = \mathit{WTP}_s\left(\frac{I_p}{I_s}\right)^{\varepsilon},$$

    with income measured as GDP per head at purchasing-power parity and \(\varepsilon = 0.8\) in the EU Handbook. The same formula, applied over time instead of across places, is how manuals let values grow with real income.

    Inside a crash cost. The Handbook splits the cost of a fatality into human costs (from the VSL), medical, administrative and production costs, and material damage. To avoid counting lost production twice, it deducts the victim's lost consumption from the VSL: on average €668,000, so the human cost of a fatality in the EU28 is €2.9 million. It then decides which parts are external. Human costs of people in other vehicles are fully external; those of the car's own occupants are internal. Half of the medical and administrative costs are assumed to be uninsured and thus external, and 55 % of the production loss. Germany's federal transport infrastructure plan (BVWP) makes a similar split: of its €2.48 million per fatality (2012), €1.16 million are resource costs and €1.32 million a willingness-to-pay "risk value".

    3. Marginal or average?

    A unit value can mean two different things. The average cost is the total harm divided by all the traffic. The marginal cost is the extra harm from one more vehicle, at a given place and time. They can differ by a large factor, and each answers a different question.

    • Noise. Because decibels are logarithmic, one more car on a road with 20,000 vehicles a day adds almost nothing. One more car on a quiet street at night adds a lot. The marginal cost is highest exactly where the average cost is lowest (section 7).
    • Crashes. More traffic means more encounters, but also slower, more careful driving. The Handbook assumes that on urban roads one more vehicle leaves everyone else's risk per kilometer unchanged, and that on motorways and other roads it slightly lowers it. The harm one more vehicle does to others is then mainly the risk it brings itself, not a rise in everyone else's risk.
    • Air pollution. The damage per kilogram is roughly the same for the first and the last kilogram in a place, but it varies strongly between places (section 6).
    • Delay between drivers. At a near-full road, one more car adds much more delay to others than the average car does. The Handbook puts the total delay on Europe's roads in 2016 at €271 billion, compared with free-flowing traffic. The part that pricing could actually recover, the economists' deadweight loss, is about €46 billion. (As section 9 explains, neither is subtracted in Term C.)

    The rule of use: charges and per-vehicle values should be marginal, for the right place and time. Totals and averages answer a different question: whether a mode, as a whole, pays for the harm it causes. A project appraisal is best done a third way: compute the change in each physical quantity (tonnes, people exposed, expected deaths) between the scenarios, and multiply by a price per unit of harm. Then the marginal-or-average question disappears for the quantities, and only the price per unit remains.

    4. The European reference: the Handbook on External Costs

    The European Commission's Handbook on the External Costs of Transport (version 2019 – 1.1, published 2020, prepared by a consortium led by CE Delft for DG MOVE) is the most complete public source of unit values. It gives total, average and marginal costs by mode, country and traffic situation, in 2016 euros.

    Its headline: the external costs of transport in the EU28 in 2016 were €716 billion, 4.8 % of GDP, excluding congestion. Accidents are the largest category, followed by climate change and air pollution, then noise, well-to-tank emissions (from producing fuel and electricity) and habitat damage. Road transport causes 83 % of the total, including aviation and shipping. The Handbook adds €271 billion of road delay, for a total of €987 billion (6.6 % of GDP). For the reasons in section 9, this course reads the €716 billion as the Term C total, and the €271 billion as a measure of something else.

    Its key inputs are a VSL of €3.6 million for the EU28, and a climate value of €100 per tonne of CO₂-equivalent up to 2030 (range 60–189), rising to €269 for 2040–2060 (range 156–498), all in 2016 euros.

    5. Climate C limits

    The quantity

    Tailpipe CO₂ is almost exactly proportional to fuel burned, because nearly all the carbon in the fuel ends up as CO₂. That is roughly 2.3 kg per liter of gasoline and 2.6–2.7 kg per liter of diesel. Emissions per vehicle-km therefore depend on speed, acceleration, gradient, load and vehicle technology. The standard European tools are the Handbook Emission Factors for Road Transport (HBEFA), used in Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Sweden, Norway and France, and COPERT, the model behind most EU national inventories and the EMEP/EEA emission inventory guidebook. Both give factors by vehicle type, emission standard and traffic situation. For appraisal, add the emissions from producing fuel and electricity. They can dominate for battery-electric cars on a fossil-heavy grid (Module 1, "How electric cars change Term C").

    The price: what countries use

    Module 1 explains the two ways to put a price on a tonne of CO₂: the damage it causes (the social cost of carbon), or the cost of meeting a target (its shadow price). It also lists the current scientific and official estimates. The table here shows what national appraisal manuals actually use.

    Why the values differ

    Among the values in force, the highest in the table (UBA's €1,000) is ten times the lowest (the EU Handbook's €100 up to 2030). Five choices explain most of the gap, and each can be put to an analyst as a question.

    1. Damage or target? France and the EU Handbook price the target. Germany's environment agency prices the damage. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions (Module 1).
    2. How much the future counts. Most climate damage happens decades from now. UBA's value triples, from €350 to €1,000, when all generations are weighted equally instead of discounting the future at 1 % a year.
    3. Whose damage counts. A value that counts only damage within one country is far lower than one that counts damage worldwide. The US switched to "domestic-only" values in 2017 (below).
    4. Which year. Most values rise over time. Germany's BVWP held its value constant over the whole appraisal; France lets it grow 4.5 % a year after 2040. Over a 40-year road project, that difference alone can matter more than the starting value.
    5. Price year. €100 in 2016 euros and €350 in 2025 euros are not directly comparable. Convert before comparing.

    "The valuation of carbon dioxide emissions shall be reflected in the gasoline and diesel prices that are assumptions for the traffic forecasts [...].     Source: Trafikverket, ASEK 8.0 (2024), recommendation AR 14.3. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    The Swedish rule deserves a careful reading in terms of \(W\). The forecast assumes fuel prices high enough to meet the 2045 target. That price lowers travelers' surplus in Term A, but as a tax it is a transfer, so it is not itself the climate cost. What the rule really does is treat climate as a limit: the forecast assumes the target is met anyway, so a project's remaining emissions are not charged again. The consequence is worth discussing. A road project looks "climate-neutral" in the benefit–cost ratio, because the forecast assumes that climate policy succeeds whatever the project does. If the policy does not happen, the project's emissions were never priced.

    History · The US social cost of carbon in five steps
    1. 2010. A federal Interagency Working Group (IWG) publishes a central value of about $21 per tonne of CO₂ for 2010 emissions (2007 dollars, 3 % discount rate).
    2. 2017. Executive Order 13783 disbands the IWG and withdraws its documents. Agencies switch to "domestic-only" interim values of a few dollars per tonne.
    3. 2021. The re-established IWG publishes interim values of $51/t for 2020 emissions (2020 dollars, 3 %).
    4. 2023. The EPA's updated estimate, using newer damage functions and a 2 % near-term discount rate, gives about $190/t for 2020 emissions (2020 dollars).
    5. 2025. Executive Order 14154, Unleashing American Energy (20 January 2025), disbands the IWG again and withdraws its estimates "as no longer representative of governmental policy".

    A factor of nine between the 2010 and 2023 values, and two withdrawals, show the central lesson of this module: a unit value is a policy choice informed by science, not a natural constant. Appraisals should test their ranking over the plausible range. A carbon budget, written as a limit, makes the result less dependent on any one number.

    6. Air pollution C

    Road traffic emits nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) and fine particles (PM₂.₅). The particles come from exhausts and, increasingly, from brakes, tires and road wear. Traffic also emits gases that later form ozone and further particles in the air. The standard valuation method is the damage-cost chain of section 2, developed in the EU's ExternE research projects and called the impact-pathway approach:

    $$\text{emission} \rightarrow \text{dispersion} \rightarrow \Delta\text{concentration} \rightarrow \Delta\text{exposure} \rightarrow \Delta\text{deaths, illness} \rightarrow \text{€}.$$

    The fourth step is decisive: the damage depends on how many people breathe the air near the source. For Austria, the EU Handbook gives a PM₂.₅ damage cost from transport of €466 per kg in metropolitan areas, €151 in other cities and €87 in rural areas (€2016). A kilogram emitted in a metropolitan area does more than five times the damage of one emitted in a rural area. A single value per vehicle-km that ignores location is close to meaningless for urban decisions.

    The World Health Organization's 2021 guideline for annual PM₂.₅ is 5 µg/m³. It is far below the EU limit values that applied until the 2024 revision of the Ambient Air Quality Directive, which tightens them from 2030.

    In \(W\), air pollution is the clearest case where the harm depends on where a plan puts traffic. A bypass that moves the same vehicle-km from a town center to open country reduces the air-pollution cost, even if total emissions rise.

    7. Noise C

    Sound levels are measured in decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale. Doubling the traffic adds about 3 dB. Halving the distance to a busy road also adds about 3 dB. Above roughly 30–40 km/h, the noise of tires on the road dominates engine noise in cars. That is why speed limits and road surfaces matter more than engine technology for urban car noise. European noise maps under the Environmental Noise Directive 2002/49/EC use a day-evening-night level \(L_{\text{den}}\), which adds 5 dB to evening and 10 dB to night-time noise to reflect how much more it disturbs.

    The WHO's Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region (2018) strongly recommend keeping road-traffic noise below 53 dB \(L_{\text{den}}\) and 45 dB at night, based on the evidence for annoyance, sleep disturbance and heart disease. Valuation multiplies the number of people exposed by a price per decibel above a threshold. The EU Handbook's values for road traffic, combining annoyance and health, rise from €17 per dB per person per year in the 50–54 dB band to €63 at 65–69 dB and €72 at 75 dB or above (€2016).

    Marginal noise cost is low where it is already loud

    One more car on a road with 20,000 vehicles a day adds almost nothing in decibels. One more car on a quiet residential street adds a lot. Marginal noise costs are therefore highest at night and in quiet areas, which is the opposite of where total noise costs are concentrated. Routing and charging should use marginal values. Decisions about noise barriers and speed limits on busy roads should use total exposure.

    8. Crashes C limits

    The quantity

    Crash severity is governed by the energy that has to be absorbed, \(\tfrac12 mv^2\), and by how much the human body can tolerate. Two regularities are central to Module 11: pedestrian fatality risk rises steeply with impact speed between about 30 and 60 km/h, and Nilsson's "power model" links changes in mean speed to changes in deaths with an exponent of about 4 (Module 1). The number of crashes on a road is usually modeled from exposure (vehicle-km, conflicting flows at junctions) with statistical relations called safety performance functions. Germany's federal transport infrastructure plan (BVWP) and the appraisal manual of Japan's transport ministry (MLIT) both use cost rates per vehicle-km that differ by road type. Japan's manual gives a separate formula for each combination of road class, urban area and median.

    What is external?

    Three parts of crash costs are external. The first is the risk imposed on other road users, which is largest when masses and speeds differ: a car is a small risk to its occupants and a large one to pedestrians. The second is costs paid by society rather than by those involved: emergency services, uninsured health care, lost production and taxes. The third is the change in everyone else's risk when one more vehicle joins (section 3).

    9. Congestion and crowding: counted in Term A

    The Handbook's 41 cents per passenger-km for city congestion (section 1) dwarfs every other harm on its list. Why, then, is congestion not part of Term C in this course?

    The answer is in Module 1, "Where is congestion?". In short: when one more driver joins a busy road, every other driver loses a little time. That loss is real. But Term A is computed from everyone's travel times as the model predicts them, with all the traffic on the road. So the other drivers' lost minutes are already in Term A. Subtracting them again in Term C would count them twice. The same holds for crowding on buses and trains: the discomfort passengers cause each other is in Term A, through the crowding multipliers of Module 2.

    The delay a driver imposes on others still matters, for two other purposes.

    • It sets the right congestion charge. Each driver ignores the delay they cause others, so too many people drive at the peak. A charge equal to that delay, in money, makes each driver weigh it. This is the marginal external congestion cost, the basis of congestion pricing (Module 13).
    • It shows what pricing could gain. The deadweight loss, €46 billion a year in the Handbook's estimate for the EU28, is the welfare that ideal pricing could recover. The total delay compared with free flow, €271 billion, is not a loss anyone could avoid: no affordable network runs at free-flow speed all day.

    One exception. Some appraisals hold travel times fixed, for example a spreadsheet that multiplies extra vehicle-km by Handbook unit values without running a traffic model. Then the model's travel times do not show the extra delay, and the marginal congestion cost per vehicle-km must be added. The rule: count each delay once, either through modeled travel times in Term A, or as a separate per-km cost, never both.

    Under the hoodHow large is the delay one car imposes? A worked example on a link

    Let \(t(q)\) be the travel time on a road as a function of the flow \(q\), in vehicles per hour. Total travel time is \(q\,t(q)\). One more vehicle adds

    $$\frac{d\,[q\,t(q)]}{dq} = \underbrace{t(q)}_{\text{own time}} + \underbrace{q\,t'(q)}_{\text{delay to others}} .$$

    The first part is what the driver experiences and weighs. The second is the marginal external congestion cost. Both end up in Term A through the modeled travel times. The second is the basis of the charge.

    The US Bureau of Public Roads (BPR) link function is \(t(q)=t_0\left[1+0.15\,(q/Q)^4\right]\), with free-flow time \(t_0\) and capacity \(Q\). Then \(q\,t'(q)=t_0\cdot 0.15\cdot 4\,(q/Q)^4 = 4\,[t(q)-t_0]\): the delay imposed on others is four times each driver's own delay. Take \(t_0=10\) min, \(Q=2{,}000\) veh/h and a value of time of €15 per vehicle-hour (€0.25/min).

    10. Severance, land and parking C D

    Severance

    History · Three streets in San Francisco

    In the late 1960s the planner Donald Appleyard and his students compared three residential streets in San Francisco. They were alike in most respects except traffic: about 2,000, 8,000 and 16,000 vehicles a day. Residents of the light street knew their neighbors on both sides and used the street to talk and play. Residents of the heavy street kept to their houses. On the light street, residents counted about three times as many local friends and twice as many acquaintances as on the heavy street. Appleyard published the work in Livable Streets (1981).

    Sources: Project for Public Spaces, "Donald Appleyard"; Bruce Appleyard, "Livable Streets" presentation, Texas A&M Transportation Institute.

    A busy road is a barrier. People walk around it, children are not allowed to cross it, and neighbors on opposite sides meet less. This is severance, the least standardized category of Term C. Some guides value it through the extra walking and waiting at crossings, others through survey values, and many not at all.

    The rule of counting once applies here too. If the model includes walking trips and their crossing times, the detours and waits are already in pedestrians' Term A. If it also lets people change where they walk to, or whether they go at all, the trips no longer made are in Term A too. Term C should then hold only what the model misses: children kept indoors, older people who stop going out, the friendships Appleyard counted. Most models include none of this, which is why severance is so often invisible.

    Land

    Roads and parking occupy land that could hold homes, shops or parks. Its value is Term D, \(rL\), where \(L\) is the area and \(r\) its yearly rent in the best other use (Module 1). The unit value \(r\) can be read from market rents, or from land prices times an interest rate. It ranges from almost nothing at a city's edge to very large sums in its center. An urban parking space of 12–15 m², or roughly twice that with access aisles, can occupy land worth as much as the car parked on it.

    Count land once. Land bought for a new road goes either into the construction cost (Term E) as a purchase price, or into Term D as a yearly rent, never both. This course puts all land in D, so that roads built long ago and roads built tomorrow are treated alike.

    Land becomes an externality in the strict sense when its cost is shifted to others. Minimum parking requirements bundle the cost of parking into rents and prices, so people without cars pay for drivers' spaces. Free curb parking leads drivers to cruise in search of a space, which adds traffic. Donald Shoup's figure that about 30 % of downtown traffic was cruising came from a small set of older studies and has been contested; later measurements in many cities found much lower shares. Parking policy is developed in Module 5 and Module 13.

    11. From unit values to charges and health E B

    Charges

    Arthur Pigou's rule (1920; Module 1) says: charge each trip its marginal external cost, and travelers will weigh the harm they cause. In terms of \(W\), the charge lowers Term A for those who pay and arrives as revenue in Term E. As a transfer, it cancels, apart from the factor \(\mu\) when the revenue replaces distorting taxes. The welfare gain comes only from changed behavior: fewer or different trips, so the quantities \(E_k\) fall.

    Real charges are rough proxies for the marginal harm. Fuel taxes match CO₂ well, because emissions are proportional to fuel, but match noise and local air pollution poorly, because these depend on time and place. Distance-based truck tolls, such as Germany's Lkw-Maut with its CO₂ component since 2023, come closer. Whether existing taxes "cover" external costs on average is a common debate, but for efficiency only the marginal comparison at each place and time matters (section 3). Module 13 develops the instruments.

    The same values in Term B

    Walking and cycling bring health benefits that travelers largely ignore when choosing how to travel. This is Term B, the positive counterpart of Term C. The WHO's Health Economic Assessment Tool (HEAT) estimates the deaths avoided and values them with the same VSL as crashes. So the VSL chosen for Term C also scales Term B. Module 6 covers HEAT. One caution: if the mode-choice model already contains a constant for the enjoyment or perceived health of cycling, only the part travelers do not perceive belongs in Term B.

    12. Which values should a decision maker question?

    A decision maker does not need to rebuild the unit values. They need to know which ones move the result, and ask for the result under other values. Seven questions cover most of the risk.

    1. Which carbon value, and which path over time? Ask for the ranking at a low value (around €100 per tonne) and a high one (around €350, or the French path). If the ranking flips, the carbon value is the decision, and it should be made openly.
    2. Which value of a statistical life? A project that trades minutes for risk can look good at €2.5 million and bad at €5 million. Ask whether safety should be a limit instead.
    3. Are the values for the right place? Air-pollution and noise values should depend on how many people live nearby. National averages hide where the harm falls.
    4. Marginal or average? Charges need marginal values for the right place and time. Averages answer a different question.
    5. Was the harm of new traffic counted? A road that attracts extra traffic adds crashes, emissions and noise. If the model held demand fixed, these are missing.
    6. Is any delay counted twice? If the appraisal both runs a traffic model and adds a per-km congestion cost, the delay is in twice.
    7. Which price years and currencies? Values from different sources must be converted to one year and currency before they are added.

    Two further harms are often left out entirely: severance and land. An appraisal that prices them at zero is not neutral. It favors the modes and designs that use the most space.

    1. Find the unpriced equilibrium (demand price = own cost) numerically.
    2. Find the best flow (demand price = own cost + delay to others) and the charge that achieves it.
    3. Compute the deadweight loss of the unpriced equilibrium, and the "total delay cost" relative to free flow.
    4. Which of these numbers is already contained in Term A when the model computes everyone's travel time? Which one is the charge? Explain why neither is subtracted in Term C.
