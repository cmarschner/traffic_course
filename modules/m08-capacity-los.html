    Part III · Traffic engineering · Module 8

    Capacity, design hours and level of service — and its critics

    ≈ 4 hours, plus about 1 hour for the "Under the hood" boxes · Prerequisites: Module 1, Module 7 · Key terms: A C D E

    Every road is sized for some hour of the year. Which hour, and what quality of traffic is demanded in it, are among the most consequential choices in transport engineering. Yet they are usually made by convention rather than by analysis. This module tells where the best-known convention, the letter grades A to F of "level of service", came from, and what it optimizes. It explains how design volumes and grades are computed in the US, Germany, Spain and Japan, and how the UK and the Netherlands judge roads instead. It then examines how a grade meant to describe traffic became an objective, why California and many of its cities moved away from it, and what it is still good for. The central claim: choosing a design hour and a minimum grade is an implicit weighting of the terms of \(W\).

    In brief
    The question
    How big should a road or a junction be, and who decides what "good enough" traffic means?
    What matters
    Level of service grades the delay of motor vehicles at one place in one busy hour. It was introduced in the US Highway Capacity Manual of 1965 to describe the quality of traffic and let engineers choose a grade. Written into plans as a minimum ("LOS D or better"), it became an objective. It counts vehicles, not people, ignores induced traffic, and gives land and safety no weight.
    Numbers to question
    The design hour (the 30th busiest hour of the year is a US custom, not a law of nature); the minimum grade; the forecast volume and whether it includes traffic induced by the new capacity; and whether the cost of the land is counted.
    Levers
    Choose the design hour and the minimum grade per project; grade people, not vehicles, and all modes; replace delay with vehicle-kilometers or access as the test of a development's impact, as California did in 2020; or price the peak instead of building for it (Module 13).
    Ask your analysts
    Which hour is this road sized for, and why that one? What would a design for a lower hour save in money and land, and what would it cost in delay? How do pedestrians, cyclists and bus riders fare under each option?
    Learning outcomes
    • Compute average daily traffic from counts, build an hourly duration curve and derive a design-hour volume.
    • Explain where level of service came from (1950 and 1965 Highway Capacity Manuals) and what it measures and optimizes.
    • Explain how the HCM (US) and HBS (Germany) assign grades A–F, how Spain and Japan set design volumes, and how the UK and the Netherlands judge roads without letter grades.
    • State the main critiques of LOS as an objective, describe California's switch from LOS to VMT (SB 743) and multimodal LOS, and say where LOS remains useful.
    • Show that the choice of design hour trades off Term A (rare delay) against Terms D and E (land and money every day), and evaluate it explicitly.

    1. Counting traffic

    The basic demand statistic is average daily traffic over a year: AADT (annual average daily traffic) in the US, DTV (durchschnittliche tägliche Verkehrsstärke) in Germany, IMD (intensidad media diaria) in Spain, 年平均日交通量 in Japan. Only a small share of road sections have permanent counting stations. Most volumes are estimated from short counts, of a day or a week, scaled up to a yearly average. The scaling factors come from a "similar" permanent station: how traffic at that station varies by hour, by day of the week and by month.

    Under the hoodHow a short count becomes a yearly average

    Spain's Nota de Servicio 5/2014 writes the expansion as \(\text{IMD}=I_{24}\cdot N\cdot L\cdot S\). Here \(I_{24}\) is the 24-hour count, and \(N\), \(L\) and \(S\) are expansion factors taken from the estación afín (the matched permanent station). The US Traffic Monitoring Guide describes the same logic with hour-of-day, day-of-week and month-of-year factors.

    Two sources of error dominate: choosing the wrong matched station (a leisure route expanded with commuter factors), and counting demand where there is already a queue. A count downstream of a bottleneck measures throughput, not demand. If a road is congested in the peak, its counts understate the demand that a wider road would attract, before induced demand is even considered (Module 5).

    2. Duration curves and the design hour

    A road sized for the single busiest hour of the year would sit half-empty for most of the other 8,759. A road sized for an average hour would be jammed every weekday. Engineers therefore choose a design hour in between, and the choice is made with a simple picture. Rank the 8,760 hourly volumes of a year from highest to lowest and plot them as a share of AADT. This duration curve falls steeply over the first few dozen hours and then flattens. The \(n\)-th highest hour defines the factor

    $$K_n = \frac{q_{(n)}}{\text{AADT}}, \qquad \text{DHV} = K_n\cdot\text{AADT}\cdot D ,$$

    where DHV is the design-hour volume and \(D\) the share in the peak direction (giving the directional DDHV). FHWA's Traffic Data Computation Method Pocket Guide (2018) notes that two-way DHV is usually 8–12% of ADT and that US practice "typically" takes the 30th highest hour, a convention attributed to the Highway Performance Monitoring System. Its worked example shows why: on an urban highway the 1st, 30th, 50th and 100th hours were 3,992, 3,850, 3,735 and 3,355 veh/h. Urban duration curves are flat because the same commuter peak recurs every weekday; rural and recreational roads have spiky curves driven by holidays.

    Art. 2(22): "Planned traffic volume means the daily motor-vehicle traffic volume, determined… taking into account the development trend of the area in which the road is located, future motor-vehicle traffic conditions and the like, to serve as the basis for the design of the road."

    Art. 5(2)–(3), summarized: a road has two lanes if its planned traffic volume does not exceed the "design standard traffic volume (the maximum permissible motor-vehicle traffic volume)" for its class and terrain, for example 14,000 vehicles/day for a Class 1, Grade 2 road on level terrain, or 12,000 for an urban Class 4, Grade 1 street. Otherwise the number of lanes (four or more, normally even) follows from the ratio of planned traffic to a per-lane value, for example 12,000 vehicles/day per lane for a Class 1, Grade 1 expressway on level terrain, 18,000 for an urban expressway (Class 2, Grade 1) and 12,000 for a Class 4, Grade 1 urban street. For Class 4 streets with many intersections, the per-lane value is multiplied by 0.6. Source: e-Gov Japanese Law Search. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted); this is a statute and is translated and summarised more fully.

    The Japanese approach is instructive. It sizes roads from a daily volume and a fixed per-lane standard rather than from a chosen peak hour and a target grade. That is cruder hydraulically, but it makes the policy choice visible: the 0.6 multiplier for streets with many intersections is a statement that urban streets should be judged by different standards. The planned volume is a forecast, so the quality of the demand model, and whether it includes induced traffic, determines the result.

    3. Within the hour: the peak hour factor

    Traffic within the peak hour is not uniform. US practice sizes roads for the busiest quarter of an hour within the design hour. Take 1,800 vehicles in the hour, of which 500 come in the busiest quarter. That quarter runs at a rate of 4 × 500 = 2,000 vehicles per hour, and the road is checked against 2,000, not 1,800. The ratio of the two, 1,800/2,000 = 0.90, is called the peak hour factor. Typical values are 0.85–0.95. Values near 1 suggest that the count was limited by capacity, so that demand was higher than what was counted.

    National methods differ even here. GIRABASE, the French roundabout tool, explicitly does not include a peak factor (Module 9).

    Under the hoodThe peak hour factor as a formula $$\text{PHF} = \frac{V_{60}}{4\,V_{15,\max}},$$

    where \(V_{60}\) is the hourly volume and \(V_{15,\max}\) the highest 15-minute volume in that hour. The HCM converts hourly volumes to peak 15-minute flow rates by dividing by PHF. The 1965 Highway Capacity Manual introduced this kind of peaking factor, at first for 5- or 15-minute periods.

    4. Where level of service came from, and what it optimizes

    The first US Highway Capacity Manual appeared in 1950. It was written by a committee of the Highway Research Board, formed around 1944 under the engineer O. K. Normann. It gave each road two numbers. Possible capacity was the most vehicles a road could carry in an hour. Practical capacity was the most it could carry "without the traffic density being so great as to cause unreasonable delay, hazard, or restriction to the driver's freedom to maneuver". Engineers designed roads to the practical capacity.

    The second edition, published by the Highway Research Board in 1965, replaced the single practical capacity with a scale. A road could offer different levels of service, graded from A (free flow) to F ("forced flow", a queue). Each grade came with a "service volume", the most traffic the road could carry at that grade. The designer, or the agency paying, could then choose the grade.

    History · "Out of mud"

    In October 1965, just before the new manual came out, Charles Keese of the Texas Transportation Institute explained the idea to the annual meeting of the Institute of Traffic Engineers in Boston. Before the Second World War, he said, roads were built to provide all-weather routes. Now drivers wanted more. "The motorist is no longer satisfied to be 'out of mud.'" The new concept, he said, tried to take into account "speed and travel time, traffic interruption, freedom to maneuver, safety, driving comfort and convenience, and vehicular operational costs." The revised manual would give engineers "a range of service volumes (rather than one 'practical' capacity)", so that the highest level of service could be "provided based on economics and other management and engineering controls."

    Source: C. J. Keese, C. Pinnell and D. R. Drew, Highway Capacity: The Level of Service Concept, Texas Transportation Institute Bulletin 34 (1965). The manual: Highway Research Board, Highway Capacity Manual 1965, Special Report 87.

    Two things stand out in this origin. First, the grade was meant as a description and a menu. It told engineers and the public what the motorist would experience at a given volume, and left the choice of grade to "economics". Second, every item on Keese's list is the motorist's experience: speed, interruption, freedom to maneuver, comfort, the cost of running the vehicle. Pedestrians, people on buses, residents along the road and the land the road occupies are not on it.

    In the language of this course, level of service measures one part of Term A: the time and comfort of people in motor vehicles, at one place, in one busy hour. Later editions made the measure precise. Today it is the average delay per vehicle at a junction, or the density of vehicles on a freeway (next section). Section 7 shows what happened when plans turned this measure into a rule.

    5. Capacity and grades of service: HCM and HBS

    Capacity \(C\) is the largest flow a road or junction can sustain under the prevailing conditions (Module 7). The ratio of the actual flow \(q\) to capacity, \(x=q/C\), is called the degree of saturation. Both major manuals translate a measure of operation, such as delay, into six letter grades.

    The US Highway Capacity Manual

    The Transportation Research Board's HCM (6th edition 2016, subtitled A Guide for Multimodal Mobility Analysis; 7th edition 2022) defines level of service A–F by a service measure that differs by facility: density for freeway segments, control delay for intersections, speed or delay along urban streets.

    Thresholds from the HCM 2010, as reproduced in Spain's Nota de Servicio 5/2014 (Tables 11, 12 and 14). The roundabout values are confirmed there; the signalized values are standard HCM practice.

    The German HBS and its QSV

    Germany's Handbuch für die Bemessung von Straßenverkehrsanlagen (HBS), published by the FGSV (2015 edition), plays the HCM's role. It is organized in three parts: A (motorways), L (rural roads) and S (urban streets). The grades are called QSV, Qualitätsstufen des Verkehrsablaufs (quality levels of traffic flow).

    German · FGSV on the HBS 2015

    Das HBS enthält standardisierte Verfahren, mit denen … deren Kapazität ermittelt und darauf aufbauend die Qualität des Verkehrsablaufs in sechs Qualitätsstufen des Verkehrsablaufs (QSV) von A bis F bewertet werden kann. Es enthält auch Verfahren zur Ermittlung der … benötigten Verkehrsnachfrage. Diese wird als Bemessungsverkehrsstärke bezeichnet.

    "The HBS contains standardized procedures with which … the capacity [of road facilities] is determined and, building on this, the quality of traffic flow can be assessed in six quality levels of traffic flow (QSV) from A to F. It also contains procedures for determining the traffic demand required … This is called the design traffic volume." Source: FGSV, information page on the HBS 2015. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Differences between the two manuals are of emphasis. The HBS uses its own empirically derived capacity functions for German conditions; capacity is often treated as a random variable, following German research on breakdown (Module 7, section 6); and the QSV delay thresholds for signalized intersections are more generous than the HCM's at the upper end. As commonly reproduced from the HBS 2015, the motor-vehicle thresholds at signals are about 20, 35, 50 and 70 s for A–D, with F when demand exceeds capacity; check the handbook before using them. The HBS also contains QSV procedures for pedestrians, cyclists and public transport at signals. Both manuals treat F as "failure". Neither tells you whether D is acceptable: that is a policy decision.

    QSV is not a model-fit statistic

    German practitioners sometimes confuse the HBS QSV with goodness-of-fit scores used to validate simulation models, such as the "scalable GEH" (SQV) of Friedrich et al. (2019). The first grades the performance of a road; the second grades how well a model reproduces counts. See Module 10.

    The comparison shows two things. First, every national measure in the table is about motor traffic; none grades how well people reach places. Second, the measures differ in where they look. A grade per junction invites fixes per junction, such as an extra turn lane. A travel-time ratio per corridor, as in the Netherlands, invites a look at the whole route. Neither is a welfare test, but the second is closer to what travelers experience.

    7. The critique: when a grade becomes an objective

    LOS was designed to describe traffic conditions and to offer engineers a menu of grades. It became an objective when cities, counties and states wrote minimum grades into general plans and development rules: "intersections shall operate at LOS D or better in the peak hour". In the language of this course that rule is a constraint on one component of Term A, motor-vehicle delay at a design hour, with everything else unconstrained. The critique has five parts:

    1. It counts vehicles, not people. A bus with 60 riders and a car with one count the same in delay per vehicle. Pedestrian waiting at a wide junction does not appear.
    2. It ignores induced demand. A widening that restores LOS D in the forecast year attracts more traffic (Module 5); the grade then degrades again, triggering another widening.
    3. It penalizes density and infill. Under LOS-based impact review, a new building in a dense, walkable area, where streets are busy, triggers costly mitigation, while a greenfield development on empty roads does not. The rule pushes development to where it generates most driving.
    4. It prices space and safety at zero. The usual mitigations, extra turn lanes and wider entries, take land (Term D) and lengthen pedestrian crossings and raise speeds (Term C, crash and severance).
    5. It optimizes one hour. A junction sized for LOS D in the 30th hour is oversized for the other 8,700 hours, when wide geometry mainly enables speeding.

    California SB 743: from LOS to VMT

    Some cities moved first. In November 2014 the city council of Pasadena, near Los Angeles, voted to drop LOS from its environmental review of new development. In its place it adopted five measures: vehicle miles traveled per capita, vehicle trips per capita, and the proximity and quality of the bicycle network, of the transit network and of pedestrian access. Junction LOS is still estimated for large developments, but separately (SSTI, 2014). The state followed.

    California's Senate Bill 743 (Steinberg, 2013) required the state to replace automobile delay, as measured by LOS, as the metric of transportation impact in environmental review under CEQA. The state's guidelines adopted vehicle miles traveled (VMT) as the primary metric, applying statewide from July 2020, with a technical advisory recommending thresholds (for example, development whose VMT per capita is well below the regional average is presumed to have a less-than-significant impact). Local agencies may still use LOS in their general plans, but it can no longer by itself define an environmental impact. In the terms of \(W\), California moved weight from the time part of Term A to the vehicle-km driver of Term C, and stopped treating delay at a few junctions as the measure of harm.

    VMT is not a perfect objective either. It treats all driving as harm and ignores the access benefits that some driving creates; a welfare test would compare the logsum gain of a development with the external costs of its VMT. But as a correction to a rule that penalized the most efficient locations, it is widely regarded as an improvement. That is a judgment, and critics argue that VMT thresholds make some needed roads and housing harder to approve.

    Multimodal level of service and people throughput

    The HCM 2010 introduced multimodal LOS for urban streets, based on NCHRP Report 616 (2008), which asked travelers to rate video clips of streets. It produces separate grades for auto, transit, bicycle and pedestrian users, driven by traffic volume and speed on the adjacent lane, separation, crossing difficulty and transit frequency. Its value is that it makes trade-offs visible: a road diet that worsens auto LOS from C to D may improve the pedestrian and bicycle grades from E to B. Its weakness is that it still produces four grades rather than one welfare number, and the user ratings were collected in the US context.

    A simpler correction is people throughput: count persons per hour per meter of street width, by mode. As an illustration (orders of magnitude, not measured values), a general traffic lane carrying 800–1,800 cars at 1.2–1.4 persons per car moves roughly 1,000–2,500 persons per hour; the same lane as a bus lane with 40–60 buses per hour at typical loads can move several thousand; a two-way cycle track of similar width can move several thousand cyclists per hour at modest densities. People throughput is still only a component of Term A: it says nothing about trip lengths or destinations reached.

    What level of service is still good for

    The critique is of LOS as an objective, not as a measurement. As a diagnostic tool, it remains useful, and the profession uses it daily for good reasons:

    • Operations. Delay per vehicle at a junction is exactly the quantity a signal engineer needs when retiming signals or finding which approach fails first. Bus delay at a junction is delay to its riders, too.
    • Diagnosis. A grade per junction quickly shows where a network breaks down, and whether a queue will spill back into the junction upstream (Module 7).
    • Control. Zurich's perimeter control aggregates measured flows in its city center into a level of service, and uses it to decide when to hold cars at the edge (Module 7, section 8).
    • Communication. A letter grade is easy to explain at a public hearing.

    The rule that follows: report LOS, for all modes, as one effect among many. Do not let a minimum grade decide on its own what gets built.

    8. The design hour as an implicit weight in \(W\)

    Why the 30th hour rather than the 1st or the 200th? The conventional justification is a "knee" in the duration curve. The welfare justification is a cost–benefit test. Capacity built to serve the \(n\)-th hour costs money every year (Term E) and occupies land every hour (Term D); it reduces delay only in the hours that would otherwise exceed capacity (Term A), and it may increase speed-related harm in all other hours (Term C).

    Worked example

    A 2 km suburban arterial is sized either for its 30th hour (needs a third lane each way) or for its 100th hour (two lanes suffice). With two lanes, about 70 hours per year exceed capacity.

    • Term E. Widening costs €12 million for construction. The land is not in this figure, because it is counted once, in Term D below (Module 1). Annualized at 4% over 40 years, the annuity factor is \(0.04/(1-1.04^{-40})\approx0.0505\), so about €606,000 per year, plus €20,000 maintenance. With \(\mu=0.2\) the welfare cost is about €750,000 per year.
    • Term D. Two extra 3.5 m lanes over 2 km take 1.4 ha. At an annual land rent \(r\) of €50/m² (plausible on a suburban edge; far higher in a city center), that is €700,000 per year. Most appraisals set this to zero because the land is already public.
    • Term A. Suppose that in each of the 70 congested hours about 3,600 vehicles each lose 4 minutes: 16,800 vehicle-hours a year, worth about €250,000 at €15/h. This is an upper bound under fixed demand; with induced demand the realized gain is smaller.
    • Term C. Wider cross-sections raise off-peak speeds and pedestrian crossing distances; not quantified here, but the sign is negative.

    Even ignoring land, \(-\)€750,000 + €250,000 is a net loss. A 30th-hour design rule implicitly asserts that 70 hours of moderate delay are worth more than €0.75–1.45 million a year. Few decision-makers would choose that if shown the numbers. The point is not that the 100th hour is right everywhere, but that the design hour is a price — on peak time relative to money and land — that should be chosen for each project, not inherited.

    Country contrasts, as readings of \(W\)

    US: the 30th-hour and LOS conventions embody a high implicit value of peak motor-vehicle time and \(r\approx0\); SB 743 is a deliberate departure. Germany: stochastic capacity and a move toward all-year analysis make the trade-off more explicit, and the BVWP cost–benefit appraisal (Module 14) prices time and safety. Spain: publishes the 100th and 500th hours, giving designers the duration curve rather than one number. Japan: daily-volume standards per lane, with a lower standard for intersection-dense urban streets, keep the rule simple and are combined in cities with a rail-first policy that keeps road demand down. Singapore: rather than sizing roads to the peak, it prices the peak (ERP) and caps the fleet (Module 13), which is what you do when \(r\) is very high. These are qualitative interpretations, not measured weights.

    Exercise 8.1 · Your own duration curve

    Download a year of hourly data from a permanent counting station (for example from a national open-data portal: BASt automatische Dauerzählstellen in Germany, the US state DOT continuous count programs, or Spain's Ministerio de Transportes traffic maps).

    1. Compute AADT and plot the duration curve as % of AADT. Compute \(K_1\), \(K_{30}\), \(K_{50}\), \(K_{100}\) and \(K_{500}\).
    2. Compute PHF for the 30th hour.
    3. Repeat for an urban and a rural or recreational station. How much capacity does each step from the 100th to the 30th hour add, in %?
    4. Using the worked example's costs, find the break-even value of time at which designing for the 30th rather than the 100th hour is justified, with and without land cost.
    Exercise 8.2 · An LOS rule versus a welfare test

    A city rule requires LOS D at all signalized intersections. A proposed mid-rise housing project near a tram stop would push one junction from LOS D (52 s) to LOS E (61 s) in the evening peak. The developer proposes a second left-turn lane that would add 7 m to a pedestrian crossing.

    1. List the terms of \(W\) affected by (a) the housing, (b) the turn lane, and their signs.
    2. Under SB 743-style review, what metric would decide significance, and what mitigation would make sense instead of the turn lane?
    3. Estimate the change in pedestrian crossing time and the effect on the signal cycle (minimum pedestrian green at 1.2 m/s).
