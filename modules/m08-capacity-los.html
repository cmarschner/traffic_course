    Part III · Traffic engineering · Module 8

    Capacity, design hours and level of service — and its critics

    ≈ 5 hours · Prerequisites: Module 7 · Key terms: A C D E

    Every road is sized for some hour of the year. Which hour, and what quality of traffic flow is demanded in it, are among the most consequential choices in transport engineering, yet they are usually made by convention rather than by analysis. This module explains how design volumes and level-of-service grades are computed in the US, Germany, Spain and Japan, and then examines the critique that turned "level of service" from a neutral description into a contested objective. The central claim: choosing a design hour and a minimum grade is an implicit weighting of the terms of \(W\).

    Learning outcomes
    • Compute AADT/DTV from counts, build an hourly duration curve and derive \(K_{30}\), \(K_{50}\), \(K_{100}\) and a design-hour volume.
    • Compute and use the peak hour factor.
    • Explain how the HCM (US) and HBS (Germany) assign grades A–F, and how Spain and Japan set design volumes.
    • State the main critiques of LOS as an objective and describe California's switch from LOS to VMT (SB 743) and multimodal LOS.
    • Show that the choice of design hour trades off Term A (rare delay) against Terms D and E (land and money every day), and evaluate it explicitly.

    1. Counting traffic

    The basic demand statistic is average daily traffic over a year: AADT (annual average daily traffic) in the US, DTV (durchschnittliche tägliche Verkehrsstärke) in Germany, IMD (intensidad media diaria) in Spain, 年平均日交通量 in Japan. Only a small share of road sections have permanent counting stations. Most volumes are estimated from short counts (a day or a week) expanded with factors from a "similar" permanent station: hour-of-day, day-of-week and month-of-year factors. Spain's Nota de Servicio 5/2014 writes this as \(\text{IMD}=I_{24}\cdot N\cdot L\cdot S\), with factors taken from the estación afín (the matched permanent station). The US Traffic Monitoring Guide describes the same logic.

    Two sources of error dominate: choosing the wrong matched station (a leisure route expanded with commuter factors), and counting demand where there is already a queue. A count downstream of a bottleneck measures throughput, not demand. If a road is congested in the peak, its counts understate the demand that a wider road would attract, before induced demand is even considered (Module 5).

    2. Duration curves and the design hour

    Rank the 8,760 hourly volumes of a year from highest to lowest and plot them as a share of AADT. This duration curve falls steeply over the first few dozen hours and then flattens. The \(n\)-th highest hour defines the factor

    $$K_n = \frac{q_{(n)}}{\text{AADT}}, \qquad \text{DHV} = K_n\cdot\text{AADT}\cdot D ,$$

    where DHV is the design-hour volume and \(D\) the share in the peak direction (giving the directional DDHV). FHWA's Traffic Data Computation Method Pocket Guide (2018) notes that two-way DHV is usually 8–12% of ADT and that US practice "typically" takes the 30th highest hour, a convention attributed to the Highway Performance Monitoring System. Its worked example shows why: on an urban highway the 1st, 30th, 50th and 100th hours were 3,992, 3,850, 3,735 and 3,355 veh/h. Urban duration curves are flat because the same commuter peak recurs every weekday; rural and recreational roads have spiky curves driven by holidays.

    Japanese · Road Structure Ordinance (道路構造令, Cabinet Order No. 320 of 1970), Art. 2 and Art. 5

    計画交通量　道路の設計の基礎とするために、当該道路の存する地域の発展の動向、将来の自動車交通の状況等を勘案して…定める自動車の日交通量をいう。

    Art. 2(22): "Planned traffic volume means the daily motor-vehicle traffic volume, determined… taking into account the development trend of the area in which the road is located, future motor-vehicle traffic conditions and the like, to serve as the basis for the design of the road."

    Art. 5(2)–(3), summarised: a road has two lanes if its planned traffic volume does not exceed the "design standard traffic volume (the maximum permissible motor-vehicle traffic volume)" for its class and terrain, for example 14,000 vehicles/day for a Class 1, Grade 2 road on level terrain, or 12,000 for an urban Class 4, Grade 1 street. Otherwise the number of lanes (four or more, normally even) follows from the ratio of planned traffic to a per-lane value, for example 12,000 vehicles/day per lane for a Class 1, Grade 1 expressway on level terrain, 18,000 for an urban expressway (Class 2, Grade 1) and 12,000 for a Class 4, Grade 1 urban street. For Class 4 streets with many intersections, the per-lane value is multiplied by 0.6. Source: e-Gov Japanese Law Search. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted); this is a statute and is translated and summarised more fully.

    The Japanese approach is instructive. It sizes roads from a daily volume and a fixed per-lane standard rather than from a chosen peak hour and a target grade. That is cruder hydraulically, but it makes the policy choice visible: the 0.6 multiplier for streets with many intersections is a statement that urban streets should be judged by different standards. The planned volume is a forecast, so the quality of the demand model, and whether it includes induced traffic, determines the result.

    3. Within the hour: the peak hour factor

    Traffic within the peak hour is not uniform. The peak hour factor is

    $$\text{PHF} = \frac{V_{60}}{4\,V_{15,\max}},$$

    where \(V_{60}\) is the hourly volume and \(V_{15,\max}\) the highest 15-minute volume in that hour. The HCM converts hourly volumes to peak 15-minute flow rates by dividing by PHF. Example: 1,800 veh in the hour with a peak quarter of 500 veh gives PHF = 0.90 and a design flow rate of 2,000 veh/h. Typical values are 0.85–0.95; values near 1 indicate saturated conditions where the count is limited by capacity. Note that GIRABASE, the French roundabout tool, explicitly does not include a peak factor (Module 9): national methods differ even in which quarter-hour they size for.

    4. Capacity and grades of service: HCM and HBS

    Capacity \(C\) is the maximum sustainable flow under prevailing conditions; the degree of saturation is \(x=q/C\). Both major manuals then translate an operational measure into six letter grades.

    The US Highway Capacity Manual

    The Transportation Research Board's HCM (6th edition 2016, subtitled A Guide for Multimodal Mobility Analysis; 7th edition 2022) defines level of service A–F by a service measure that differs by facility: density for freeway segments, control delay for intersections, speed or delay along urban streets.

    LOSSignalised intersection: control delay (s/veh)Unsignalised intersection or roundabout (s/veh)
    A≤ 10≤ 10
    B10–2010–15
    C20–3515–25
    D35–5525–35
    E55–8035–50
    F> 80, or demand exceeds capacity> 50, or demand exceeds capacity

    Thresholds from the HCM 2010, as reproduced in Spain's Nota de Servicio 5/2014 (Tables 11, 12 and 14). The roundabout values are confirmed there; the signalised values are standard HCM practice.

    The German HBS and its QSV

    Germany's Handbuch für die Bemessung von Straßenverkehrsanlagen (HBS), published by the FGSV (2015 edition), plays the HCM's role. It is organised in three parts: A (motorways), L (rural roads) and S (urban streets). The grades are called QSV, Qualitätsstufen des Verkehrsablaufs (quality levels of traffic flow).

    German · FGSV on the HBS 2015

    Das HBS enthält standardisierte Verfahren, mit denen … deren Kapazität ermittelt und darauf aufbauend die Qualität des Verkehrsablaufs in sechs Qualitätsstufen des Verkehrsablaufs (QSV) von A bis F bewertet werden kann. Es enthält auch Verfahren zur Ermittlung der … benötigten Verkehrsnachfrage. Diese wird als Bemessungsverkehrsstärke bezeichnet.

    "The HBS contains standardised procedures with which … the capacity [of road facilities] is determined and, building on this, the quality of traffic flow can be assessed in six quality levels of traffic flow (QSV) from A to F. It also contains procedures for determining the traffic demand required … This is called the design traffic volume." Source: FGSV, information page on the HBS 2015. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Differences between the two manuals are of emphasis. The HBS uses its own empirically derived capacity functions for German conditions; capacity is often treated stochastically (§5 of Module 7); and the QSV delay thresholds for signalised intersections are more generous than the HCM's at the upper end. As commonly reproduced from the HBS 2015, the motor-vehicle thresholds at signals are about 20, 35, 50 and 70 s for A–D, with F when demand exceeds capacity; check the handbook before using them. The HBS also contains QSV procedures for pedestrians, cyclists and public transport at signals. Both manuals treat F as "failure". Neither tells you whether D is acceptable: that is a policy decision.

    QSV is not a model-fit statistic

    German practitioners sometimes confuse the HBS QSV with goodness-of-fit scores used to validate simulation models, such as the "scalable GEH" (SQV) of Friedrich et al. (2019). The first grades the performance of a road; the second grades how well a model reproduces counts. See Module 10.

    5. The critique: when a grade becomes an objective

    LOS was designed to describe traffic conditions to non-specialists. It became an objective when cities, counties and states wrote minimum grades into general plans and development rules: "intersections shall operate at LOS D or better in the peak hour". In the language of this course that rule is a constraint on one component of Term A, motor-vehicle delay at a design hour, with everything else unconstrained. The critique has five parts:

    1. It counts vehicles, not people. A bus with 60 riders and a car with one count the same in delay per vehicle. Pedestrian waiting at a wide junction does not appear.
    2. It ignores induced demand. A widening that restores LOS D in the forecast year attracts more traffic (Module 5); the grade then degrades again, triggering another widening.
    3. It penalises density and infill. Under LOS-based impact review, a new building in a dense, walkable area, where streets are busy, triggers costly mitigation, while a greenfield development on empty roads does not. The rule pushes development to where it generates most driving.
    4. It prices space and safety at zero. The usual mitigations, extra turn lanes and wider entries, take land (Term D) and lengthen pedestrian crossings and raise speeds (Term C, crash and severance).
    5. It optimises one hour. A junction sized for LOS D in the 30th hour is oversized for the other 8,700 hours, when wide geometry mainly enables speeding.

    California SB 743: from LOS to VMT

    California's Senate Bill 743 (Steinberg, 2013) required the state to replace automobile delay, as measured by LOS, as the metric of transportation impact in environmental review under CEQA. The state's guidelines adopted vehicle miles travelled (VMT) as the primary metric, applying statewide from July 2020, with a technical advisory recommending thresholds (for example, development whose VMT per capita is well below the regional average is presumed to have a less-than-significant impact). Local agencies may still use LOS in their general plans, but it can no longer by itself define an environmental impact. In the terms of \(W\), California moved weight from the time part of Term A to the vehicle-km driver of Term C, and stopped treating delay at a few junctions as the measure of harm.

    VMT is not a perfect objective either. It treats all driving as harm and ignores the access benefits that some driving creates; a welfare test would compare the logsum gain of a development with the external costs of its VMT. But as a correction to a rule that penalised the most efficient locations, it is widely regarded as an improvement. That is a judgement, and critics argue that VMT thresholds make some needed roads and housing harder to approve.

    Multimodal level of service and people throughput

    The HCM 2010 introduced multimodal LOS for urban streets, based on NCHRP Report 616 (2008), which asked travellers to rate video clips of streets. It produces separate grades for auto, transit, bicycle and pedestrian users, driven by traffic volume and speed on the adjacent lane, separation, crossing difficulty and transit frequency. Its value is that it makes trade-offs visible: a road diet that worsens auto LOS from C to D may improve the pedestrian and bicycle grades from E to B. Its weakness is that it still produces four grades rather than one welfare number, and the user ratings were collected in the US context.

    A simpler correction is people throughput: count persons per hour per metre of street width, by mode. As an illustration (orders of magnitude, not measured values), a general traffic lane carrying 800–1,800 cars at 1.2–1.4 persons per car moves roughly 1,000–2,500 persons per hour; the same lane as a bus lane with 40–60 buses per hour at typical loads can move several thousand; a two-way cycle track of similar width can move several thousand cyclists per hour at modest densities. People throughput is still only a component of Term A: it says nothing about trip lengths or destinations reached.

    6. The design hour as an implicit weight in \(W\)

    Why the 30th hour rather than the 1st or the 200th? The conventional justification is a "knee" in the duration curve. The welfare justification is a cost–benefit test. Capacity built to serve the \(n\)-th hour costs money every year (Term E) and occupies land every hour (Term D); it reduces delay only in the hours that would otherwise exceed capacity (Term A), and it may increase speed-related harm in all other hours (Term C).

    Worked example

    A 2 km suburban arterial is sized either for its 30th hour (needs a third lane each way) or for its 100th hour (two lanes suffice). With two lanes, about 70 hours per year exceed capacity.

    • Term E. Widening costs €12 million. Annualised at 4% over 40 years, the annuity factor is \(0.04/(1-1.04^{-40})\approx0.0505\), so about €606,000 per year, plus €20,000 maintenance. With \(\mu=0.2\) the welfare cost is about €750,000 per year.
    • Term D. Two extra 3.5 m lanes over 2 km take 1.4 ha. At an annual land rent \(r\) of €50/m² (plausible on a suburban edge; far higher in a city centre), that is €700,000 per year. Most appraisals set this to zero because the land is already public.
    • Term A. Suppose that in each of the 70 congested hours about 3,600 vehicles each lose 4 minutes: 16,800 vehicle-hours a year, worth about €250,000 at €15/h. This is an upper bound under fixed demand; with induced demand the realised gain is smaller.
    • Term C. Wider cross-sections raise off-peak speeds and pedestrian crossing distances; not quantified here, but the sign is negative.

    Even ignoring land, \(-\)€750,000 + €250,000 is a net loss. A 30th-hour design rule implicitly asserts that 70 hours of moderate delay are worth more than €0.75–1.45 million a year. Few decision-makers would choose that if shown the numbers. The point is not that the 100th hour is right everywhere, but that the design hour is a price — on peak time relative to money and land — that should be chosen for each project, not inherited.

    Country contrasts, as readings of \(W\)

    US: the 30th-hour and LOS conventions embody a high implicit value of peak motor-vehicle time and \(r\approx0\); SB 743 is a deliberate departure. Germany: stochastic capacity and a move toward all-year analysis make the trade-off more explicit, and the BVWP cost–benefit appraisal (Module 14) prices time and safety. Spain: publishes the 100th and 500th hours, giving designers the duration curve rather than one number. Japan: daily-volume standards per lane, with a lower standard for intersection-dense urban streets, keep the rule simple and are combined in cities with a rail-first policy that keeps road demand down. Singapore: rather than sizing roads to the peak, it prices the peak (ERP) and caps the fleet (Module 13), which is what you do when \(r\) is very high. These are qualitative interpretations, not measured weights.

    Exercise 8.1 · Your own duration curve

    Download a year of hourly data from a permanent counting station (for example from a national open-data portal: BASt automatische Dauerzählstellen in Germany, the US state DOT continuous count programmes, or Spain's Ministerio de Transportes traffic maps).

    1. Compute AADT and plot the duration curve as % of AADT. Compute \(K_1\), \(K_{30}\), \(K_{50}\), \(K_{100}\) and \(K_{500}\).
    2. Compute PHF for the 30th hour.
    3. Repeat for an urban and a rural or recreational station. How much capacity does each step from the 100th to the 30th hour add, in %?
    4. Using the worked example's costs, find the break-even value of time at which designing for the 30th rather than the 100th hour is justified, with and without land cost.
    Exercise 8.2 · An LOS rule versus a welfare test

    A city rule requires LOS D at all signalised intersections. A proposed mid-rise housing project near a tram stop would push one junction from LOS D (52 s) to LOS E (61 s) in the evening peak. The developer proposes a second left-turn lane that would add 7 m to a pedestrian crossing.

    1. List the terms of \(W\) affected by (a) the housing, (b) the turn lane, and their signs.
    2. Under SB 743-style review, what metric would decide significance, and what mitigation would make sense instead of the turn lane?
    3. Estimate the change in pedestrian crossing time and the effect on the signal cycle (minimum pedestrian green at 1.2 m/s).
