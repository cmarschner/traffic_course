    Part IV · Policy levers · Module 13

    Pricing and demand management

    ≈ 4 hours · Prerequisites: Modules 1, 2 and 3 · Key terms: A (users' surplus, including all delay), C (emissions, crashes, noise), E (revenue \(R\), marginal cost of public funds \(\mu\)), group weights \(\omega_g\)

    In January 2006 Stockholm switched on cameras at every road into its inner city and began charging drivers to cross. Two-thirds of residents were against it. The newspapers were overwhelmingly hostile. On the first morning, traffic across the cordon dropped sharply. Eight months later a majority voted to keep the charge, and within a few years no Swedish party wanted to abolish it. Economists have recommended road pricing for a century. Few policies are as unpopular before they start and as accepted afterwards, and many proposals never get that far. This module explains what a congestion charge does to welfare, why the money it raises is not itself a gain, and how a handful of cities made it politically possible while others voted it down. It ends with the prices that matter as much: parking, and the charges that must replace fuel taxes as cars electrify.

    In brief
    The question
    Should drivers pay for the delay they impose on others at busy times? If so, how much, and how can a city make it acceptable to the people who will pay?
    What matters
    A charge improves welfare only by changing behavior: fewer, shorter, earlier, later or different trips, so the road flows. The money is a transfer from drivers to the public purse. It adds to welfare only if it is spent better than the drivers would have spent it. Whether a scheme happens at all is decided by politics far more than by economics.
    Numbers to question
    The forecast drop in traffic (Stockholm 22 %, London 18 %, New York about 11 % in their first year). The value of time. Who pays, by income and place of residence. Collection costs. Any "benefit" that counts the revenue.
    Levers
    The price level and its hours, the boundary, exemptions, how the revenue is used, a trial before any vote, and who decides. Beyond road charges: curb parking prices and parking rules, and distance-based charges as fuel taxes fade.
    Ask your analysts
    Did the model let people change their trips, or did it hold demand fixed? Is the time saved counted once? Is the revenue counted as a benefit? Who loses before the money is returned, and how are they compensated? What happens just outside the boundary?
    Learning outcomes
    • Explain why the right congestion charge equals the delay a driver imposes on others, and compute it for a simple road.
    • Decompose the welfare effect of a charge into users' surplus (Term A), other harms (Term C) and revenue (Term E, with \(\mu\)), counting each delay once and treating revenue as a transfer.
    • Explain why most drivers lose before the revenue is used, and how the use of revenue changes who gains.
    • Describe how Singapore, London, Stockholm, Milan and New York introduced charges, and why Edinburgh and Manchester voted them down.
    • Explain the case for parking prices and the choice between fuel taxes and distance-based charges as cars electrify.

    1. The idea: pay for the delay you impose on others A E

    Module 1 ("Where is congestion?") showed what happens when one more driver joins a busy road. The newcomer bears their own travel time. They also slow every other driver a little, and that delay, added up over all the others, is the external congestion cost. The newcomer ignores it, so too many people drive at the peak. Module 1 also explained where this delay sits in the welfare function \(W\): inside Term A, because Term A is computed from everyone's actual travel times. It is not a separate cost in Term C. What the external cost gives us is the right congestion charge: a price equal to the delay a driver imposes on others, in money.

    The idea goes back to Arthur Pigou's 1920 example of two roads (told in Module 1). Alan Walters measured the gap between the cost a driver bears and the cost to all road users in 1961. William Vickrey, later a Nobel laureate, argued for half a century that roads and subways should be priced by time of day. Implementation lagged the theory by decades, for political rather than technical reasons. Britain's Smeed Report is the classic case.

    History · "Pricing by itself cannot produce a cure"

    In the early 1960s Britain's Ministry of Transport asked a panel of engineers and economists whether drivers could be charged for using congested roads. Its chairman was Reuben Smeed (1909–1976), a statistician at the Road Research Laboratory. He was already known for "Smeed's law" (1949), an empirical rule linking road deaths to the number of vehicles and people. One member was the young economist Alan Walters (1926–2009). Walters had left school at fifteen to work in a shoe factory, and later became Margaret Thatcher's economic adviser.

    The panel's report, Road Pricing: The Economic and Technical Possibilities, appeared in 1964. It found that practical pricing methods "could probably be devised". It estimated the net gain from faster traffic at £100–150 million a year. And it made an assumption that politicians later forgot: road prices would come with lower motoring taxes, "so that the motoring population as a whole would pay no more". In his foreword Smeed warned that "pricing by itself cannot produce a 'cure' for congestion". It was a way to get better value from roads, old and new.

    Successive governments did nothing with the report. Smeed left the laboratory soon after its publication, frustrated. London introduced a congestion charge 39 years later.

    Sources: Ministry of Transport, Road Pricing (1964), free PDF, Foreword and list of members; Reuben Smeed and Alan Walters (Wikipedia). No free portraits are available.

    2. What a charge does to welfare A C E

    A worked example shows every piece. Take one road into a city center at the morning peak. Without traffic, the trip takes 10 minutes. The road carries about 2,000 vehicles an hour before it gets badly congested. Drivers value their time at €15 an hour. Some trips are worth a lot to the people who make them, others little. If the trip gets more expensive, the least valuable trips are dropped or moved to another time. (The exact demand curve and travel-time formula are in the "Under the hood" box.)

    Worked example · Pricing one busy road
    Flow (veh/h)Travel time (min)Charge (€)Time cost + charge (€)
    No charge2,05511.702.92
    Best charge1,69210.80.773.46
    1. The charge. At the best flow, each driver delays all the others by a total worth €0.77. That is the charge.
    2. Behavior. About 360 trips an hour (18 %) are dropped, moved to another time or made another way. Those who still drive save 0.9 minutes.
    3. Drivers (Term A). The 0.9 minutes are worth €0.23, but drivers pay €0.77. Every driver who stays is worse off, and so is every driver priced off the road. In total, drivers lose about €1,018 an hour.
    4. Revenue (Term E). 1,692 drivers × €0.77 = about €1,302 an hour.
    5. Net gain. Revenue minus the drivers' loss: about €283 an hour. This is the efficiency gain: trips that cost others more in delay than they were worth to the driver no longer happen.

    The net gain is small next to the two large numbers it comes from: a big loss to drivers and a slightly bigger amount of money for the public purse. That is the whole political problem of road pricing in one line. Drivers who value their time highly can gain even before the money is returned; with a single value of time, as here, nobody who drives gains.

    Where it sits in \(W\)

    The welfare change from introducing the charge has four parts:

    $$\Delta W=\underbrace{\sum_g\omega_g\,\Delta CS_g}_{\text{A: users, including all delay}}\;-\;\underbrace{\sum_k p_k\,\Delta E_k}_{\text{C: emissions, crashes, noise}}\;+\;\underbrace{(1+\mu)\,R}_{\text{E: revenue}}\;-\;\underbrace{(1+\mu)\,\Delta K}_{\text{E: cost of collection}} .$$

    Here \(\Delta CS_g\) is the change in users' surplus for group \(g\) (Term A), with weight \(\omega_g\). \(p_k\Delta E_k\) is the change in each other harm, priced. \(R\) is the revenue, \(\Delta K\) the yearly cost of running the scheme, and \(\mu\) the marginal cost of public funds from Module 1: the extra welfare lost when a euro is raised through ordinary taxes. Four rules follow.

    1. Count the delay once. The time drivers save is in \(\Delta CS\), Term A. Nothing for congestion goes into Term C, before or after the charge. Term C holds only the other harms that fall when fewer vehicles drive: CO₂, air pollution, noise and crashes. These are often of the same order as the efficiency gain. The same rule applies to buses and trains that get more crowded when drivers switch to them: the extra crowding shows up in those passengers' surplus in Term A.
    2. Revenue is a transfer. Drivers pay \(R\), which lowers their surplus in Term A. The same \(R\) arrives in Term E. With \(\mu=0\) the two cancel exactly, and the charge adds welfare only through changed behavior: the €283. With \(\mu=0.2\), the €1,302 is worth €1,562, because it lets the government raise less through distorting taxes. The net gain then rises to about €544 an hour. Revenue never counts as a benefit in itself.
    3. Who gets the money matters, through \(\omega_g\). If revenue is returned to people as payments \(T_g\), the expression becomes \(\sum_g\omega_g(\Delta CS_g+T_g)+(1+\mu)\big(R-\sum_g T_g\big)\). A euro handed back adds welfare only if it goes to a group whose weight exceeds \(1+\mu\).
    4. Collection costs can eat the gain. London's original scheme was criticized for high operating costs relative to its revenue. Camera and satellite systems have lowered these costs, but they belong in the appraisal.

    The classic engineering objective, minimizing total vehicle-hours with demand held fixed, cannot evaluate pricing at all. If nobody changes their trip, a charge changes nothing on the road. A fixed-demand model that adds the charge to drivers' costs is worse: it counts the payment as a pure loss and ignores the revenue. Pricing is one of the clearest cases where the choice of objective decides the answer.

    Real schemes are not textbook charges

    • Cordons and zones are blunt. One price for every trip that crosses a line, regardless of distance or route. They capture part of the possible gain, often a large part when the boundary matches the congested core.
    • Untolled alternatives. If a parallel road or a different time is free, the best charge on the tolled road is lower than the full external delay, because some traffic will simply divert.
    • Timing. Much congestion is a queue at a bottleneck at the peak. A charge that varies through the peak can spread departures and remove most of the queue, with few trips given up at all.
    • The boundary. Traffic can grow just outside the charged area, and parking can spill into neighborhoods next to it. Both need monitoring.
    Under the hoodAverage and marginal cost on a BPR link, the worked example step by step, and the bottleneck

    Travel time. Let \(q\) be the flow (veh/h) on a link with free-flow time \(t_0\) and capacity \(Q_c\). The Bureau of Public Roads (BPR) function (Module 1) gives

    $$t(q)=t_0\Big[1+\alpha\Big(\frac{q}{Q_c}\Big)^{\beta}\Big],\qquad \alpha=0.15,\ \beta=4 .$$

    Average and marginal cost. With value of time \(\mathrm{VOT}\), each driver bears the average cost \(AC(q)=\mathrm{VOT}\cdot t(q)\). The total time cost of all drivers is \(TC=q\cdot AC(q)\). One more vehicle raises it by the marginal social cost

    $$MSC(q)=\frac{d\,TC}{dq}=AC(q)+q\,AC'(q)=\mathrm{VOT}\,t_0\Big[1+\alpha(1+\beta)\Big(\frac{q}{Q_c}\Big)^{\beta}\Big].$$

    The second term, \(q\,AC'(q)=\mathrm{VOT}\,t_0\,\alpha\beta\,(q/Q_c)^{\beta}\), is the delay one more vehicle imposes on all the others. Let \(D(q)\) be the inverse demand: the value of the \(q\)-th trip to its maker. Drivers choose the flow where \(D=AC\). Welfare is highest where \(D=MSC\). The best charge is the gap at that flow:

    $$\tau^{*}=MSC(q^{*})-AC(q^{*})=q^{*}AC'(q^{*}).$$

    The example. \(t_0=10\) min, \(Q_c=2{,}000\) veh/h, \(\mathrm{VOT}=€15\)/h, so \(\mathrm{VOT}\cdot t_0=€2.50\). Inverse demand \(D(q)=6-0.0015\,q\) (€ per trip). Write \(x=q/Q_c\).

    1. No charge: solve \(6-3x=2.5+0.375x^{4}\). Then \(x_e\approx1.027\), \(q_e\approx2{,}055\) veh/h, \(AC=€2.92\), travel time 11.7 min.
    2. Best flow: solve \(6-3x=2.5+1.875x^{4}\). Then \(x^{*}\approx0.846\), \(q^{*}\approx1{,}692\) veh/h, travel time 10.8 min, \(AC=€2.69\).
    3. Charge: \(\tau^{*}=1.5\,x^{*4}=€0.77\). The full price \(2.69+0.77=€3.46\) equals \(D(q^{*})\).
    4. Efficiency gain: \(\displaystyle\int_{q^{*}}^{q_e}\big(MSC-D\big)\,dq=2000\int_{0.846}^{1.027}\big(1.875x^{4}+3x-3.5\big)dx\approx €283\) per hour.
    5. Revenue: \(R=\tau^{*}q^{*}\approx €1{,}302\) per hour.
    6. Users' surplus: the price drivers face rises from €2.92 to €3.46, so \(\Delta CS=-\int_{2.92}^{3.46}q(p)\,dp\approx -€1{,}018\) per hour.

    Check: \(-1{,}018+1{,}302\approx 283\). The efficiency gain equals the change in users' surplus plus revenue, which is why adding the delay again in Term C would count it twice.

    Dynamic congestion. In William Vickrey's bottleneck model (1969), drivers choose when to leave, trading queuing time against arriving early or late. A charge that rises and falls through the peak can remove the queue entirely. The gain then comes from eliminating queuing, not from fewer trips. See Small & Verhoef (2007), chapter 4.

    3. Singapore: price and quantity together A constraints

    Singapore introduced the world's first urban congestion charge, the Area Licensing Scheme, on 2 June 1975. Drivers had to buy and display a paper license to enter a restricted zone of about 6 km² in the city center during the morning peak. Staff at the entry points checked the licenses. The hours were extended to the evening peak in 1989 and to the whole working day in 1994. In 1998 Electronic Road Pricing (ERP) replaced it. Gantries charge an in-vehicle unit on each passage, at rates that vary by location and half-hour. The Land Transport Authority (LTA) reviews the rates regularly against target speed bands. It raises the charge where speeds fall below the band and lowers it where traffic flows freely. This is a practical, feedback version of the textbook charge. The next step is ERP 2, which uses satellite positioning and a new on-board unit. In September 2026 the LTA announced that ERP 2 will launch on 1 January 2027, and that more than 98 % of eligible Singapore-registered vehicles had been fitted with the new unit.

    Singapore also caps the number of vehicles. Since May 1990, anyone registering a vehicle needs a Certificate of Entitlement (COE), valid for ten years and bought at auction for a fixed quota. The quota follows an allowed growth rate of the vehicle population, which the government cut to zero for cars and motorcycles in February 2018. COE premiums for cars have at times exceeded S$100,000.

    The COE premium is a shadow price

    The COE enforces a limit on the vehicle stock, a version of the land limit in Module 1, section 9. The auction finds the price at which demand equals the quota. So the COE premium is a market estimate of the limit's shadow price. ERP is the matching price for use. Martin Weitzman's 1974 analysis of prices versus quantities applies. A quota fixes the quantity and lets the price vary; a tax does the reverse. Which is better depends on whether getting the quantity wrong is more costly than getting the price wrong. Singapore uses the quantity tool where land is the hard limit, and the price tool where conditions vary by hour and place.

    Singapore's political conditions are unusual: one party has governed since independence, and land scarcity is visible to every resident. That made a charge easier to introduce than in most democracies. The design lessons travel; the political path does not.

    4. London 2003: a new mayor with a mandate A E

    London had no city-wide government between 1986 and 2000. The Greater London Authority Act 1999 created an elected mayor and gave the office the power to charge for road use. The first mayor, Ken Livingstone, had made a congestion charge part of his election manifesto. The charge started on 17 February 2003, at £5 per day for driving in central London on weekdays, enforced by cameras that read number plates. Under the 1999 Act, the net revenue must be spent on transport in London.

    After the first year, Transport for London reported that traffic entering the zone during charging hours had fallen by 18 %, and car traffic by 30 %. Delays inside the zone were about 30 % lower than before (TfL, February 2004). Congestion later returned toward earlier levels. Road space was given to buses, cycling and public space, and the number of private-hire vehicles grew. In \(W\) this need not be a failure: the scheme freed road space that was then used for other purposes with value in Terms A, C and D.

    The charge rose in steps to £15 (2020) and to £18 from 2 January 2026. It now applies 07:00–18:00 on weekdays and 12:00–18:00 at weekends and bank holidays. Electric cars lost their full exemption in January 2026 and now receive a discount. The Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ) is a separate daily charge on vehicles that do not meet emission standards. It started in central London in April 2019 and grew to all of Greater London in August 2023. It targets air pollution (Term C) rather than congestion. Its London-wide expansion was a major issue in the July 2023 Uxbridge by-election: pricing politics at the urban fringe, where public transport is weaker.

    5. Stockholm: trial first, vote second A E

    Stockholm's charge began with a broken promise. Before the 2002 election, the Social Democratic mayor publicly promised that there would be no road tolls in Stockholm. The Social Democrats won, but their national government needed the support of the small Green Party. The Greens' price was a "several-year, full-scale congestion charging trial" in Stockholm. The debate that followed was fierce. Of the newspaper articles on the topic, 39 % were negative and 3 % positive. Opponents demanded a referendum, sure they would win. It was set for September 2006, after the trial, on the day of the next general election. That timing turned out to be decisive.

    The trial ran from 3 January to 31 July 2006, with charges that varied by time of day at a cordon around the inner city. Traffic across the cordon fell immediately and settled about 22 % below 2005 levels. Because traffic near capacity is so sensitive (section 2), queues fell by 30–50 %. The press turned: positive articles rose from 3 % to 42 %. In the referendum, 53 % of valid votes in the City of Stockholm were for keeping the charge. Some surrounding municipalities held their own votes, mostly against, though they had no legal say.

    The center-right parties that had opposed the charge won the election, nationally and in the city. They had promised to follow the referendum, and they reintroduced the charge permanently on 1 August 2007. The deal that settled it was about money. The charge is legally a national tax, so the revenue went to the state. Regional and national politicians agreed on a large infrastructure package in which the revenue was earmarked for a new motorway bypass around Stockholm. Support kept rising, to about 70 % by 2011. By 2013 the former opponents had agreed to raise the charge themselves. (J. Eliasson, "The Stockholm congestion pricing syndrome", KTH, 2014.)

    Lag (2004:629) om trängselskatt, § 1 (Swedish)

    "Trängselskatt skall betalas till staten enligt denna lag."

    "Congestion tax shall be paid to the State in accordance with this Act." In Sweden the charge is legally a national tax, not a municipal fee. This sidestepped the constitutional limits on local charges, and it means the decision belongs to the Riksdag. A local referendum can advise but not abolish.

    Why did opinion turn? Jonas Eliasson, who led the trial's evaluation, found that people's self-interest and their belief in the charge's effect explain attitudes at any one time. But they explain little of the change. Many residents did not notice how much their own behavior had changed. People simply got used to the new normal, and the charge's benefits had become visible. Börjesson, Eliasson, Hugosson and Brundell-Freij (2012) found the traffic reduction stable five years on.

    Gothenburg shows the other side. Its congestion tax started in January 2013, mainly to co-finance the West Swedish Package of investments, including the Västlänken rail tunnel. In a non-binding referendum held alongside the September 2014 elections, about 57 % voted against it. The tax stayed, because it was tied to the financing package and only the Riksdag could remove it. Stockholm's charge was justified by congestion relief and won acceptance. Gothenburg's was framed as financing and stayed unpopular.

    6. Proposals that voters rejected A constraints

    For every scheme in operation, several have been voted down or abandoned. Two British cities held referendums before anyone had experienced a charge.

    Edinburgh, 2005. The city council proposed two cordons: one at the city bypass, crossed in the morning peak, and one around the center, charged through the working day. The revenue would fund a package of transport investment. In a postal ballot from 7 to 21 February 2005, residents were asked whether they supported the council's strategy. 74 % voted no, on a turnout of 62 % (BBC News, 22 February 2005). The council dropped the plan.

    Greater Manchester, 2008. The ten councils of Greater Manchester bid for a national government grant from the Transport Innovation Fund. The bid was for a package of transport investment worth about £3 billion, to be paid for partly by a congestion charge. In a referendum on 12 December 2008, 79 % voted against, on a turnout of 53 %. Every one of the ten boroughs said no (results as reported by the Manchester Evening News).

    New York, 2008. Mayor Michael Bloomberg's plan for a Manhattan charge needed approval from the state legislature. It never reached a vote there, and died.

    In our reading, the failures share two features. The charge was presented as part of a package, with the investments far in the future and the payment certain and near. And voters were asked before they had seen any effect.

    7. Milan, New York, Oslo A C E

    Milan: strengthened by referendum

    Milan introduced Ecopass in 2008, a charge that varied with vehicle emissions. It lost effect as the fleet renewed. In a 2011 municipal referendum, voters backed a stronger scheme. On 16 January 2012 Area C replaced it: a flat congestion charge on the historic center (the Cerchia dei Bastioni), with access bans for the most polluting vehicles. The charge stayed at €5 until 30 October 2023, when it rose to €7.50. It applies on weekdays from 07:30 to 19:30. Area C is one of the few urban charges in Europe to have been strengthened by direct democracy, and the vote came after four years of experience with Ecopass.

    New York: the first US cordon charge (2025)

    After the 2008 failure, a 2019 state law authorized the Metropolitan Transportation Authority (MTA) to charge vehicles entering Manhattan south of 60th Street. Years of federal environmental review and lawsuits followed. The MTA set the peak charge at $15. On 5 June 2024, weeks before the start, Governor Kathy Hochul paused the scheme indefinitely, saying $15 was too much for working families. In November 2024 she revived it at $9, and the MTA board approved. The Congestion Relief Zone opened on 5 January 2025. In February 2025 the new federal administration tried to withdraw its approval, and the dispute went to court. In March 2026 a federal judge ruled that the attempt was unlawful (Inside Climate News, March 2026). The charge has stayed in operation throughout.

    At the one-year mark, the Governor and the MTA reported about 11 % less traffic entering the zone (around 27 million fewer vehicles) and more than $550 million in net revenue. The revenue is pledged to back about $15 billion of MTA investment, such as new signals and step-free stations. These are official figures from the scheme's own promoters. Independent evaluations of its effects at the boundary, on low-income drivers and on air quality are still emerging.

    Oslo: a toll ring for finance

    Norway pioneered toll rings for financing roads: Bergen in 1986, Oslo in February 1990. Oslo's ring was a political compromise. Its revenue funded a package of road investments (Oslo Package 1), with a share for public transport that has grown in later packages. Its effect on traffic was small, a few percent, because the charges were set to raise money, not to relieve congestion. They now vary by time of day, with higher rush-hour rates, and by fuel type. Oslo shows that the same instrument can be set for revenue or for traffic. Set for revenue, it is mostly a transfer from drivers to the road budget. It adds to \(W\) only through \(\mu\), if it replaces more distorting taxes, and through the investments it pays for, which must pass their own appraisal. Set for traffic, it changes behavior, and that is where the larger gains lie.

    8. Who pays, who gains, and how schemes win acceptance A E constraints

    Who pays?

    Whether a charge is regressive, falling hardest on the poor, depends on who drives into the charged area. In many European and Asian city centers, peak car commuters have above-average incomes. Most low-income commuters already use transit. There, a cordon charge is progressive or neutral in its first-round effect. In car-dependent suburbs, and in US metro areas without good transit, the opposite can hold. Group weights \(\omega_g\) make the judgment explicit. In the worked example, suppose the €1,018 loss falls on a group with weight 0.8, and the revenue is spent on buses used by a group with weight 1.3. The case for the scheme then becomes stronger than the unweighted €283. If the weights are reversed, it may disappear. The minimum-access limit of Module 1 can also bind: a charge that leaves some people without any affordable way to reach work fails the limit, even if \(W\) rises.

    Uses of revenue

    Revenue is a transfer, so its welfare value is the value of what it pays for.

    • Improve the alternatives (London's buses, New York's MTA investment, Oslo's public transport). This raises access for the people priced off the road and builds a coalition of beneficiaries. Each investment still has to pass its own appraisal.
    • Cut other taxes. Each euro then gains \(\mu\), because it replaces a euro raised with distortion. Efficient, but nearly invisible to voters. This is what the Smeed panel assumed.
    • Return it as equal payments per resident. No gain through \(\mu\). But low-income households, who drive less, get back more than they pay, which counts if their weight \(\omega_g\) is high.
    • Finance roads (Oslo's early packages, Stockholm's bypass, Gothenburg). Politically easier to agree, but new capacity can attract new traffic and erode the effect of the charge (Module 5).

    The acceptance curve

    Public support for road pricing typically follows a U shape over time. It is moderate while the idea is abstract. It falls as the decision approaches and the costs become concrete. It rises after introduction, once the benefits are visible and paying becomes routine. The transport researcher Phil Goodwin called the trough the "valley of political death". Stockholm is the classic case. Gothenburg shows the curve can stay low if the benefits are not felt. Edinburgh and Manchester voted at the bottom of the valley. Several lessons recur:

    1. Trials help, because people judge outcomes, not forecasts (Stockholm 2006).
    2. Who decides, and when, matters. Referendums held before any experience usually fail (Edinburgh, Manchester). Decisions by elected bodies, followed by experience, usually stick (London, Stockholm, New York).
    3. Visible, local use of revenue helps (Milan, London, New York).
    4. Framing matters. "Less congestion" and "cleaner air" persuade more than "financing" (Stockholm against Gothenburg).
    5. Exemptions breed exemptions. Every exempted group weakens both the effect and the fairness argument.

    A political reading of \(W\): pricing has concentrated, visible losers, the drivers who pay. Its beneficiaries are diffuse: taxpayers, transit users, residents breathing cleaner air, and the drivers whose time is saved. How the revenue is used is the bridge between the efficiency case and a majority.

    9. Parking: the most common price in transport D A

    Most car trips end in a parking space, and most parking is free or underpriced. Module 1 introduced Donald Shoup and his three proposals: charge the right price for curb parking, return the revenue to the neighborhood, and abolish minimum parking requirements for buildings. In the terms of \(W\), the argument has three parts. Curb space is land, and giving it away free hides a Term D cost: the land value calculation in Module 12 puts it at about €1,500 per space per year at €3,000/m². Free curbs that are full make drivers circle in search of a space. That "cruising" costs the searchers time and adds traffic that delays everyone else, all of which shows up in Term A. And minimum parking requirements bundle the cost of parking into rents and prices, so that people who do not drive pay for it too.

    Shoup's remedy is a price that keeps a target share of spaces free on every block, so that a driver can always find one. SFpark in San Francisco (pilot launched April 2011) put the idea into practice. Meter prices were adjusted every few weeks by block and time of day to keep occupancy between 60 and 80 %. The SFMTA's 2014 evaluation reported that the target was met 31 % more often in pilot areas. The time to find a space fell by 43 %, against 13 % in control areas. Average meter prices actually fell slightly, because many blocks had been overpriced while a few were underpriced.

    Other countries regulate quantity instead of price. In Japan, a car can be registered only with proof of an off-street space (the garage certificate), and overnight street parking is banned. In Germany, a 2020 change to federal law let the states free their cities from the old cap of about €30 a year on residents' parking permits. Several cities raised fees sharply. The Federal Administrative Court annulled Freiburg's tariff in 2023 on legal grounds, showing how contested even modest steps toward cost-based parking prices remain. The parking fee, like a congestion charge, is a transfer. Its welfare effect comes from freed land, less cruising and changed travel choices.

    10. Fuel taxes and distance charges as cars electrify C E

    What a fuel tax does and does not price

    A fuel tax is an almost perfect tax on CO₂, because emissions are proportional to fuel burned. It is a poor price for congestion, crashes and local pollution, which depend on where and when one drives. Germany's energy tax has been 65.45 cents per liter of gasoline and 47.04 cents per liter of diesel since 2003. A national CO₂ price on fuels comes on top, €55 per tonne in 2025. Module 1 showed that together these come close to recent damage estimates for carbon. The US federal gasoline tax has been 18.4 cents per gallon since 1993, not adjusted for inflation.

    What changes as cars electrify

    Module 1 ("How electric cars change Term C") showed that electric cars cut CO₂ and exhaust but leave congestion, crashes, road space and tire dust unchanged. They also pay no fuel tax. Two consequences follow, and they are often confused.

    • The lost revenue is a transfer, not a loss of welfare in itself. Governments must raise the money elsewhere, and \(W\) falls only by \(\mu\) times that amount, through the cost of the replacement taxes.
    • The lost price signal matters more. Driving an electric car costs little per kilometer. People drive more (the rebound effect), while the harms that do not depend on the engine remain. Any charge that replaces the fuel tax should therefore aim at those harms: by distance, by place and time, and by weight.

    Governments are choosing among three designs, with a trade-off between privacy and precision:

    DesignExampleWhat it prices
    Flat charge per kilometer or mileOregon's OReGO, since 1 July 2015: volunteers pay 2 cents per mile and get a credit for fuel tax paid at the pump (ODOT). The United Kingdom announced in its November 2025 budget a per-mile duty on electric and plug-in hybrid cars from April 2028. Mileage is self-reported, with no tracking of where or when people drive. The rate is about half the fuel duty of an average petrol driver, around £240 a year for an average electric car (HM Treasury, Budget 2025).Road wear, crashes and space on average. Not congestion, because a rural mile costs the same as a rush-hour mile in the city.
    Distance charge by vehicle type and weightGermany's truck toll (below)Road wear, air pollution, noise, CO₂ for heavy vehicles
    Charge by place and timeSingapore's ERP 2 from 2027, using satellite positioning (section 3)Congestion where and when it occurs, if the price follows the delay

    A flat per-kilometer charge is easy to accept and protects privacy, but it cannot do the job of a congestion charge. A charge by place and time can, but it requires knowing where vehicles are. The choice is political as much as technical. The UK's explicit promise of no tracking shows which way voters' worries run.

    Germany: the truck toll and its CO₂ component

    Germany has charged a distance-based toll on heavy goods vehicles since January 2005, first on motorways and since July 2018 on all federal highways. Rates vary by weight, axles and emission class, and include the external costs of air pollution and noise allowed under the EU Eurovignette Directive. In 2023 Germany added a CO₂ component:

    Federal Ministry of Transport (BMV) — Lkw-Maut (German)

    "Am 01.12.2023 wurde die CO2-Differenzierung der Lkw-Maut eingeführt und es erfolgte beim Fahrzeuggewicht die Umstellung auf die technisch zulässige Gesamtmasse (tzGm) als für die Mauthöhe und Mautpflichtgrenze maßgebliches Merkmal."

    "On 1 December 2023 the CO₂ differentiation of the truck toll was introduced, and for vehicle weight the switch was made to the technically permissible maximum mass as the decisive characteristic for the toll rate and the threshold for liability to pay." The CO₂ surcharge corresponds to €200 per tonne of CO₂. From 1 July 2024 the toll was extended to vehicles above 3.5 t. Zero-emission trucks were exempt until the end of 2025.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Source: bmv.de.

    A CO₂ charge of €200 per tonne on trucks sits on top of the general CO₂ price on fuel. Road freight thus pays a carbon price well above most other sectors, close to France's official carbon value for 2025 (€256, Module 1).

    Under the hoodTwo more national instruments: France's payroll transit levy and Japan's expressway tolls

    France: the versement mobilité. Most French motorways are tolled and run by private concession companies; the major companies were fully privatized in 2006. Tolls are distance-based, finance construction and maintenance, and push some traffic onto parallel national roads. On the urban side, France funds public transport partly through a payroll levy on employers. It was created as the versement transport in the Paris region in 1971 and renamed versement mobilité by the 2019 mobility law (LOM).

    Service-Public.fr — Versement mobilité (French)

    "Le versement mobilité est une contribution patronale, versée par toutes les entreprises qui emploient au moins 11 salariés. Le versement mobilité permet de financer les transports en commun, il est recouvré par l'Urssaf."

    "The versement mobilité is an employer contribution paid by all businesses that employ at least 11 people. It finances public transport and is collected by Urssaf." It applies in Île-de-France and in areas whose transport authority has introduced it, with rates set locally up to legal ceilings (the highest, up to 3.2 % of payroll, in central Île-de-France). Since 2025 a regional versement mobilité can also be introduced outside Île-de-France.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Source: entreprendre.service-public.gouv.fr.

    In \(W\), the levy is a transfer from employers to the transit authority. It does not make transit cheaper for society: it is itself a tax on labor, with the distortion that \(\mu\) measures. Its case is one of fairness and politics. Employers gain from the accessibility transit gives them (larger labor markets), and the levy charges them for part of it.

    Japan: tolls with a receding horizon. Japan's national expressways are tolled, with relatively high distance-based rates and electronic toll collection (ETC) discounts by time of day. In 2005 the Japan Highway Public Corporation was privatized into three regional NEXCO operating companies and a debt-holding agency, with the promise that tolls would end once construction debt was repaid. The horizon has receded twice:

    MLIT — toll collection period after the 2023 law amendment (Japanese)

    「更新・進化のため、料金徴収期間を延長し、必要な事業を追加。……なお、料金徴収期間の延長は、最長でも令和97年（2115年）までと設定（料金徴収期限）。」

    "To renew and upgrade [the expressways], the toll collection period is extended and the necessary projects are added. … The extension of the toll collection period is capped at 2115 at the latest (toll collection deadline)." The original deadline was 2050, extended to 2065 by a 2014 amendment and to a maximum of 2115 by the 2023 amendment.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Source: MLIT council document (2023 Road Development Special Measures Act amendment).

    Japanese tolls are set to recover costs, not to manage congestion. As payments they are transfers from users to the operator. Their main welfare effect is a side effect noted by Japanese economists: high tolls push traffic, including trucks, onto parallel untolled roads through towns, which raises crashes, noise and pollution there (Term C). That is the "untolled alternatives" problem of section 2 in practice.
