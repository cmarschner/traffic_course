    Part IV · Policy levers · Module 13

    Pricing and demand management

    ≈ 4 hours · Prerequisites: Modules 2, 3 and 7 · Key terms: A (consumer surplus), C (external congestion and emissions), E (revenue, \(\mu\)), weights \(\omega_g\)

    Economists have recommended road pricing for a century, and few policies have been as unpopular before introduction and as accepted afterwards. This module derives the Pigouvian toll, works through a numerical example, and then follows the real schemes from Singapore's 1975 paper licences to New York's 2025 congestion charge. The welfare objective clarifies what pricing does and does not achieve: it gains through changes in behaviour, while the revenue itself is a transfer whose value depends on how it is used and on who pays.

    Learning outcomes
    • Derive average and marginal social cost from a link performance function and compute the optimal (Pigouvian) toll.
    • Decompose the welfare effect of a toll into the change in consumer surplus (Term A), revenue (Term E, with \(\mu\)) and other externalities (Term C), and show why most drivers lose before revenue is recycled.
    • Describe the design and outcomes of the Singapore, London, Stockholm, Gothenburg, Milan, New York and Oslo schemes.
    • Explain price and quantity instruments (ERP vs COE), parking pricing, fuel taxes, distance-based truck tolls and France's payroll transit levy.
    • Use distributional weights \(\omega_g\) to evaluate revenue recycling, and explain the political acceptance curve.

    1. The idea: drivers do not pay for the delay they cause C

    A. C. Pigou (The Economics of Welfare, 1920) used a congested road as an example of an activity whose private cost is below its social cost. Each additional vehicle on a congested road slows down all the others. The driver bears their own travel time but not the extra time imposed on everyone else, so too many trips are made at the busiest times. A charge equal to the external cost at the optimum, the Pigouvian toll, aligns private and social cost. William Vickrey, later a Nobel laureate, proposed peak pricing for New York's subway in the 1950s and electronic road pricing in the 1960s. Alan Walters measured the gap between private and social cost of congestion in 1961. In the UK, the Smeed Report (1964) found road pricing technically feasible. Implementation lagged theory by decades, for political rather than technical reasons.

    2. Theory with a BPR link A C E

    Average and marginal cost

    Let \(q\) be the flow (veh/h) on a link with free-flow time \(t_0\) and capacity \(C\). The Bureau of Public Roads (BPR) function from Module 7 gives the travel time

    $$t(q)=t_0\Big[1+\alpha\Big(\frac{q}{C}\Big)^{\beta}\Big],\qquad \alpha=0.15,\ \beta=4 .$$

    With value of time \(\mathrm{VOT}\), each driver experiences the average cost \(AC(q)=\mathrm{VOT}\cdot t(q)\). Total cost is \(TC=q\cdot AC(q)\). Adding one vehicle raises total cost by the marginal social cost

    $$MSC(q)=\frac{d\,TC}{dq}=AC(q)+q\,AC'(q)=\mathrm{VOT}\,t_0\Big[1+\alpha(1+\beta)\Big(\frac{q}{C}\Big)^{\beta}\Big].$$

    The second term, \(q\,AC'(q)=\mathrm{VOT}\,t_0\,\alpha\beta\,(q/C)^{\beta}\), is the external congestion cost: the delay one more vehicle imposes on all the others. Travellers choose \(q\) where their willingness to pay, the inverse demand \(D(q)\), equals the cost they bear, \(AC\). Society would choose \(q^{*}\) where \(D(q^{*})=MSC(q^{*})\). The optimal toll is the gap at the optimum:

    $$\tau^{*}=MSC(q^{*})-AC(q^{*})=q^{*}AC'(q^{*}).$$

    In a supply–demand diagram, \(AC\) and \(MSC\) are rising curves with \(MSC\) above \(AC\). Demand slopes down. The untolled equilibrium is where demand crosses \(AC\), the optimum is where it crosses \(MSC\), and the deadweight loss is the triangle between \(MSC\) and \(D\) from \(q^{*}\) to the equilibrium flow \(q_e\). The worked example computes each element.

    Worked example · Pricing a BPR link

    Inputs: \(t_0=10\) min, \(C=2{,}000\) veh/h, \(\mathrm{VOT}=€15\)/h, so \(\mathrm{VOT}\cdot t_0=€2.50\). Inverse demand \(D(q)=6-0.0015\,q\) (€ per trip). Write \(x=q/C\).

    1. No toll. Solve \(D=AC\): \(6-3x=2.5+0.375x^{4}\). Then \(x_e\approx1.027\), so \(q_e\approx2{,}055\) veh/h. Generalised cost \(AC=€2.92\) (travel time 11.7 min).
    2. Optimum. Solve \(D=MSC\): \(6-3x=2.5+1.875x^{4}\). Then \(x^{*}\approx0.846\), so \(q^{*}\approx1{,}692\) veh/h. Travel time 10.8 min, \(AC=€2.69\).
    3. Toll. \(\tau^{*}=1.5\,x^{*4}=€0.77\) per trip. The full price \(2.69+0.77=€3.46\) equals \(D(q^{*})\) ✓.
    4. Efficiency gain. \(\displaystyle\int_{q^{*}}^{q_e}\big(MSC-D\big)\,dq=2000\int_{0.846}^{1.027}\big(1.875x^{4}+3x-3.5\big)dx\approx €283\) per hour.
    5. Revenue. \(R=\tau^{*}q^{*}\approx €1{,}301\) per hour.
    6. Consumer surplus of travellers (Term A). The price they face rises from €2.92 to €3.46, so \(\Delta CS=-\int_{2.92}^{3.46}q(p)\,dp\approx -€1{,}017\) per hour.
    Flow (veh/h)Time (min)Toll (€)Full price (€)
    No toll2,05511.702.92
    Optimal toll1,69210.80.773.46

    Check: \(-1{,}017+1{,}301=284\approx283\). The efficiency gain is the sum of a large loss to travellers and a slightly larger revenue. Those who keep driving pay €0.77 to save 0.9 minutes, worth €0.23 at their VOT. Before the revenue is used, they are worse off. Those priced off the road are worse off too. Pricing benefits travellers directly only when VOT is heterogeneous: people with high values of time gain, which is one reason pricing is contested on equity grounds.

    Where it sits in \(W\)

    The welfare change of introducing the toll is

    $$\Delta W=\underbrace{\sum_g\omega_g\,\Delta CS_g}_{\text{A}}\;-\;\underbrace{\sum_k p_k\,\Delta E_k}_{\text{C: emissions, crashes, noise}}\;+\;\underbrace{(1+\mu)\,R}_{\text{E}}\;-\;\underbrace{(1+\mu)\,\Delta K}_{\text{E: collection cost}} .$$

    Three points follow.

    1. The delay externality is already counted. Here all affected parties are drivers on the link, so the external congestion cost shows up as time savings inside \(\Delta CS\). Adding it again in Term C would double-count (see Module 1). Term C captures the other externalities that fall with fewer vehicles: CO₂, air pollution, noise and crashes. These are often of the same order as the congestion gain.
    2. Revenue use matters through \(\mu\) and \(\omega_g\). With \(\mu=0.2\), the €1,301 of revenue is worth €1,561 because it replaces distorting taxes, and the net gain rises from €284 to €544 per hour. If revenue is returned as transfers \(T_g\), the expression becomes \(\sum_g\omega_g(\Delta CS_g+T_g)+(1+\mu)\big(R-\sum_g T_g\big)\). Transfers only add welfare if they go to groups with \(\omega_g>1+\mu\).
    3. Collection costs can eat the gain. London's original scheme was criticised for high operating costs relative to revenue. Satellite and camera systems have lowered these costs, but \(\Delta K\) belongs in the appraisal.

    The classic engineering objective \(\min\sum q\,t\) cannot evaluate pricing at all: it holds demand fixed, so a toll changes nothing. Even a fixed-demand model with tolls treated as cost would count revenue as a loss to travellers and miss its return through Term E. Pricing is one of the clearest cases where the choice of objective function decides the answer.

    Second-best and practical complications

    • Cordon and area charges are blunt: one price for every trip that crosses a line, regardless of distance or route. They capture a part of the first-best gain, often a large part when the cordon matches the congested core.
    • Untolled alternatives. If a parallel road or a different time is untolled, the optimal toll on the tolled facility is lower than the Pigouvian rate (second-best pricing).
    • Dynamic congestion. In the bottleneck model of Vickrey (1969), a time-varying toll can remove queuing entirely by spreading departures. The efficiency gain then comes from eliminating queues, not from reducing the number of trips.
    • Transit crowding. Diverted drivers board buses and trains. If those are crowded, a crowding externality appears there (Module 6).

    3. Singapore: price and quantity together C constraints

    Singapore introduced the world's first urban congestion charge, the Area Licensing Scheme (ALS), on 2 June 1975. Drivers had to buy and display a paper licence to enter a restricted zone of about 6 km² in the city centre during the morning peak, checked by staff at entry points. The hours were extended to the evening peak in 1989 and to the whole working day in 1994. On 1 April 1998 the Electronic Road Pricing (ERP) system replaced it: gantries charge an in-vehicle unit per passage, at rates that vary by location and half-hour. The Land Transport Authority (LTA) reviews the rates regularly against target speed bands, raising the charge where speeds fall below the band and lowering it where traffic flows freely. This is an empirical feedback form of Pigouvian pricing. The next step is ERP 2.0, which uses satellite positioning and a new on-board unit. In September 2026 the LTA announced that the ERP 2 system will launch on 1 January 2027 and that more than 98 % of eligible Singapore-registered vehicles had been fitted with the new unit.

    Singapore also caps the stock of vehicles. Since May 1990, anyone registering a vehicle needs a Certificate of Entitlement (COE), valid for ten years, bought in an auction for a fixed quota. The quota is set from an allowed vehicle-population growth rate, which the government cut to zero for cars and motorcycles in February 2018. COE premiums for cars have at times exceeded S$100,000.

    The COE premium is a shadow price

    In \(W\), the COE implements a constraint on the vehicle stock, \(N\le\bar N\), which is a version of the land constraint \(L\le\bar L\). The auction finds the price at which demand equals the quota, so the COE premium is a market estimate of the Lagrange multiplier on the constraint. ERP is the matching price instrument for use. Weitzman's (1974) analysis of prices and quantities applies: a quota fixes the quantity and lets the price vary, while a tax does the reverse. Which is better depends on whether the cost of getting the quantity wrong is steeper than the cost of getting the price wrong. Singapore uses the quantity tool where land is the hard limit and the price tool where conditions vary by hour and place.

    4. London: congestion charge and ULEZ C E

    London's congestion charge started on 17 February 2003 at £5 per day for driving in central London on weekdays, enforced by automatic number-plate recognition cameras. The charge rose in steps to £15 (2020) and to £18 from 2 January 2026. It now applies 07:00–18:00 on weekdays and 12:00–18:00 at weekends and bank holidays. Under the Greater London Authority Act 1999, net revenues must be spent on transport in London. Electric cars lost their full exemption in January 2026 and now receive a discount. Transport for London's monitoring reported large reductions in traffic and congestion in the first years. Congestion later returned toward earlier levels, as road space was reallocated to buses, cycling and public space, and as the number of private-hire vehicles grew. In \(W\) this need not be a failure: it can mean that the scheme freed road space that was then used for other purposes with value in Terms A, C and D.

    The Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ) started in central London in April 2019, expanded to inner London in October 2021 and to all of Greater London in August 2023. It is a daily charge on vehicles that do not meet emission standards. It targets Term C (air pollution) rather than congestion. Its London-wide expansion was a major issue in the July 2023 Uxbridge by-election, an example of pricing politics at the urban fringe, where public transport alternatives are weaker.

    5. Stockholm and Gothenburg: the acceptance swing A E

    Stockholm ran a full-scale congestion charging trial from 3 January to 31 July 2006, with charges varying by time of day at a cordon around the inner city. Traffic across the cordon fell by roughly 20 %, more than the 10–15 % planners had hoped for, and queues fell much more because traffic near capacity is highly non-linear (the BPR curve above). In a referendum in September 2006, a majority of voters in the City of Stockholm voted to keep the charge, although many surrounding municipalities voted against in their own consultations. The charge became permanent on 1 August 2007.

    The striking finding is the shift in opinion. Before the trial a clear majority of Stockholm residents opposed the charge. After the trial, and in the years after the permanent scheme began, support rose to a majority. Börjesson, Eliasson, Hugosson and Brundell-Freij (2012) attribute this to visible travel-time gains, to the status quo effect (people get used to the new normal) and to the scheme's benefits becoming easier to see than its costs.

    Lag (2004:629) om trängselskatt, § 1 (Swedish)

    "Trängselskatt skall betalas till staten enligt denna lag."

    "Congestion tax shall be paid to the State in accordance with this Act." In Sweden the charge is legally a national tax, not a municipal fee. This sidestepped the constitutional limits on local charges, and it means the decision belongs to the Riksdag. A local referendum can advise but not abolish.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Official legal text, riksdagen.se.

    Gothenburg shows the other side. Its congestion tax started in January 2013, mainly to co-finance the West Swedish Package, including the Västlänken rail tunnel. In a non-binding referendum held alongside the September 2014 elections, about 57 % voted against it. The tax stayed, because it was tied to the financing package and only the Riksdag could remove it. Stockholm's charge was justified by congestion relief and won acceptance. Gothenburg's was framed as financing and stayed unpopular.

    6. Milan, New York, Oslo C E

    Milan: from pollution charge to congestion charge

    Milan introduced Ecopass in 2008, a charge that varied with vehicle emissions. It lost effect as the fleet renewed. In a 2011 municipal referendum, voters backed a stronger scheme, and on 16 January 2012 Area C replaced it as a flat congestion charge on the historic centre (the Cerchia dei Bastioni), with access bans for the most polluting vehicles. The charge stayed at €5 until 30 October 2023, when it rose to €7.50. It applies on weekdays from 07:30 to 19:30. Area C is one of the few urban charges in Europe to have been strengthened by direct democracy.

    New York: the first US cordon charge (2025)

    New York's first attempt, Mayor Bloomberg's 2008 plan, died in the state legislature. A 2019 state law authorised the Metropolitan Transportation Authority (MTA) to charge vehicles entering Manhattan south of 60th Street. After years of federal environmental review, litigation and a last-minute pause in 2024, the Congestion Relief Zone opened on 5 January 2025 with a peak charge of $9 for passenger cars paying by E-ZPass. The new federal administration tried to withdraw its approval in early 2025, and the dispute went to court. The charge stayed in operation. At the one-year mark, the State Governor and the MTA reported about 11 % less traffic entering the zone (around 27 million fewer vehicles) and more than $550 million in net revenue. The revenue is pledged to back about $15 billion of MTA capital investment such as signals and station accessibility. These are official figures from the scheme's proponents. Independent evaluations of effects on boundary areas, on low-income drivers and on air quality are still emerging.

    Oslo: a toll ring for finance

    Norway pioneered toll rings for infrastructure finance: Bergen in 1986, Oslo in February 1990. Oslo's ring was a political compromise. Revenues funded a package of road investments (Oslo Package 1), with a share for public transport that has grown in later packages. Its traffic effect was small (a few per cent), because charges were set for revenue, not for congestion. Charges have since become time-differentiated with rush-hour rates and differentiated by fuel type. In \(W\) terms, Oslo shows that the same instrument can serve Term E (finance) or Terms A and C (demand management). The price level and its time profile decide which.

    7. Parking: the most common price in transport D C

    Most car trips end in a parking space, and most parking is free or underpriced. Donald Shoup (The High Cost of Free Parking, 2005) argued that free kerb parking causes cruising (drivers circling for a space, adding traffic), that minimum parking requirements for buildings bundle the cost of parking into rents and prices, and that cities should price kerb space to keep a target occupancy (he suggested about 85 %) and spend the revenue locally. The land value calculation in Module 12 (about €1,500 per space per year at €3,000/m²) shows the size of the implicit subsidy, a Term D cost that nobody sees.

    SFpark in San Francisco (pilot launched April 2011) put the idea into practice. Meter prices were adjusted every few weeks by block and time of day to keep occupancy between 60 and 80 %. The SFMTA's 2014 evaluation reported that the target was met 31 % more often in pilot areas, that the time to find a space fell by 43 % (against 13 % in control areas), and that average meter prices actually fell slightly, because many blocks had been overpriced while a few were underpriced. Japan regulates quantity instead: the garage certificate requires proof of an off-street space before a car can be registered, and overnight street parking is banned. In Germany, a 2020 change to federal law let the states free cities from the old cap of about €30 per year on residents' parking permits. Several cities raised fees sharply, and the Federal Administrative Court annulled Freiburg's tariff in 2023 on legal grounds, showing how contested even modest steps toward cost-based parking prices remain.

    8. Fuel taxes, truck tolls and other national instruments C E

    Fuel taxes

    A fuel tax is an almost perfect Pigouvian tax on CO₂, because emissions are proportional to fuel burned. It is a poor tax on congestion, crashes and local pollution, which depend on where and when one drives. Germany's energy tax has been 65.45 cents per litre of petrol and 47.04 cents per litre of diesel since 2003, plus a national CO₂ price on fuels under the Fuel Emissions Trading Act (BEHG), which rose to €55 per tonne in 2025. The US federal gasoline tax has been 18.4 cents per gallon since 1993, not indexed to inflation. As vehicles electrify, fuel-tax revenue shrinks and the case for distance-based road user charging grows. Several US states run pilot "road usage charge" programmes.

    Germany: the truck toll and its CO₂ component

    Germany has charged a distance-based toll on heavy goods vehicles since January 2005, first on motorways and since July 2018 on all federal highways. Rates are differentiated by weight, axles and emission class and include external costs of air pollution and noise allowed under the EU Eurovignette Directive. In 2023 Germany added a CO₂ component:

    Federal Ministry of Transport (BMV) — Lkw-Maut (German)

    "Am 01.12.2023 wurde die CO2-Differenzierung der Lkw-Maut eingeführt und es erfolgte beim Fahrzeuggewicht die Umstellung auf die technisch zulässige Gesamtmasse (tzGm) als für die Mauthöhe und Mautpflichtgrenze maßgebliches Merkmal."

    "On 1 December 2023 the CO₂ differentiation of the truck toll was introduced, and for vehicle weight the switch was made to the technically permissible maximum mass as the decisive characteristic for the toll rate and the threshold for liability to pay." The CO₂ surcharge corresponds to €200 per tonne of CO₂. From 1 July 2024 the toll was extended to vehicles above 3.5 t. Zero-emission trucks were exempt until the end of 2025.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Source: bmv.de.

    A CO₂ charge of €200/t on trucks sits on top of the general fuel CO₂ price, so road freight pays a carbon price well above most other sectors. In \(W\), this is close to France's high valeur tutélaire for carbon, with the carbon budget \(E_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\le B\) behind it.

    France: motorway concessions and the versement mobilité

    Most French motorways are tolled and run by private concession companies (the major companies were fully privatised in 2006). Tolls are distance-based, finance construction and maintenance, and divert some traffic onto parallel national roads. On the urban side, France funds public transport partly through a payroll levy on employers, created as the versement transport in the Paris region in 1971 and renamed versement mobilité by the 2019 mobility law (LOM):

    Service-Public.fr — Versement mobilité (French)

    "Le versement mobilité est une contribution patronale, versée par toutes les entreprises qui emploient au moins 11 salariés. Le versement mobilité permet de financer les transports en commun, il est recouvré par l'Urssaf."

    "The versement mobilité is an employer contribution paid by all businesses that employ at least 11 people. It finances public transport and is collected by Urssaf." It applies in Île-de-France and in areas whose transport authority has introduced it, with rates set locally up to legal ceilings (the highest, up to 3.2 % of payroll, in central Île-de-France). Since 2025 a regional versement mobilité can also be introduced outside Île-de-France.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Source: entreprendre.service-public.gouv.fr.

    In \(W\), the levy is a value-capture instrument. Employers gain from the accessibility that transit gives them (larger labour markets, Term A agglomeration effects), and the levy charges them for part of it. It lowers \(K-R\) for the transit authority but is itself a tax, so it carries the \(\mu\) premium like any other.

    Japan: tolled expressways with a receding horizon

    Japan's national expressways are tolled, with relatively high distance-based rates and electronic toll collection (ETC) discounts by time of day. In 2005 the Japan Highway Public Corporation was privatised into three regional NEXCO operating companies and a debt-holding agency, with the promise that tolls would end once construction debt was repaid. The horizon has receded twice:

    MLIT — toll collection period after the 2023 law amendment (Japanese)

    「更新・進化のため、料金徴収期間を延長し、必要な事業を追加。……なお、料金徴収期間の延長は、最長でも令和97年（2115年）までと設定（料金徴収期限）。」

    "To renew and upgrade [the expressways], the toll collection period is extended and the necessary projects are added. … The extension of the toll collection period is capped at 2115 at the latest (toll collection deadline)." The original deadline was 2050, extended to 2065 by a 2014 amendment and to a maximum of 2115 by the 2023 amendment.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Source: MLIT council document (2023 Road Development Special Measures Act amendment).

    Japanese tolls are set to recover costs, not to manage congestion, so they are a Term E instrument. A side effect noted by Japanese economists is that high tolls push traffic, including trucks, onto parallel untolled ordinary roads through towns, which raises Term C there. That is the second-best problem of Section 2 in practice.

    9. Revenue, equity and political economy A E constraints

    Who pays?

    Whether pricing is regressive depends on who drives into the charged area. In many European and Asian city centres, peak car commuters have above-average incomes, and most low-income commuters already use transit, so a cordon charge is progressive or neutral in its first-round effect. In car-dependent suburbs and in US metro areas without good transit, the opposite can hold. With distributional weights \(\omega_g\), the analyst can make the judgement explicit. In the worked example, if the €1,017 loss falls on a group with \(\omega=0.8\) and the revenue is spent on buses used by a group with \(\omega=1.3\), the case for the scheme becomes stronger than the unweighted €284. If the weights are reversed, it may disappear. The equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\) can also bind: a charge that leaves some people without any affordable way to reach work fails the constraint even if \(W\) rises.

    Uses of revenue

    • Improve the alternatives (London buses, New York's MTA capital plan, Oslo's public transport packages). This raises the logsum for the people priced off the road and builds a coalition of beneficiaries.
    • Return it as lump sums or tax cuts. Efficient if \(\mu>0\) and the returns are well targeted, but less visible.
    • Finance roads (Oslo's early packages, Gothenburg). Politically easier to agree, but the new capacity can erode the demand-management effect.

    The acceptance curve

    Public support for road pricing typically follows a U shape over time. It is moderate when the idea is abstract, falls as the decision approaches and costs become concrete, and rises after introduction once the benefits are visible and the payment becomes routine. Phil Goodwin called the trough the "valley of political death". Stockholm is the canonical case. Gothenburg shows the curve can stay low if the benefits are not felt. Several lessons recur:

    1. Trials help because people judge outcomes, not forecasts (Stockholm 2006).
    2. Visible, local use of revenue helps (Milan, London, New York).
    3. Framing matters. "Congestion" and "clean air" persuade more than "financing" (Stockholm vs Gothenburg).
    4. Who decides matters. Referendums before a trial usually fail. Decisions by elected bodies, followed by experience, usually stick. Edinburgh rejected a charge in a 2005 referendum, before any experience of it.
    5. Exemptions breed exemptions. Every exempted group weakens both the effect and the fairness argument.

    A political-economy reading of \(W\): pricing has concentrated, visible losers (drivers who pay, \(\Delta CS<0\)) and diffuse beneficiaries (taxpayers, transit users, residents breathing cleaner air). Revenue recycling is the bridge between the efficiency case and a majority.
