    Part III · Traffic engineering · Module 10

    Simulation, calibration and verification

    ≈ 6 hours · Prerequisites: Module 4, Module 7, Module 9; basic statistics · Key terms: A C and the whole of \(W\)

    Simulation models are how most transport plans are tested before they are built, so their errors become the plan's errors. This module separates three questions that practice often blurs: is the model built right (verification), has it been tuned to local data (calibration), and does it predict what it is used to predict (validation)? It compares how Japan, the US, the UK, Singapore, Spain, France and Germany answer them, explains why a good fit to counts can hide a bad model, and ends with what a simulation can and cannot tell you about \(W\).

    Learning outcomes
    • Define verification, calibration and validation and give an example of a failure in each.
    • Describe the Japanese JSTE standard verification process and its six test phenomena.
    • Compute GEH, %RMSE and journey-time criteria and apply the UK TAG, Spanish, Singaporean and FHWA acceptance rules.
    • Explain overfitting in OD-matrix estimation and network tuning, and design a validation with independent data.
    • Choose between microscopic (SUMO), mesoscopic and agent-based (MATSim) tools for a given question.
    • State which terms of \(W\) a calibrated traffic simulation can estimate, and which require other models.
    • Design a verification suite of synthetic networks for a simulator.

    1. Three different questions

    StepQuestionCompared againstTypical failure
    VerificationDoes the program do what its model says?Theory, on idealised test networksA merge that discharges 10% above the specified capacity; queues that do not spill back
    CalibrationWhich parameter values fit this place?Local observations used for fittingDemand inflated to hit counts while the real cause is a mis-coded junction
    ValidationDoes the fitted model predict data it has not seen, for the use intended?Independent observations: other days, other sites, before–after changesPerfect base-year fit; wrong response to a road closure

    The terms are used inconsistently. US guidance often calls the whole process "calibration"; the UK's TAG calls its count comparisons "validation" even when the same counts helped build the matrix. The useful distinction is about information: data used to set parameters cannot validate them.

    2. Japan: verification as a public, standard test

    Around 2000, a working group of the Japan Society of Civil Engineers and then the Traffic Simulation Committee of the Japan Society of Traffic Engineers (JSTE), chaired by Masao Kuwahara (University of Tokyo), with Ryota Horiguchi and Toshio Yoshii, published a Standard Verification Process for Traffic Flow Simulation Models. Its premise was that simulators had become black boxes whose results depended on the model and the operator, so users could not choose between them. The response was a common set of tests on virtual networks, and a public "clearinghouse" where developers post their results.

    Japanese · JSTE Traffic Simulation Clearinghouse

    モデル検証プロセスは大きく2段階に分けられますが，ここでは理想的なデータセットを使って，モデルの動作原理が正しく機能しているかどうかをチェックする「基本検証(verification)」の結果を紹介します．

    "The model verification process is broadly divided into two stages. Here we present the results of 'basic verification', which uses idealised data sets to check whether the model's operating principles function correctly." The clearinghouse also records the criticism it responds to: that simulation is "a black box, so it is not clear what calculation is being done", and that "if the model or the operator differs, the answers to the same problem vary". The second stage, validation (妥当性確認) against real data, was to use shared benchmark data sets. Source: JSTE Traffic Simulation Clearinghouse pages (archived 2025).     The six basic verification tests, as listed in the clearinghouse table and the English manual:

    The archived clearinghouse lists results for about twenty models, including AIMSUN, VISSIM, Paramics and many Japanese models (AVENUE, SOUND, NETSTREAM, SIPA and others), each marked as published, in preparation, or not applicable. Two ideas from the manual deserve to travel. First, it explicitly states that verification is not about whether the model agrees with real traffic, and not about ranking models: it checks that each model does what it claims, so that users understand its behavior. Second, it shows why the difference between point queues and physical queues matters: in a physical queue, vehicles that do not need to pass the bottleneck are delayed by the spillback, in a point queue they are not. A model can fit link counts with either representation and still allocate delay to entirely different people.

    3. Calibration in practice: what the guidance says

    United States: FHWA Traffic Analysis Toolbox Volume III (2019 update)

    FHWA's guidance for microsimulation calibration starts from day-to-day variability. Analysts cluster observed days into "travel conditions" (for example, normal weekday, incident, rain), choose for each a representative day closest to the cluster's average time profile, and calibrate a model variant to that day, never to a synthetic average day that combines bottlenecks which never occur together. Bottleneck throughput and its timing are calibrated first, then demand. Acceptance uses four criteria per key measure, applied to time series (for example 15-minute travel times):

    1. 95% of simulated values lie within about ±2 standard deviations of the representative day (the day-to-day spread within the cluster);
    2. two-thirds of values, and both critical intervals (for example the two highest travel times), lie within ±1 standard deviation;
    3. the mean absolute error to the representative day is no larger than the typical difference between the representative day and other days in the cluster (the "bounded dynamic absolute error");
    4. the simulation is not systematically biased by more than a third of that bound.

    The logic is welfare-relevant: a model need not be closer to one day than real days are to each other. FHWA also notes that variation between random seeds is small compared with day-to-day variation, and that large seed-to-seed differences usually indicate coding errors or gridlock rather than genuine behavior.

    United Kingdom: TAG unit M3.1

    The Department for Transport's TAG unit M3.1 (Highway Assignment Modeling; updated May 2026) sets widely copied acceptability guidelines for strategic highway models. Flows are compared with counts using the GEH statistic

    $$\text{GEH}=\sqrt{\frac{2\,(M-C)^2}{M+C}},$$

    with \(M\) the modeled and \(C\) the observed hourly flow. The statistic is named after its inventor, Geoffrey E. Havers. TAG's criteria:

    CriterionGuideline
    Flows < 700 veh/h within ±100 veh/h; 700–2,700 within ±15%; > 2,700 within ±400 veh/h> 85% of cases
    GEH < 5> 85% of cases
    Modeled journey times within 15% of observed (or 1 minute, if higher)> 85% of routes

    TAG asks for these comparisons on counts that were not used in matrix estimation where possible, and limits how far matrix estimation may change the prior matrix: for example, a regression of zonal cell values after against before should have a slope between 0.98 and 1.02 and \(R^2\) above 0.95, and mean trip lengths should change by less than 5% (TAG M3.1, Table 5). Those rules are as important as the thresholds.

    Singapore: LTA transport impact assessments

    The Land Transport Authority's 2020 addendum to its Transport Impact Assessment guidelines requires developers' consultants to submit link and turn GEH tables with \(R^2\) plots, queue-length comparisons, and travel-time calibration on agreed corridors, measured by floating car or extracted from Google Maps with timestamps. Acceptable travel-time error is ±1 minute for 3–5 minute trips, ±2 for 6–10, ±3 for 11–15, ±4 for 16–20 and ±20% above 20 minutes. Validation is separate: observed versus modeled average speeds on key corridors and the number of signal cycles needed to clear each junction. Changes to default driver-behavior parameters require prior LTA approval and must be listed with justifications. This last rule is an anti-overfitting device.

    Spain: %RMSE and a hold-out sample

    Spanish · Nota de Servicio 5/2014, §6.3

    El valor de %RMSE debe ser inferior al 30%. En redes complejas con muchos tramos, para asegurar la validez de la calibración deberá de cumplirse también esta condición en una muestra de las observaciones, cuyos valores no hayan sido utilizados en la calibración del modelo. El tamaño de esta muestra de contraste será al menos del 10% de los aforos.

    "The %RMSE must be below 30%. In complex networks with many links, to ensure the validity of the calibration, this condition must also be met in a sample of observations whose values were not used in calibrating the model. The size of this test sample shall be at least 10% of the counts." The note also asks for a regression of modeled on observed volumes with slope near 1, intercept near 0 and \(r^2\) desirably above 0.7, and for sensitivity tests on value of time and toll sensitivity. Source: Dirección General de Carreteras, Nota de Servicio 5/2014. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Here \(\%\text{RMSE}=100\,\sqrt{\sum_i(E_i-O_i)^2/(N-1)}\,\big/\,(\sum_i O_i/N)\). Spain's explicit hold-out sample is a small rule with large consequences: it is the simplest guard against fitting noise.

    France: do not chase a perfect fit

    French · CEREMA (2019), Recommendations for traffic studies and socio-economic appraisals of road projects

    Il est utile de rappeler qu'il ne faut pas chercher un calage parfait du modèle. Au contraire, au-delà d'un certain point, améliorer artificiellement la qualité d'un modèle se fera aux dépens de la capacité du modèle à se comporter de façon adéquate en prévision.

    "It is worth recalling that one should not seek a perfect calibration of the model. On the contrary, beyond a certain point, artificially improving the quality of a model will come at the expense of the model's ability to behave adequately in forecasting." The same section says calibration "is often not sufficient to ensure validity", distinguishes validation on another data set (another year, data from before a past project opened) from continuous maintenance of a model across projects, and asks that validation include checking that the model's elasticities have plausible orders of magnitude. It asks reports to map GEH values and all network modifications made during calibration, and warns that errors in reproduced travel-time savings have a "double impact": on route choice in the traffic study and on total time savings in the appraisal. Source: Cerema, Recommandations pour la réalisation d'études de trafic et d'évaluations socio-économiques de projets d'infrastructure de transport routier, §3.2.3. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Germany: research on calibration and the Stuttgart school

    German practice relies on the FGSV's guidance on transport models and on strong university groups. At the University of Stuttgart, Markus Friedrich's chair (Verkehrsplanung und Verkehrsleittechnik) proposed the scalable GEH (SQV), a bounded 0–1 quality measure for comparing observed and modeled values that adds a scaling factor so the same threshold can be used for hourly and daily volumes (Friedrich, Pestel, Schiller and Simon, 2019). The chair's current projects include SEED (2025–2028), a single-source approach to jointly estimating origin–destination relationships and calibrating demand models, and specification and quality assurance for the update of the Munich city and regional demand model (2025–2027). The SQV is a model-fit statistic; it has nothing to do with the HBS quality levels (QSV) in Module 8.

    4. GEH and its limits

    GEH is popular because one threshold works across flow levels: it behaves like a relative error at high flows and an absolute error at low flows. Three examples: \(M=1100, C=1000\) gives GEH 3.1; \(M=5300, C=5000\) gives 4.2; \(M=150, C=100\) gives 4.5. All "pass", though the last is a 50% error. Limits to keep in mind:

    • It is defined for hourly flows. Applied to daily volumes, the same threshold is far stricter; applied to 15-minute counts, far looser. The SQV's scaling factor addresses this.
    • It ignores spatial correlation. 85% of links passing can hide one corridor where every link is 12% high because a single OD pair is wrong.
    • It says nothing about speeds, queues or delay. A model can match flows downstream of a bottleneck exactly (they equal capacity) while its queue is in the wrong place. Delay is what enters \(W\).
    • It rewards the wrong fixes. Flows can be matched by distorting demand, link capacities or route-choice parameters. Only some of these are true.

    Good practice reports distributions of errors by road class, volume range, time of day and area, maps them (as CEREMA asks), and always includes travel times and, in congested networks, queue lengths and bottleneck activation times (as FHWA and LTA ask).

    5. Overfitting and identifiability

    A city model with 1,000 zones has about a million OD cells, and perhaps a few hundred counts per hour. OD-matrix estimation (matrix correction) adjusts the prior matrix until assigned flows match counts. With so many more unknowns than observations, an infinite number of matrices fit the counts equally well; the method picks the one closest to the prior. If the prior is poor, or if network errors are being absorbed by the matrix, the fitted matrix will reproduce counts and mispredict everything else: trip lengths, mode shares, and the response to a new road.

    Symptoms of overfitting:

    • large changes to the prior matrix, especially in short trips or intrazonal cells; trip-length distributions that drift away from survey data;
    • many local edits to link capacities, speeds or "dummy" penalties, each justified only by fit;
    • excellent fit on calibration counts, noticeably worse on hold-out counts;
    • implausible elasticities, for example a road closure that diverts almost all traffic with no change in total travel.

    Remedies are the ones the guidance already contains: hold out counts (Spain: at least 10%), validate on another period or a past intervention (France), limit parameter changes and document them (Singapore), use time series rather than single averages (FHWA), and check elasticities. Parameter identifiability deserves a test of its own: if two parameters (say, the IDM time gap and a link capacity factor) can be traded off with no change in fit, the data cannot tell you which is right, and the model's response to a design change may depend on the arbitrary choice.

    7. What a simulation can and cannot say about \(W\)

    TermA calibrated traffic simulation can estimate……but needs other models or data for
    AShort-run changes in travel time, delay and reliability for today's demand on the modeled networkInduced demand, mode shift, destination and departure-time change (demand models, Module 4); land-use response (Module 5); option value of new modes
    BPedestrian and cyclist delay, if they are modeledChanges in walking and cycling volumes and their health effects (Module 6)
    CSpeed profiles and stops for emission and noise models; external congestion cost; surrogate safety indicators (time-to-collision, conflicts)Crash numbers and severity (crash prediction models, Module 11): simulated drivers do not crash; severance
    DNothing directlyLand take from the design drawings; land value
    EOperating cost of signals, transit run timesConstruction cost, revenue

    The most common misuse is to run a microsimulation of a road widening or a road diet with a fixed OD matrix and report the change in vehicle-hours as the benefit. That is the classic objective \(\min\sum q\,t\) of Module 1 with all five simplifications. It is legitimate for operational questions over weeks; it is not a welfare estimate for a change that will alter travel behavior for decades. A simulation report should say so explicitly.

    8. A proposal: a verification suite of synthetic networks

    Building on the JSTE tests and the national capacity methods of Modules 7–9, a simulator used for planning should pass a suite of small synthetic networks before it is calibrated to any city. Each case has a known or independently estimated answer and a stated tolerance. A possible core set:

    #NetworkExpected result (reference)
    1Single link, varying demandArrival distribution and free-flow travel time; stable maximum throughput equal to specified capacity
    2Lane drop with demand > capacityDischarge rate, queue-tail speed and clearance time as in the LWR worked example (Module 7)
    3Same, demand falls after 45 minReceding shock speed; total delay vs N-curve area
    4Ring road, dense trafficPresence/absence of stop-and-go waves consistent with the model's stability conditions
    5On-ramp merge, varied ramp shareMerge capacity and which approach queues (JSTE test 4)
    6Weaving sectionCapacity within the range of HCM/HBS weaving methods
    7Fixed-time signal, single approachSaturation flow; delay vs Webster/HCM at \(x\) = 0.5, 0.8, 0.95
    8Opposed turn at signalTurning capacity vs gap-acceptance theory (JSTE test 5)
    9Priority junctionMinor-stream capacity vs Harders/Siegloch with the model's own \(t_c\), \(t_f\)
    10Single-lane roundabout entry, conflicting flow 0–1,400 pcu/hEntry capacity curve vs SETRA/GIRABASE, Kimber and HBS (Module 9); report the deviation, not pass/fail
    11Two-lane roundabout, asymmetric turningLane use consistent with destination-based lane choice (Spanish DGT guidance)
    12Short link between two signalsSpillback blocks the upstream junction (physical queue), as Singapore's LTA warns
    13Two parallel routesEquilibrium or logit split as specified; stable convergence (JSTE test 6)
    14Grid with increasing demandA network MFD with a maximum and a declining branch; gridlock behavior
    15Mixed cars and bicycles on a shared laneOvertaking and queueing consistent with observed speeds and headways; no cyclists "teleported"
    16Repeated seeds, every caseSeed-to-seed variance small relative to day-to-day variance (FHWA)

    The suite should run automatically after every model or software update, like the unit tests of any engineering code. It separates two questions that a city-wide fit statistic confounds: does the simulator reproduce the mechanics of traffic? and does its demand reproduce the traffic of this city? Until they are separated, improving GEH can hide defects in either.

    Exercise 10.1 · Count fit is not enough

    You are given hourly counts at 120 sites and a model with 85% of sites at GEH < 5.

    1. Randomly hold out 20 sites, recalibrate the OD matrix on the remaining 100, and recompute GEH on both sets. How large is the gap?
    2. Compare trip-length distributions before and after matrix estimation. Which changed most?
    3. Close one major link in the model. Compute the resulting change in total vehicle-km and vehicle-hours. Is the implied elasticity plausible compared with evidence on traffic evaporation after road closures?
    4. Write the paragraph of the model report that tells a decision-maker what the model can and cannot be used for.
    Exercise 10.2 · Verify a simulator

    Implement cases 2, 7 and 10 of the verification suite in SUMO (or another simulator). For case 10, plot simulated entry capacity against conflicting flow alongside the SETRA, Swiss and Siegloch curves from Module 9. Change one behavioral parameter (for example the minimum gap or impatience) and show how much the curve moves. Which parameter would you calibrate against local roundabout data, and what data would you need?
