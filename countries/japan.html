    Role model country

    Japan

    Reading time ≈ 40 minutes · Related modules: 2, 4, 5, 6, 11, 14 · Key terms: A D E constraints

    Japan is the clearest large-scale example of a country that delivers Term A, access to opportunities, through urban structure rather than vehicle speed. In Tokyo and Osaka, private railway companies built lines and towns together and recovered the cost from fares and land. Yet the national road appraisal is one of the narrowest in the OECD: it counts only three benefits. And outside the big metropolitan areas, most trips are made by car. This page is about all three Japans.

    What to take from this page
    • Why the Tokyo region reaches a rail share of about a third of all trips, and what role private railways and land readjustment played.
    • What MLIT's road cost–benefit manual counts (time, operating cost, crashes) and what it deliberately leaves out.
    • How the Location Normalization Plan (立地適正化計画, 2014) writes "compact plus network" into planning law, and why it nudges rather than regulates.
    • Strengths (rail-and-property value capture, person-trip surveys, the garage certificate, a new 30 km/h default) and documented weaknesses (crowding, car-dependent regions, pedestrian deaths, shrinking demand).

    2. Japan's reading of \(W\)

    Module 1 summarized Japan as "Term A as accessibility through urban structure: dense development around rail stations, built and financed by private railways. Road appraisal counts only three benefits." This section unpacks both halves, because they describe different decision processes.

    Metropolitan rail: Term A through \(\ln O_j\), not \(\beta_t t\) A D

    Recall the utility of alternative \(j\): \(V_{nj} = \beta_t t_{nj} + \beta_c c_{nj} + \boldsymbol{\beta}_x^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{nj} + \ln O_j\). A road-centered plan raises the logsum mainly by reducing \(t\). The Japanese metropolitan model raises it mainly by putting more opportunities \(O_j\) within a short walk of a station, and by putting homes within walking distance of the same line. Speeds are moderate. Peak trains are crowded, which is a large negative term in \(\mathbf{x}\). But the number of destinations reachable in 45 minutes without a car is very high.

    Two features make this a welfare reading rather than just a density statistic:

    • Land is used at its opportunity cost. Little urban land goes to parking because of the garage certificate rule (section 4). The station area is the most valuable land in the region, and it is used for shops and offices, not park-and-ride. In terms of Term D, \(r\) is high and \(L\) is kept small.
    • The operator internalizes the accessibility benefit. A private railway that owns land along its line captures part of the access gain as higher land values and rents. Its private objective (fare revenue plus property profit) then lines up with part of Term A, something a pure transit operator cannot do. Section 5 gives the Tokyu example.

    Road appraisal: Term A as three monetized benefits, fixed demand A E

    National road projects are appraised with MLIT's 費用便益分析マニュアル (cost–benefit analysis manual), first issued as a draft in 1998. The edition in use was revised in February 2025 and corrected in August 2025, which updated the unit values to 2024 prices (MLIT Kantō Regional Bureau explainer, 2025). The manual counts exactly three benefits:

    $$B = \underbrace{\big(T^{\text{without}} - T^{\text{with}}\big)}_{\text{走行時間短縮便益: travel time}} + \underbrace{\big(C^{\text{without}} - C^{\text{with}}\big)}_{\text{走行経費減少便益: operating cost}} + \underbrace{\big(A^{\text{without}} - A^{\text{with}}\big)}_{\text{交通事故減少便益: crash losses}}$$

    Each term is the money value of total time, operating cost and crash losses on the network, computed from an assignment of forecast traffic. Benefits are discounted at 4 % over 50 years after opening. Since fiscal 2023, results at 2 % and 1 % are also reported as reference values (same source). The manual's own text explains why only three: they are the effects that can, with current knowledge, be measured "with sufficient accuracy" and expressed in money (translated in section 7).

    In the notation of the course this is the classic special case from Module 1, with two improvements. It counts money costs as well as time, and it prices one externality, crashes. It sets every other \(p_k=0\): no CO₂, air pollution, noise or severance. It sets \(r=0\) for land already in public hands. It has no health term B. Wider benefits (reliability, disaster resilience, regional development) are reported separately as non-monetized "objective evaluation indicators" alongside the B/C ratio. That makes the B/C ratio a conservative test for most road projects, as MLIT's own evaluation-method committee papers noted: leaving benefits out makes a ratio above 1 harder to reach. But it is not conservative for a road that adds traffic through a town, because the omitted noise, air pollution and severance would count against it.

    Railways are appraised differently

    MLIT's Railway Bureau uses a separate manual, the 鉄道プロジェクトの評価手法マニュアル (2012 edition, partly amended March 2025). It distinguishes user benefits, supplier (operator) benefits and environmental benefits, so rail appraisal reaches further into Term C than road appraisal does. A comparison of the two manuals is a good exercise in how the choice of benefit list tilts modal decisions (Module 14).

    Constraints constraints

    • Population decline as a binding budget. The Japanese government frames the compact-city policy as a response to falling population and rising per-capita infrastructure cost, not mainly as a climate policy. In terms of \(W\), it bears on Term E: fewer taxpayers must maintain the same network, so \(K\) per resident rises unless the urban footprint shrinks.
    • Safety. The Traffic Safety Basic Plans set national casualty targets, but crashes are priced in appraisal rather than treated as a hard constraint in the Swedish sense. The new 30 km/h default speed on residential streets (section 4) is the closest Japan has come to building the constraint into the rules.
    • Equity floor. The 2007 Act on the Revitalisation of Regional Public Transport, amended several times since, provides for municipal local public transport plans (地域公共交通計画). Since 2020, municipalities are expected to make them, although this is a duty to make efforts, not a strict obligation. By the end of fiscal 2024, 1,184 such plans had been published (MLIT White Paper 2025). They are about keeping a minimum service for people without a car, \(A_g \ge A_{\min}\), in shrinking towns.

    3. Institutions and standards

    Who plans what

    • MLIT owns national roads, sets road design standards, funds urban planning, licenses railways and approves their fares. Within MLIT, the Road Bureau, City Bureau and Railway Bureau each have their own appraisal manuals and guidance.
    • The police, not road authorities, set speed limits, install and time traffic signals and decide most traffic regulations. Prefectural Public Safety Commissions do this under the Road Traffic Act. Changing a junction therefore needs two agencies: the road authority for geometry and the police for control. This split is a frequent source of friction in street redesign.
    • Railways are mostly run by companies: the six passenger JR companies (from the 1987 privatization of Japanese National Railways), large private railways (大手私鉄) such as Tokyu, Odakyu, Hankyu and Kintetsu, subways run by city governments or companies, and many small regional lines. Most metropolitan operating costs are covered by fares and related businesses, not by operating subsidies.
    • Municipalities write city master plans, Location Normalization Plans and local public transport plans. Since 2014 these plans are meant to be written together.

    4. Signature ideas and documented weaknesses

    Ideas the world can learn from

    1. Railway plus property. Private railways built lines into farmland, developed housing and shopping at stations, and ran department stores at the terminals. The value created by access was captured by the investor. This is the strongest real-world example of financing Term A out of the land value it creates. The World Bank study Transforming Cities with Transit (Suzuki, Cervero and Iuchi, 2013) uses Tokyo as a central case.
    2. Land readjustment (土地区画整理). Landowners pool their plots. Each gets back a smaller but serviced plot, and the land contributed pays for roads, parks and part of the infrastructure. This avoids large compulsory purchases and is how much of suburban Tokyo was built.
    3. The garage certificate. Since 1962 a car can be registered in most of Japan only with proof of an off-street parking space near the owner's home, and long-term on-street parking, including more than eight hours at night, is prohibited. The land cost of storing a car (Term D) is paid by the car owner, not by the street. Few countries have dared to copy this.
    4. Person-based travel surveys at regional scale, repeated for almost sixty years, which give unusually long comparable series on mode choice (Module 4).
    5. Compact plus network in planning law. Since 2014 a municipality can define where it will encourage housing and services, tied to public transport. By 31 July 2026, 972 municipalities had made or were actively working on such a plan (MLIT, 2026). At the end of fiscal 2024, 636 had published one.
    6. A default of 30 km/h. The Road Traffic Act Enforcement Order now sets the statutory maximum speed on ordinary roads with no center line, no lane markings and no physical separation of directions at 30 km/h, instead of 60 km/h (art. 11, current text). This complements area-wide "Zone 30" schemes. Japan has had them since 2011, and since 2021 "Zone 30 Plus" combines the limit with physical measures such as humps and narrowings.
    7. Testing traffic simulators against shared benchmarks. JSTE has run a scheme in which simulation packages are checked against standard test cases and published validation datasets, an idea developed further in Module 10.

    Documented weaknesses and criticisms

    1. Crowding. Average peak-hour load on 31 major Tokyo-area rail sections was 221 % of nominal capacity in fiscal 1975 and still 163 % in fiscal 2019. It fell to 107 % in the pandemic year and was back up to 143 % in fiscal 2025 (MLIT Railway Bureau). Crowding is a real cost in Term A (the \(\mathbf{x}\) attributes). A system can have high accessibility and still impose a daily disutility that a fare-box-funded operator has little incentive to remove.
    2. Narrow road appraisal. With only three benefits and a fixed land-use forecast, the road B/C ratio ignores environmental costs and long-run induced traffic. In Module 1 terms, it inherits most of the bias of \(\min \sum q t\). Course judgement: the bias is less serious in Japan than in a country that builds most of its urban growth around roads, but it matters for bypasses and widenings in regional cities.
    3. Two Japans. Outside the big three regions, and in Chūkyō itself, car shares look North American. Many regional cities spread out along roads with large out-of-town retail. The rail model is a metropolitan model, not a national one.
    4. Vulnerable road users. Japan's overall fatality rate is low, but a large share of those killed are pedestrians, many of them elderly. Many residential streets have no sidewalks. The 30 km/h default and Zone 30 Plus respond to this.
    5. Location Normalization Plans are weak instruments. They work through notifications, subsidies and incentives, not prohibitions. Commentators have criticised plans whose residential inducement zones cover most of the existing built-up area, which leaves little real concentration. Since a 2020 amendment the plans must also include a disaster-prevention guideline, because some inducement zones lay in flood-risk areas.
    6. Shrinking demand. Rail and property value capture depends on growth. In older suburbs and regional cities, aging, vacant homes and falling ridership weaken the business model. Some regional lines have closed or been converted to bus services.
    7. Prices that are not congestion prices. Expressways are tolled, but the tolls were designed to repay construction debt, not to internalize congestion. Urban street congestion is unpriced. Parking minimums for large buildings still exist, although MLIT now advises municipalities to consolidate and relocate required parking.

    5. Case studies

    Tokyu Den-en-toshi Line and Tama Den-en-toshi A D E

    In 1953 Keita Gotō, head of the Tokyu group, presented a plan to develop the hills south-west of Tokyo around a new railway. Tokyu acted as agent for landowner associations in land readjustment (the 一括代行方式). It paid for site works in advance and was repaid with reserved plots that it sold. Between 1959 and 2000, 55 readjustment districts covering 3,204 ha were developed. The Den-en-toshi Line opened in stages from 1966 and today links the area to central Tokyo with through-running into the Hanzōmon subway line. The Tama Den-en-toshi area covers about 5,000 ha across four cities, with about 620,000 residents in 2017 (municipal and company figures as reported in the Japanese Wikipedia article, which cites them).

    Reading with \(W\). The railway raised \(O_j\)-weighted access for hundreds of thousands of households (A). The land was readjusted rather than bought, which kept the public cost E low. Profits from the land financed the line. The line has been among Tokyo's most crowded, and since the 2010s the earliest neighborhoods have aged, with vacant houses appearing far from stations. Tokyu and Yokohama City signed an agreement in 2012 on "next-generation suburban town-making" to respond.

    Worked example: why the railway builds the town

    Hypothetical numbers, for illustration only. A new line costs ¥100 bn. Discounted fare surplus over the appraisal period is ¥60 bn, so a pure rail operator would not build it. The line raises the value of 1,000 ha of land within walking distance of stations by ¥8,000 per m², a total of ¥80 bn. If the railway owns or readjusts a quarter of that land, it captures ¥20 bn, which is still not enough. If, in addition, it builds stations, shops and housing that generate ¥30 bn of discounted property profit, the project pays: \(60+20+30 = 110 > 100\).

    The welfare gain is larger than the operator's profit, because most of the land-value rise goes to other owners. The land-value rise is also largely a capitalisation of the same accessibility gain measured by the logsum. Adding it to the logsum in Term A would double-count. Value capture is a financing tool for Term E, not an extra benefit.

    Toyama: compact city along light rail A E constraints

    Toyama, a prefectural capital of about 400,000 people on the Sea of Japan coast, is the best-known Japanese compact-city case. Its official policy is "compact town-making with concentrated hubs, built on public transport" (公共交通を軸とした拠点集中型のコンパクトなまちづくり), described on the city's planning page. The policy is often summarized as "skewers and dumplings": transit lines are the skewers and walkable centers the dumplings. Key steps:

    • 2006: the declining JR Toyamakō Line was converted into a light rail line, Portram, run by the public–private company Toyama Light Rail with higher frequencies and low-floor trams.
    • 2009: a city-center tram loop was completed.
    • 2020: tracks were connected through the rebuilt Toyama Station, joining the northern and southern tram networks.
    • Alongside: subsidies for housing in the city center and along the corridors, and later a Location Normalization Plan.

    Reading with \(W\). The policy was justified explicitly through Term E (the cost of serving a dispersed, aging population) and the equity floor (residents who cannot drive), more than through Term C. Its limits are also instructive. The region remains car-oriented, and the city itself says that "the real era of population decline is only beginning", with public transport facing driver shortages and rising costs. Compact-city policy here slows dispersal. It does not reverse it.

    A newer case worth following is Utsunomiya, which opened Japan's first entirely new light rail line in decades in August 2023, planned together with the city's "network compact city" strategy.

    7. Translated key passages

    MLIT road cost–benefit manual: why only three benefits

    それらの効果のうち、現時点における知見により、十分な精度で計測が可能でかつ金銭表現が可能である、「走行時間短縮」、「走行経費減少」、「交通事故減少」の項目について、道路投資の評価手法として定着している消費者余剰を計測することにより便益を算出する。

    Of these effects, the benefits are calculated for the items that, given current knowledge, can be measured with sufficient accuracy and expressed in money, namely "travel time savings", "reduction in vehicle operating costs" and "reduction in traffic accidents". This is done by measuring consumer surplus, the established method for appraising road investment.

    Source: MLIT Road Bureau, 費用便益分析マニュアル（案）, committee draft, 2003, section 1(1). The preceding sentence lists the effects left out, including driving comfort, roadside environment, disaster-time alternative routes, wider exchange and new industrial location.     二十二 計画交通量 道路の設計の基礎とするために、当該道路の存する地域の発展の動向、将来の自動車交通の状況等を勘案して…定める自動車の日交通量をいう。 二十三 設計速度 道路の設計の基礎とする自動車の速度をいう。

    Discussion questions
    1. Tokyo and Nagoya differ by more than a factor of two in car share. List three causes (history, railway ownership, industry structure, land readjustment, road investment) and say which term of \(W\) each acts on. Which of them could a city outside Japan actually copy?
    2. The road manual counts three benefits because they can be measured "with sufficient accuracy". Is leaving out an effect better than including it with a wide error band? Answer separately for a rural bypass and for an urban widening.
    3. In the worked example in section 5, the railway captures part of the land-value rise. Show why adding the land-value rise to the logsum in Term A double-counts. When might land values capture more than the access benefit, for example through agglomeration?
    4. Peak crowding of 140–160 % is a cost to passengers but not, at the margin, to the operator. Write down the external crowding cost as a term \(p_k E_k\). What instrument would internalize it: fares by time of day, capacity investment or something else?
    5. The garage certificate makes every car owner pay for Term D. Model it as a fixed cost in the car-ownership choice and discuss its effect on the equity floor in a rural town without buses.
    6. A Location Normalization Plan "may" be prepared and "induces" rather than restricts. Is a nudge-based instrument the right choice when the problem is long-run decline? What would a binding version look like, and who would lose?
