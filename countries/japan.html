    Role model country

    Japan

    Reading time ≈ 50 minutes · Related modules: 2, 4, 5, 6, 11, 14 · Key terms: A D E constraints

    Japan is the clearest large-scale example of a country that delivers Term A, access to opportunities, through urban structure rather than vehicle speed. In Tokyo and Osaka, private railway companies built lines and towns together and recovered the cost from fares and land. Yet the national road appraisal is one of the narrowest among rich countries: it counts only three benefits. And outside the big metropolitan areas, most trips are made by car. The same national laws, standards and appraisal manuals apply everywhere. This page asks why they produced such different places, and finds most of the answer in who owns the land, who pays for roads, and history.

    In brief
    The question
    Why do Tokyo and Nagoya, two wealthy, industrial Japanese regions under the same national rules, differ by more than a factor of two in car use?
    Stated objectives
    "Compact plus network": since 2014, planning law lets towns steer homes and services toward public transport. National safety plans set casualty targets. Laws on regional public transport aim to keep a minimum service in shrinking towns.
    How projects are appraised
    Roads: only three monetized benefits (travel time, operating cost, crashes), at 4% over 50 years. No carbon, noise or air pollution. Railways: a separate manual that also counts operator and environmental benefits.
    Design standards
    Roads are sized by daily motor-vehicle volume. The police, not the road authority, set speed limits and signals. A car can be registered only with proof of an off-street parking space.
    What gets built
    Rail carries a third of weekday trips in the Tokyo region; cars carry nearly two-thirds in the Nagoya region. Households own 0.4 cars on average in Tokyo and 1.7 in Fukui. In 2025, 2,547 people died on the roads, more than half of them aged 65 or over.
    Lessons for a decision maker
    Whoever captures the rise in land value has a reason to build access: railways that owned land built towns around their stations. Ask what a road appraisal leaves out, and who pays for parking: the driver, or the street.
    Learning outcomes
    • Separate four layers of Japanese practice (stated objectives, appraisal, design rules, outcomes) and show where metropolitan and regional Japan diverge.
    • Explain why the Tokyo region reaches a rail share of about a third of all trips, and what role private railways and land readjustment played.
    • Explain what MLIT's road cost–benefit manual counts (time, operating cost, crashes) and what it deliberately leaves out.
    • Explain how the Location Normalization Plan (立地適正化計画, 2014) writes "compact plus network" into planning law, and why it nudges rather than regulates.
    • Use Japan to answer the course's central question for one country: how much of the Tokyo–Nagoya difference is objectives, constraints, models or institutions?

    2. Stated objectives: what the laws and plans say

    Module 1 summarized Japan as "Term A as accessibility through urban structure: dense development around rail stations, built and financed by private railways. Road appraisal counts only three benefits." That describes what Japan does in its big cities. What the national government says it wants is different again.

    • Compact plus network. Since a 2014 amendment of the Urban Renaissance Special Measures Act, a municipality may draw up a Location Normalization Plan. It defines zones where the town will encourage housing and services, tied to public transport (translation in section 9). The government frames this as a response to falling population and rising per-capita infrastructure cost, not mainly as climate policy. In terms of \(W\), it bears on Term E: fewer taxpayers must maintain the same network, so cost per resident rises unless the town's footprint shrinks.
    • A minimum of public transport. The 2007 Act on the Revitalization of Regional Public Transport, amended several times since, provides for municipal local public transport plans (地域公共交通計画). Since 2020, municipalities are expected to make them, although this is a duty to make efforts, not a strict obligation. By the end of fiscal 2024, 1,184 such plans had been published (MLIT White Paper 2025). They aim to keep a minimum service for people without a car, \(A_g \ge A_{\min}\), in shrinking towns.
    • Safety. Five-year Traffic Safety Basic Plans set national casualty targets; the 12th plan was drawn up in 2026 (Cabinet Office). Crashes are priced in road appraisal rather than treated as a hard limit in the Swedish sense.
    • Resilience and regional development. Road policy is also justified by disaster resilience (the 2013 Basic Act for National Resilience) and by links between regions. These appear in appraisal only as non-monetized indicators (section 3).

    No national plan sets a modal-split or vehicle-kilometer target for cities. The rail-based form of Tokyo was not a stated national objective. It was the result of institutions and history (section 6).

    3. How projects are appraised

    Roads: three monetized benefits, fixed demand

    National road projects are appraised with MLIT's 費用便益分析マニュアル (cost–benefit analysis manual), first issued as a draft in 1998. The edition in use was revised in February 2025 and corrected in August 2025, which updated the unit values to 2024 prices (MLIT Kantō Regional Bureau explainer, 2025). The manual counts exactly three benefits: savings in travel time, savings in vehicle operating costs, and fewer crash losses. Each is computed by comparing the network with and without the project, using an assignment of forecast traffic. Benefits are discounted at 4 % a year over 50 years after opening. Since fiscal 2023, results at 2 % and 1 % are also reported as reference values. The manual's own text explains why only three: they are the effects that can, with current knowledge, be measured "with sufficient accuracy" and expressed in money (translated in section 9).

    What it assumes, and what it serves. In the notation of the course this is the classic special case from Module 1, with two improvements. It counts money costs as well as time, and it prices one harm to others, crashes. It sets every other price \(p_k=0\): no CO₂, air pollution, noise or severance. It sets \(r=0\) for land already in public hands. It has no health term B. Wider effects (reliability, disaster resilience, regional development) are reported separately as non-monetized "objective evaluation indicators" alongside the benefit–cost ratio.

    Where it fails. MLIT's own evaluation-method committee papers noted that leaving benefits out makes a ratio above 1 harder to reach, so the ratio is a conservative test for most road projects. But it is not conservative for a road that adds traffic through a town, because the omitted noise, air pollution and severance would count against it. Germany's federal plan and France's appraisal price these harms; Japan's road manual does not.

    Railways are appraised differently

    MLIT's Railway Bureau uses a separate manual, the 鉄道プロジェクトの評価手法マニュアル (2012 edition, partly amended March 2025). It distinguishes user benefits, supplier (operator) benefits and environmental benefits, so rail appraisal reaches further into Term C than road appraisal does. A comparison of the two manuals is a good exercise in how the choice of benefit list tilts modal decisions (Module 14).

    Funding rules: fuel taxes for roads, fares for railways

    For 55 years, Japan's road building had its own money. A 1953 law on the financing of road works, applied from 1954, earmarked the gasoline tax for roads, and further vehicle taxes were added in later decades. This "road-specific revenue" (道路特定財源) flowed to road building whatever the benefit–cost ratios said. It was abolished from fiscal 2009, when the taxes became general revenue (Kotobank; MLIT). Metropolitan railways, by contrast, have mostly paid their way from fares and related businesses, not from operating subsidies. Expressways are tolled, but the tolls were designed to repay construction debt, not to charge for congestion.

    Under the hoodThe three-benefit formula and how it is discounted

    The manual computes the benefit in each year as the sum of three differences between the network without the project and with it:

    $$B = \underbrace{\big(T^{\text{without}} - T^{\text{with}}\big)}_{\text{走行時間短縮便益: travel time}} + \underbrace{\big(C^{\text{without}} - C^{\text{with}}\big)}_{\text{走行経費減少便益: operating cost}} + \underbrace{\big(A^{\text{without}} - A^{\text{with}}\big)}_{\text{交通事故減少便益: crash losses}}$$

    Here \(T\) is the money value of total travel time on the network, computed from vehicle-hours by vehicle type and the manual's time values per vehicle. \(C\) is total vehicle operating cost and \(A\) the money value of crash losses. All three come from the same traffic assignment, with the trip matrix forecast independently of the project. The present value is \(\sum_t B_t/(1.04)^t\) over 50 years after opening, compared with the discounted construction and maintenance cost. Reference results at 2 % and 1 % have been reported since fiscal 2023.

    Two consequences follow. First, because the trip matrix does not respond to the road, induced traffic shows up only as rerouting. A bypass that later attracts new development is credited only with the time savings of today's traffic. Second, because \(T\) is counted per vehicle with fixed occupancy, a road that serves buses gains no more than one that serves cars. See Module 2 for time values and Module 14 for the comparison with other manuals.

    4. Design standards and rules

    Who controls the street

    • MLIT owns national roads, sets road design standards, funds urban planning, licenses railways and approves their fares. Within MLIT, the Road Bureau, City Bureau and Railway Bureau each have their own appraisal manuals and guidance.
    • The police, not road authorities, set speed limits, install and time traffic signals and decide most traffic regulations. Prefectural Public Safety Commissions do this under the Road Traffic Act. Changing a junction therefore needs two agencies: the road authority for geometry and the police for control. This split is a frequent source of friction in street redesign.
    • Municipalities write city master plans, Location Normalization Plans and local public transport plans. Since 2014 these plans are meant to be written together.

    Three rules that shape streets

    • The garage certificate. Since 1962 a car can be registered in most of Japan only with proof of an off-street parking space near the owner's home. Long-term on-street parking, including more than eight hours at night, is prohibited. The land cost of storing a car (Term D) is paid by the car owner, not by the street. Few countries have dared to copy this.
    • A default of 30 km/h. The Road Traffic Act Enforcement Order now sets the statutory maximum speed on ordinary roads with no center line, no lane markings and no physical separation of directions at 30 km/h, instead of 60 km/h (art. 11, current text). This complements area-wide "Zone 30" schemes. Japan has had them since 2011, and since 2021 "Zone 30 Plus" combines the limit with physical measures such as humps and narrowings.
    • Parking minimums for large buildings still exist, although MLIT now advises municipalities to consolidate and relocate required parking.

    5. What gets built: observed outcomes

    • Two Japans. In the Tokyo region, rail carries a third of weekday trips and cars about a quarter. In the Chūkyō (Nagoya) region, cars carry nearly two-thirds (section 1). Households own 0.405 cars on average in Tokyo and 1.670 in Fukui (AIRIA, 2025). Many regional cities have spread out along roads with large out-of-town retail. The rail model is a metropolitan model, not a national one.
    • Crowding. Average peak-hour load on 31 major Tokyo-area rail sections was 221 % of nominal capacity in fiscal 1975 and still 163 % in fiscal 2019. It fell to 107 % in the pandemic year and was back up to 143 % in fiscal 2025 (MLIT Railway Bureau). Crowding is a real cost in Term A (the comfort attributes \(\mathbf{x}\)). A system can have high accessibility and still impose a daily discomfort that a fare-funded operator has little incentive to remove.
    • Safety. Japan's overall fatality rate is low, 2.6 per 100,000. But in 2025 more than half of those killed were 65 or older, and pedestrians were the largest group of the dead (White Paper on Traffic Safety 2026). Many residential streets have no sidewalks. The 30 km/h default and Zone 30 Plus respond to this.
    • Compact-city plans, weak effect. By 31 July 2026, 972 municipalities had made or were actively working on a Location Normalization Plan (MLIT, 2026). At the end of fiscal 2024, 636 had published one. The plans work through notifications, subsidies and incentives, not prohibitions. Commentators have criticized plans whose residential inducement zones cover most of the existing built-up area, which leaves little real concentration. Since a 2020 amendment the plans must also include a disaster-prevention guideline, because some inducement zones lay in flood-risk areas.
    • Shrinking demand. Rail and property value capture depends on growth. In older suburbs and regional cities, aging, vacant homes and falling ridership weaken the business model. Some regional lines have closed or been converted to bus services.

    6. Where the layers diverge, and why

    LayerMetropolitan JapanRegional Japan
    Stated objectivesCompact plus network; minimum public transport; national safety targets; resilience
    AppraisalRail manual with operator and environmental benefits; private railways' own business casesThree-benefit road appraisal for bypasses and widenings
    Design rulesRoads sized by daily motor-vehicle volume; police control signals and speeds; garage certificate; 30 km/h default on narrow streets
    OutcomesRail a third of trips; high crowding; few cars per householdCars two-thirds of trips or more; dispersed retail; shrinking bus and rail service

    The same national objectives, appraisal manuals and design rules apply in both columns. So the difference must come from elsewhere. Four reasons stand out.

    • Who captures the land value. In 1910 the Minoo-Arima Electric Railway, led by Ichizō Kobayashi, opened a line north of Osaka. It bought land along the line cheaply and sold new homes to the growing middle class on installment terms (Social Science Japan Journal study, 2007; National Diet Library). The company became Hankyu. Its model of railway, housing, terminal department store and leisure was widely copied by other private railways, Tokyu among them. A railway that owns land along its line earns from the access it creates, so it has a reason to build towns around stations. Section 7 shows the Tokyu version.
    • Timing. The big private networks of Tokyo and Osaka were built before mass car ownership. Homes, jobs and shops grew around stations first. Many regional cities grew after the 1970s, when cars were common, and grew along roads.
    • Funding rules. From 1954 to 2009, fuel and vehicle taxes flowed automatically into road building. Railways had to pay from fares and land. A region without a profitable private railway got roads paid for by earmarked taxes, appraised by a method that counts mainly time savings.
    • Divided control and weak land-use rules. The police control signals and speeds; the road authority controls geometry; the municipality controls plans. Land-use control has been described as weak by planning historians (Sorensen, 2002), and the new compact-city plans induce rather than restrict.

    Stated objectives explain little of the Tokyo–Nagoya difference. Constraints explain some: Tokyo's land is far scarcer and dearer. Models explain little, since both regions use the same manuals. Institutions and history explain most.

    The same problem, here

    A suburb 15 km from the city center is growing. Its one radial road into the city is jammed every morning. What would competent Japanese planners typically propose, and why? This is a stylized judgment, not a documented case.

    • In the Tokyo region: the railway serving the suburb would add trains, lengthen platforms or run through into the subway network, and develop the station area. The road would probably not be widened, because land is scarce and the rail business case is stronger.
    • In a regional city: a bypass or widening, appraised with the three-benefit ratio, is the more likely answer. The town may also designate inducement zones along a bus or tram line.
    • Objectives: the same national goals in both places.
    • Constraints: land prices and space in Tokyo; budgets and a shrinking population in regional cities.
    • Models: the same road appraisal, which favors roads where it is applied.
    • Institutions: a private railway that profits from station land in one place; no such actor in the other.

    Compare the US, Germany and Singapore.

    The Japanese layers, read as \(W\)

    Metropolitan rail: Term A raised mainly through more opportunities \(O_j\) near stations, not through speed, with crowding as a large negative attribute. Road appraisal: time, operating cost and crashes only, with \(p_k=0\) for other harms, \(r=0\) for public land and fixed demand. Garage certificate: Term D charged to car owners. Compact-city and public transport plans: a soft equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\) motivated by Term E. This is our interpretation; agencies do not state it this way.

    Under the hoodWhy metropolitan rail raises Term A through \(\ln O_j\), not through speed

    Recall the utility of alternative \(j\) from Module 1: \(V_{nj} = \beta_t t_{nj} + \beta_c c_{nj} + \boldsymbol{\beta}_x^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{nj} + \ln O_j\). Here \(t\) is travel time, \(c\) money cost, \(\mathbf{x}\) other attributes such as comfort, and \(O_j\) the number of opportunities at destination \(j\). A road-centered plan raises the logsum mainly by reducing \(t\). The Japanese metropolitan model raises it mainly by putting more opportunities \(O_j\) within a short walk of a station, and by putting homes within walking distance of the same line. Speeds are moderate. Peak trains are crowded, which is a large negative term in \(\mathbf{x}\). But the number of destinations reachable in 45 minutes without a car is very high.

    Two features make this a welfare reading rather than just a density statistic. First, land is used at its opportunity cost: little urban land goes to parking because of the garage certificate, and the station area, the most valuable land in the region, is used for shops and offices rather than park-and-ride. In terms of Term D, \(r\) is high and \(L\) is kept small. Second, the operator internalizes part of the access benefit: a railway that owns land along its line captures part of the gain as higher land values and rents, so its private objective (fare revenue plus property profit) lines up with part of Term A.

    What other countries can borrow

    1. Railway plus property. The strongest real-world example of financing Term A out of the land value it creates. The World Bank study Transforming Cities with Transit (Suzuki, Cervero and Iuchi, 2013) uses Tokyo as a central case.
    2. Land readjustment (土地区画整理). Landowners pool their plots. Each gets back a smaller but serviced plot, and the land contributed pays for roads, parks and part of the infrastructure. This avoids large compulsory purchases and is how much of suburban Tokyo was built.
    3. The garage certificate: the owner, not the street, pays for car storage.
    4. Person-based travel surveys at regional scale, repeated for almost sixty years, which give unusually long comparable series on mode choice (Module 4).
    5. A 30 km/h default on narrow streets, written into the national rules.
    6. Testing traffic simulators against shared benchmarks. JSTE has run a scheme in which simulation packages are checked against standard test cases and published validation datasets, an idea developed further in Module 10.

    7. Case studies

    Tokyu Den-en-toshi Line and Tama Den-en-toshi A D E

    In 1953 Keita Gotō, head of the Tokyu group, presented a plan to develop the hills south-west of Tokyo around a new railway. Tokyu acted as agent for landowner associations in land readjustment (the 一括代行方式). It paid for site works in advance and was repaid with reserved plots that it sold. Between 1959 and 2000, 55 readjustment districts covering 3,204 ha were developed. The Den-en-toshi Line opened in stages from 1966 and today links the area to central Tokyo with through-running into the Hanzōmon subway line. The Tama Den-en-toshi area covers about 5,000 ha across four cities, with about 620,000 residents in 2017 (municipal and company figures as reported in the Japanese Wikipedia article, which cites them).

    Reading with \(W\). The railway raised \(O_j\)-weighted access for hundreds of thousands of households (A). The land was readjusted rather than bought, which kept the public cost E low. Profits from the land financed the line. The line has been among Tokyo's most crowded, and since the 2010s the earliest neighborhoods have aged, with vacant houses appearing far from stations. Tokyu and Yokohama City signed an agreement in 2012 on "next-generation suburban town-making" to respond.

    Worked example: why the railway builds the town

    Hypothetical numbers, for illustration only. A new line costs ¥100 bn. Discounted fare surplus over the appraisal period is ¥60 bn, so a pure rail operator would not build it. The line raises the value of 1,000 ha of land within walking distance of stations by ¥8,000 per m², a total of ¥80 bn. If the railway owns or readjusts a quarter of that land, it captures ¥20 bn, which is still not enough. If, in addition, it builds stations, shops and housing that generate ¥30 bn of discounted property profit, the project pays: \(60+20+30 = 110 > 100\).

    The welfare gain is larger than the operator's profit, because most of the land-value rise goes to other owners. The land-value rise is also largely a capitalization of the same accessibility gain measured by the logsum. Adding it to the logsum in Term A would double-count. Value capture is a financing tool for Term E, not an extra benefit.

    Toyama: compact city along light rail A E constraints

    Toyama, a prefectural capital of about 400,000 people on the Sea of Japan coast, is the best-known Japanese compact-city case. Its official policy is "compact town-making with concentrated hubs, built on public transport" (公共交通を軸とした拠点集中型のコンパクトなまちづくり), described on the city's planning page. The policy is often summarized as "skewers and dumplings": transit lines are the skewers and walkable centers the dumplings. Key steps:

    • 2006: the declining JR Toyamakō Line was converted into a light rail line, Portram, run by the public–private company Toyama Light Rail with higher frequencies and low-floor trams.
    • 2009: a city-center tram loop was completed.
    • 2020: tracks were connected through the rebuilt Toyama Station, joining the northern and southern tram networks.
    • Alongside: subsidies for housing in the city center and along the corridors, and later a Location Normalization Plan.

    Reading with \(W\). The policy was justified explicitly through Term E (the cost of serving a dispersed, aging population) and the equity floor (residents who cannot drive), more than through Term C. Its limits are also instructive. The region remains car-oriented, and the city itself says that "the real era of population decline is only beginning", with public transport facing driver shortages and rising costs. Compact-city policy here slows dispersal. It does not reverse it. Toyama shows a regional city choosing, against its institutional defaults, to act like a railway town; the stated objective mattered here because a mayor and council made it a priority.

    A newer case worth following is Utsunomiya, which opened Japan's first entirely new light rail line in decades in August 2023, planned together with the city's "network compact city" strategy.

    A municipality, alone or jointly, may prepare, for an area within a city planning area as defined in article 4(2) of the City Planning Act and on the basis of the Basic Policy for Urban Renaissance, a plan for the optimization of the location of housing and of urban-function-enhancing facilities ("Location Normalization Plan"). Urban-function-enhancing facilities means medical facilities, welfare facilities, commercial facilities and other facilities necessary for the common welfare or convenience of the city's residents that contribute significantly to the enhancement of urban functions.

    Source: 都市再生特別措置法 (Act No. 22 of 2002), art. 81(1), current text on e-Gov. Paragraph 2 requires the plan to define a residential inducement zone (居住誘導区域), with measures "such as improving the living environment and securing public transport", and an urban-function inducement zone (都市機能誘導区域). Note the verb: the municipality may prepare a plan, and the plan induces rather than restricts. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Only the Japanese text is legally binding.

    Road Structure Ordinance, art. 2(22)–(23): the design variables are motor-vehicle quantities

    二十二 計画交通量 道路の設計の基礎とするために、当該道路の存する地域の発展の動向、将来の自動車交通の状況等を勘案して…定める自動車の日交通量をいう。 二十三 設計速度 道路の設計の基礎とする自動車の速度をいう。

    (22) "Design traffic volume" means the daily volume of motor vehicles, determined […] as the basis for the design of the road, taking into account the development trends of the area in which the road is located, the future state of motor-vehicle traffic and so on. (23) "Design speed" means the speed of motor vehicles that is the basis for the design of the road.

    Source: 道路構造令 (Cabinet Order No. 320 of 1970), art. 2, current text on e-Gov. The omitted words specify who sets the volume under ministerial ordinance. Compare the welfare objective: neither people, nor pedestrians, nor cyclists appear in the two variables that size the road. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Discussion questions
    1. Tokyo and Nagoya differ by more than a factor of two in car share. List three causes (history, railway ownership, industry structure, land readjustment, road funding) and say which term of \(W\) each acts on. Which of them could a city outside Japan actually copy?
    2. The road manual counts three benefits because they can be measured "with sufficient accuracy". Is leaving out an effect better than including it with a wide error band? Answer separately for a rural bypass and for an urban widening.
    3. From 1954 to 2009 fuel taxes were earmarked for roads. How does an earmarked revenue stream change which projects get appraised, and which pass? Would you expect the end of earmarking to change what is built?
    4. In the worked example in section 7, the railway captures part of the land-value rise. Show why adding the land-value rise to the logsum in Term A double-counts. When might land values capture more than the access benefit, for example through agglomeration?
    5. Peak crowding of 140–160 % is a cost to every passenger, and each extra passenger makes the train more crowded for all the others. Where is that cost already counted in \(W\) (see Module 1, "Where is congestion?")? What does the crowding a passenger imposes on others imply for a peak fare, and why might a fare-funded operator not charge it?
    6. The garage certificate makes every car owner pay for Term D. Model it as a fixed cost in the car-ownership choice and discuss its effect on the equity floor in a rural town without buses.
    7. A Location Normalization Plan "may" be prepared and "induces" rather than restricts. Is a nudge-based instrument the right choice when the problem is long-run decline? What would a binding version look like, and who would lose?
