    Role model country

    Germany

    Reading time ≈ 40 minutes · Related modules: 3, 8, 9, 11, 12, 14 · Key terms: A C E constraints

    Germany combines two traditions that pull in different directions. At federal level it runs one of the most formalised cost–benefit appraisals in the world, built mainly around travel-time savings. In its cities, the planning goal is "more mobility with less traffic": avoid trips, shift them to walking, cycling and public transport, and improve what remains. Since 2024, road traffic law also lets municipalities act for climate, health and urban development. Germany is a good place to study how a precise, rule-bound engineering culture handles a change of objective.

    What to take from this page
    • How the federal transport infrastructure plan (BVWP 2030) puts numbers on Terms A, C and E, and which effects it leaves out, such as secondary induced traffic.
    • Why its four-module structure contains an explicit minimum accessibility test, an early form of the equity floor \(A_g \ge A_{\min}\).
    • How the 2024 reform of road traffic law (StVG and StVO) changed the legal objective of traffic orders from "safety and ease of traffic" to a wider list.
    • Where the German system is strong (standards, data, integrated public transport) and where it is criticized (carbon values, maintenance, rail reliability, speed limits).

    2. Germany's reading of \(W\)

    Module 1 summarized Germany as "formal national cost–benefit analysis for federal infrastructure; cities pursue Vermeiden – Verlagern – Verbessern". In the notation of the course, that means two different objective functions at two levels of government.

    Federal level: a priced \(W\) with fixed land use A C E

    Federal roads, railways and waterways are prioritized in the Federal Transport Infrastructure Plan 2030 (Bundesverkehrswegeplan, BVWP), adopted by the federal cabinet on 3 August 2016. It covers about 1,000 projects worth €269.6 billion: €132.8 billion for federal trunk roads, €112.3 billion for rail and €24.5 billion for waterways. Its method handbook scores projects in four modules:

    • Module A, cost–benefit analysis (NKA). This produces the benefit–cost ratio (Nutzen-Kosten-Verhältnis, NKV). Its benefit components include changes in vehicle operating costs (NB), exhaust emissions including CO₂ (NA), road safety (NS), passenger travel time (NRZ), freight transport time (NTZ), noise (NG), urban severance (NT), the implicit benefit of induced trips (NI) and life-cycle greenhouse emissions of the infrastructure (NL). The discount rate is 1.7 % a year. CO₂ is valued at €145 per tonne.
    • Module B, environmental and nature-conservation assessment. Effects that are not monetized, such as land take and the fragmentation of undisturbed landscapes.
    • Module C, spatial-planning assessment. Deficits in connection quality between higher-order centers, and each region's accessibility to the nearest motorway junction, airport or long-distance station. The handbook says explicitly that this module covers distributive questions such as minimum accessibility of regions. The NKA covers only the allocative sum of time savings.
    • Module D, urban-development assessment. Relief of town centers from through traffic, for example by bypasses.

    Mapped onto \(W\): Module A estimates Term A as time and cost savings with \(\omega_g=1\). It prices part of Term C (crashes, CO₂, air pollution, noise, severance) and counts Term E. Module B is a non-monetized stand-in for Term D and the land constraint. Module C is a soft version of the equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\). Term B (health from active travel) does not appear, because the plan covers only federal long-distance infrastructure.

    The demand model treats land use as fixed. It includes primary induced traffic: more trips and new destinations when generalized costs fall, modeled for roads with a gravity model. It explicitly excludes secondary induced traffic, meaning new residents and jobs that settle along an improved corridor (see the translated passage in section 7). In the language of Module 1, the BVWP is \(W\) evaluated at the "months–years" timescale and blind to the "decades" timescale. That is where most of the long-run demand response to motorway building takes place.

    Municipal level: avoid, shift, improve A B C

    German urban transport plans (Verkehrsentwicklungspläne, VEP, and newer sustainable urban mobility plans) mostly follow the avoid–shift–improve hierarchy. The German Environment Agency (UBA) sums it up as "more mobility with less traffic". Its point is that avoiding traffic does not mean stopping people from traveling. It means shortening distances through settlement structure and filling vehicles better (translation in section 7). In terms of \(W\), the slogan says: increase the logsum in Term A by bringing opportunities \(O_j\) closer, not by raising speeds, and so reduce vehicle-kilometers, which drive Term C.

    Many cities state their goal as a modal-split target, which is an instrument and not an objective in the sense of Module 1. Munich's Mobility Strategy 2035 (city council, 2021) aimed for "at least 80 % of traffic by exhaust-free travel by 2025", alongside Vision Zero. A share target is a reasonable proxy when mode shift is cheap and raises welfare. It can be met perversely, for example by making driving worse without making anything else better, and only \(W\) catches that.

    Constraints constraints

    • Carbon. The Federal Climate Change Act (KSG) set annual emission budgets per sector, including transport. The amendment of 15 July 2024 made compliance multi-year and cross-sector: missing the transport budget no longer automatically forces the transport ministry to adopt an emergency program. In terms of \(W\), the carbon constraint now applies to the whole economy instead of to transport alone, which weakens its effect on transport decisions.
    • Safety. Germany has Vision Zero as a stated policy aim, and Munich's strategy cites it. The Bundesrat asked in 2024 that Vision Zero be written into the road traffic regulations. According to the UBA, this had not happened. Safety is therefore priced (NS in the BVWP) and set through design rules, rather than imposed as a hard constraint as in Sweden.
    • Road traffic law as a constraint on instruments. Until 2024, §45(9) of the road traffic regulations (StVO) allowed restrictions on moving traffic only where a local danger "considerably exceeds" the general risk. This effectively ruled out many bus lanes, speed limits and cycle lanes. The 2024 reform (section 3) removed part of this constraint. It is better described as a change in the feasible set \(\Theta\) of plans than as a change in \(W\).

    A worked example: what the carbon value does C

    Take a motorway widening that, after induced traffic, adds \(\Delta E_{\mathrm{CO_2}} = 10{,}000\) t of CO₂ per year. The BVWP 2030 values a tonne at €145. The UBA's current recommendation for emissions in 2026 is €350 (2025 prices) at a 1 % pure time preference rate, and €1,000 with equal weighting of generations. The annual climate cost in Term C is therefore

    $$p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\,\Delta E = \begin{cases} 1.45\ \text{M€/yr} & p=145\\ 3.50\ \text{M€/yr} & p=350\\ 10.0\ \text{M€/yr} & p=1000\end{cases}$$

    Over an illustrative 40-year horizon at the BVWP discount rate of 1.7 %, the annuity factor is \((1-1.017^{-40})/0.017\approx 28.9\). Moving from €145 to €350 therefore adds about €59 million to the present value of costs, holding emissions constant. Martinsturner et al. (2024, TU Berlin, see resources) re-ran BVWP project appraisals with higher CO₂ prices and found that some benefit–cost ratios change materially. This is the "report the vector" rule from Module 1 in action: a project whose ranking depends on a contested price should be flagged.

    The FGSV rulebook

    The FGSV rules are the German counterpart of the AASHTO Green Book and the Highway Capacity Manual, with one difference: they are consensus documents produced by volunteer committees. Most are sold, not free. The key ones for this course:

    • HBS (Handbuch für die Bemessung von Straßenverkehrsanlagen, 2015 edition). The capacity manual, with parts A (motorways), L (rural roads) and S (urban roads). It defines quality levels (Qualitätsstufen des Verkehrsablaufs, QSV) A–F, analogous to US level of service. The FGSV runs a free question-and-answer page on it. See Module 8.
    • RASt 06 (Richtlinien für die Anlage von Stadtstraßen, 2006, corrected reprint). Urban street design. RASt designs from the edges inward ("städtebauliche Bemessung"): space for pedestrians, cycling and frontage uses is set first, and the carriageway gets what remains. In terms of \(W\), that is an implicit positive \(r\) on street space. See Module 12.
    • RiLSA (traffic signal guidelines, 2010 edition with 2015 amendments). See Module 9.
    • ERA (cycle facility recommendations). The edition on sale is still the 2010 one. A revision has been under discussion for years, and cycling advocates criticize the slow pace.
    • EAR 23 (parking facility recommendations, FGSV 283), and the rural and motorway design guidelines RAL and RAA.

    The 2024 reform of road traffic law

    The Road Traffic Act (StVG) empowers the ministry to issue the Road Traffic Regulations (StVO). Before 2024 the empowerment was framed around averting dangers to the "safety or ease of traffic" (Sicherheit oder Leichtigkeit des Verkehrs). In practice, "ease" meant the flow of motor traffic. A first attempt at reform failed in the Bundesrat in 2023. After mediation, the Bundestag and Bundesrat adopted the StVG amendment on 14 June 2024. The amended StVO, approved by the Bundesrat on 5 July 2024, entered into force on 11 October 2024.

    The new §6(4a) StVG (full translation in section 7) and the new §45(1) sentence 2 no. 7 StVO allow traffic orders "to improve the protection of the environment, including climate protection, to protect health or to support urban development". This applies in particular to bus lanes, bus priority at signals and "appropriate space" for walking and cycling, provided the ease of traffic is taken into account and safety is not impaired. The reform also makes it easier to impose 30 km/h near pedestrian crossings, playgrounds and busy school routes, and to close gaps of up to 500 m between 30 km/h sections (previously 300 m).

    Read as a change to the objective: before 2024, local traffic orders were in effect constrained to maximize vehicle flow subject to safety. After 2024 they may also serve Terms B, C and D. But "ease of traffic" must still be "taken into account" and safety must not be impaired. The legal objective is therefore a weighted list, not a single function. How courts weigh the items will determine how much really changed.

    4. Signature ideas and documented weaknesses

    Ideas the world can learn from

    • A published, multi-module federal appraisal. Few countries publish a single handbook that values time, safety, CO₂, noise and severance for road, rail and waterway projects on a common basis and adds a separate test for regional accessibility. Module C is a practical model for the equity floor. See Module 14.
    • Integrated public transport through Verkehrsverbünde. Hamburg created the first transport association in 1965. Operators share one timetable and one fare system across a region. In terms of \(W\), integration removes transfer penalties and fare discontinuities from \(V_{nj}\). The Deutschlandticket (section 5) extended the idea to the whole country.
    • Street design from the edges inward (RASt). Starting from the space needs of pedestrians and frontage uses, rather than from design traffic volumes, is a design-code version of putting a price on land.
    • Long, consistent travel-survey series. SrV (since 1972) and MiD let cities measure their objectives over decades. Munich's car share among residents' trips fell from 34 % to 29 % between 2017 and 2023, even though car ownership in the region rose (MVV). The goal can be monitored.
    • Open engineering pedagogy. German universities contributed key traffic-flow and simulation tools: the Intelligent Driver Model (Treiber, Hennecke and Helbing, 2000; developed further in Dresden), MATSim (TU Berlin with ETH Zürich) and the commercial PTV Visum/Vissim suite from Karlsruhe. See Module 7 and Module 10.

    Weaknesses and criticisms

    • Old carbon value and fixed land use in the BVWP. CO₂ at €145/t, from a method developed around 2012–2016, is well below the UBA's current €350/t. Secondary induced traffic is excluded by assumption. Both biases favor road capacity. Critics also note that the plan contains more "urgent need" projects than the budget can fund, which leaves prioritization to the political process.
    • No general motorway speed limit. Germany is the only EU member without a general speed limit on its motorways. Only an advisory speed of 130 km/h applies. The UBA has repeatedly estimated CO₂ savings from a limit. Politically, this is the clearest case where Term A, read as the speed preferences of motorway drivers, wins against Term C.
    • Maintenance backlog. The BVWP itself put maintenance and replacement at €141.6 billion for 2016–2030, about half the plan. High-profile failures followed: the A45 Rahmede viaduct was closed in December 2021 and demolished in 2023, and part of Dresden's Carolabrücke collapsed in September 2024. These show that Term E includes the life-cycle cost of assets already built, not only new capital.
    • Rail reliability. In 2024 only about 62.5 % of DB long-distance trains arrived less than six minutes late, and reports put 2025 at about 60 %. Years of under-investment in rail capacity show up as low reliability in Term A for rail users. Unreliability does not appear in a mode-share figure until people give up on the train.
    • Legal bias toward motor traffic flow until 2024 (see above), and a cycle-facility standard (ERA 2010) that has not been revised in more than fifteen years.
    • Paywalled standards. The FGSV rules are sold, which makes them harder to scrutinise in public and to teach outside Germany. This page links only to their catalog pages.

    5. Case studies

    Munich: strategy, measurement and the gap between them

    Munich created a dedicated Mobility Department (Mobilitätsreferat) in 2021 and adopted the Mobility Strategy 2035, with Vision Zero and an 80 % "exhaust-free" target for 2025. The MiD 2023 regional sample shows real movement: among residents, walking accounts for 29 % of trips, public transport 25 % and cycling 18 %. The car share fell five points to 29 %. In 2023, 40 % of Munich residents used a Deutschlandticket, against 16 % nationally. The 2025 target, however, was framed in a way that is hard to measure: it covers "traffic", and it counts exhaust-free cars. Lesson for \(W\): state targets in a measurable unit (trips, person-km, CO₂) and connect them to the welfare terms they serve. See Module 6.

    Freiburg-Vauban: parking-free by design

    Vauban was built on a 41-hectare former French army barracks site, vacated in 1991. Today about 5,500 people live there. Most streets are "parking-free" (stellplatzfrei): residents who own a car buy a space in a collective garage at the edge of the district, and households that sign up as car-free pay instead toward land held in reserve for a future garage. The city's information panels report 172 cars per 1,000 residents, based on 2013 statistics. In terms of \(W\), Vauban unbundles the price of parking from the price of housing. This makes \(r\,L\) visible to each household, so households that do not own a car stop subsidizing those that do. See Module 5 and Module 13. One caveat: Vauban's residents are self-selected and relatively affluent, so its low car ownership is not a transferable elasticity.

    The Deutschlandticket: a national flat fare

    Since 1 May 2023, one monthly subscription valid on all local and regional public transport in Germany has replaced hundreds of regional tariffs. Its price was €49, rose to €58 in 2025 and to €63 from January 2026 (deutschlandticket.de, DB Regio). In terms of \(W\), the ticket cuts the money cost \(c\) of public transport to zero at the margin for subscribers. It also removes the complexity cost of tariff boundaries, part of \(\mathbf{x}\) in \(V_{nj}\). The fare revenue lost raises Term E, multiplied by \(1+\mu\). The welfare question is whether the gains in A and the reductions in C, from diverted car trips, exceed \((1+\mu)\Delta R\) plus the cost of extra capacity on crowded lines. The answer depends heavily on how many car trips are actually diverted rather than new trips generated. The federal government and the Länder dispute the funding every year, which itself creates uncertainty for users.

    Berlin: a mobility law and its politics

    A citizens' initiative, Volksentscheid Fahrrad, collected over 100,000 signatures in 2016. In response, Berlin's parliament passed the Berlin Mobility Act (Mobilitätsgesetz) in June 2018, the first such law in Germany. It gives walking, cycling and public transport priority over private cars in planning. Implementation has been slower than the law's targets. A trial closure of Friedrichstraße to cars (2020–2023) ended in legal and political dispute, and in 2023 a new Senate put a number of cycle-lane projects on hold for review. Lesson: a statutory change to the objective does not deliver results by itself. Street space is reallocated project by project, and each project runs into the instruments, the courts and the electoral cycle.

    7. Translated key passages

    Road Traffic Act (StVG) §6(4a), as amended in 2024. Statutory text, translated in full

    Rechtsverordnungen nach Absatz 1 Satz 1 Nummer 2, 8, 15 Buchstabe b oder c, Nummer 16 oder 18 können auch erlassen werden zur Verbesserung des Schutzes der Umwelt, darunter des Klimaschutzes, zum Schutz der Gesundheit oder zur Unterstützung der städtebaulichen Entwicklung, soweit sie nicht bereits nach Absatz 4 erlassen werden können. […] Die nach Satz 1 erlassenen Rechtsverordnungen und auf ihnen beruhenden Anordnungen müssen neben der Verbesserung des Schutzes der Umwelt, des Schutzes der Gesundheit oder der Unterstützung der städtebaulichen Entwicklung die Leichtigkeit des Verkehrs berücksichtigen und dürfen die Sicherheit des Verkehrs nicht beeinträchtigen.

    (4a) Regulations under paragraph 1 sentence 1 nos. 2, 8, 15(b) or (c), 16 or 18 may also be issued to improve the protection of the environment, including climate protection, to protect health or to support urban development, insofar as they cannot already be issued under paragraph 4. These regulations shall in particular provide that municipalities may apply to the authorities designated under state law for the execution of the regulations for orders ensuring the safety and ease of traffic, improving the protection of the environment, protecting health or supporting urban development. Regulations issued under sentence 1, and orders based on them, must, in addition to improving the protection of the environment, protecting health or supporting urban development, take the ease of traffic into account and must not impair the safety of traffic.

    Source: gesetze-im-internet.de. The middle sentence is omitted from the original above for length; the translation covers all three sentences. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Only the German text is legally binding.

    German Environment Agency (UBA): "more mobility with less traffic"

    Gerade die Vermeidung von Verkehr wird oft missverstanden: Vorrangiges Ziel ist es dabei nicht, Verkehr zu unterbinden, sondern die Verkehrswege durch veränderte Siedlungs- und Produktionsstrukturen zu verkürzen oder die Auslastung der Fahrzeuge zu erhöhen […] Es geht also um mehr Mobilität mit weniger Verkehr!

    Avoiding traffic in particular is often misunderstood. The primary aim is not to prevent traffic, but to shorten the distances traveled through changed settlement and production structures, or to increase vehicle occupancy […]. So it is about more mobility with less traffic!

    Source: UBA, Nachhaltige Mobilität (topic page). Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). In course notation, "mobility" is the logsum of Term A, and "traffic" is vehicle-kilometers, the driver of Term C.

    BVWP 2030 method handbook: what induced traffic is left out

    Im BVWP 2030 wird daher davon ausgegangen, dass die nachfragebestimmenden sozioökonomischen und soziodemografischen Strukturdaten zwischen Planfall und Bezugsfall unverändert sind und sekundär induzierter Verkehr nicht Gegenstand der NKA ist.

    The BVWP 2030 therefore assumes that the socio-economic and socio-demographic structural data that determine demand are unchanged between the project case and the reference case, and that secondary induced traffic is not part of the cost–benefit analysis.

    Source: Methodenhandbuch zum Bundesverkehrswegeplan 2030, section III.2.3.1. "Secondary induced traffic" means traffic from new residents and jobs that settle near the improved infrastructure. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Discussion questions
    1. BVWP Module C tests minimum accessibility separately from the cost–benefit ratio. Is this better or worse than putting distributional weights \(\omega_g\) inside Term A? Consider transparency, manipulability and how each handles a remote region with few residents.
    2. The 2024 StVG wording requires that the "ease of traffic" be "taken into account". Write it as a constrained optimization: which is the objective, and which are the constraints? Would a court reading it as "do not reduce motor-vehicle capacity" make the reform ineffective?
    3. Using the worked example in section 2, find the CO₂ price at which a road project with a benefit–cost ratio of 1.5 at €145/t falls below 1. What else would you need to know?
    4. Munich's 2025 target counted exhaust-free cars as part of the 80 %. Which terms of \(W\) does an electric car improve, and which does it leave unchanged (congestion, crashes, land, severance)?
    5. Vauban's residents chose car-light living. How would you design a study that separates the effect of the parking rules from self-selection?
