    Role model country

    Germany

    Reading time ≈ 50 minutes · Related modules: 3, 8, 9, 11, 12, 14 · Key terms: A C E constraints

    Germany runs one of the most formalized transport appraisals in the world. It has a climate law with a cap for transport, and cities whose plans say "more mobility with less traffic". Yet transport emissions in 2023 were only about a tenth below their 1990 level, and the number of cars per resident is still rising. This page separates what German laws and plans say, how projects are appraised, which design rules apply and what actually gets built. The gaps between these layers, and the reasons for them, are the lesson. Germany is a good place to study how a precise, rule-bound engineering culture handles a change of objective.

    In brief
    The question
    Why has a country with a binding climate law, a published national appraisal and ambitious cities cut transport emissions by only about a tenth since 1990?
    Stated objectives
    A climate law that set a transport cap of 85 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent for 2030. Cities that aim to avoid, shift and improve traffic. Since 2024, a road traffic law that lets towns act for climate, health and urban development.
    How projects are appraised
    Federal roads, railways and waterways are ranked by a national benefit–cost ratio, plus three non-monetized tests. Urban rail and tram projects need a ratio above 1 to get federal co-funding. The federal plan values a tonne of CO₂ at €145; the environment agency now recommends €350.
    Design standards
    Technical rules written by expert committees (capacity, street design, signals, cycling). Until 2024, road traffic regulations allowed a bus lane or a lower speed limit only where a special danger was proven.
    What gets built
    146 million tonnes of transport emissions in 2023. 593 cars per 1,000 residents in 2026, from 330 in Berlin to 649 in Saarland. In the largest cities a third of trips are by car; in rural areas, more than 60%.
    Lessons for a decision maker
    A target steers nothing unless the appraisal and the funding rules use it. Ask which carbon value and which induced-traffic assumption sit inside a benefit–cost ratio, and which level of government pays for and decides each piece.
    Learning outcomes
    • Separate the federal and municipal layers of German practice, and within each, the stated objectives, appraisal, design rules and outcomes.
    • Explain how the federal transport infrastructure plan (BVWP 2030) puts numbers on Terms A, C and E, and which effects it leaves out, such as secondary induced traffic.
    • Explain why its four-module structure contains an explicit minimum accessibility test, an early form of the equity floor \(A_g \ge A_{\min}\).
    • Explain how the 2024 reform of road traffic law (StVG and StVO) changed the legal objective of traffic orders.
    • Use Germany to answer the course's central question for one country: why do climate targets and avoid–shift–improve plans coexist with rising car ownership?

    2. Stated objectives: what the laws and plans say

    Germany has two sets of transport objectives, written at two levels of government. Module 1 summarized them as "formal national cost–benefit analysis for federal infrastructure; cities pursue Vermeiden – Verlagern – Verbessern". This section starts with what each level says it wants.

    Federal level: climate, safety and equal living conditions

    • Carbon. The Federal Climate Change Act (KSG, 2019, tightened in 2021) set annual emission budgets per sector. For transport, the budget for 2030 was 85 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent. The amendment of 15 July 2024 made compliance multi-year and cross-sector: missing the transport budget no longer automatically forces the transport ministry to adopt an emergency program. In terms of \(W\), the carbon limit \(\bar E_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) now applies to the whole economy instead of to transport alone, which weakens its pull on transport decisions.
    • Safety. Germany has Vision Zero as a stated policy aim. The Bundesrat, the chamber of the states, asked in 2024 that Vision Zero be written into the road traffic regulations. According to the UBA, this had not happened. Safety is therefore priced in appraisal and set through design rules, rather than imposed as a hard limit as in Sweden.
    • Equal living conditions. Federal planning aims at "equivalent living conditions" in all regions. The federal infrastructure plan turns this into an explicit test of regional accessibility (section 3), a soft form of the equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\).
    • Road traffic law. Since 2024, traffic orders may also serve climate protection, health and urban development (section 4). Before, the law framed them around the "safety and ease of traffic".

    Municipal level: avoid, shift, improve

    German urban transport plans (Verkehrsentwicklungspläne, VEP, and newer sustainable urban mobility plans) mostly follow the avoid–shift–improve hierarchy. The German Environment Agency (UBA) sums it up as "more mobility with less traffic". Its point is that avoiding traffic does not mean stopping people from traveling. It means shortening distances through settlement structure and filling vehicles better (translation in section 9). In terms of \(W\), the slogan says: increase the logsum in Term A by bringing opportunities \(O_j\) closer, not by raising speeds, and so reduce vehicle-kilometers, which drive Term C.

    Many cities state their goal as a modal-split target, which is an instrument and not an objective in the sense of Module 1. Munich's Mobility Strategy 2035 (city council, 2021) aimed for "at least 80 % of traffic by exhaust-free travel by 2025", alongside Vision Zero. A share target is a reasonable proxy when mode shift is cheap and raises welfare. It can be met perversely, for example by making driving worse without making anything else better, and only \(W\) catches that. Berlin went further and wrote the priority of walking, cycling and public transport into a state law (section 7).

    Not every city says the same. Plans differ between city-states such as Berlin, large cities such as Munich, and rural districts where most trips are by car. The federal frame allows all of them.

    3. How projects are appraised

    Which appraisal a German project faces depends on who pays for it. Federal infrastructure goes through the federal transport plan. Urban rail and tram projects that want federal money go through a second, separate method.

    The federal transport infrastructure plan (BVWP)

    Federal roads, railways and waterways are prioritized in the Federal Transport Infrastructure Plan 2030 (Bundesverkehrswegeplan, BVWP), adopted by the federal cabinet on 3 August 2016. It covers about 1,000 projects worth €269.6 billion: €132.8 billion for federal trunk roads, €112.3 billion for rail and €24.5 billion for waterways. Its method handbook scores projects in four modules:

    • Module A, cost–benefit analysis. It produces the benefit–cost ratio (Nutzen-Kosten-Verhältnis, NKV). Benefits include travel-time and operating-cost savings, safety, emissions including CO₂, noise and urban severance. The discount rate is 1.7 % a year. CO₂ is valued at €145 per tonne.
    • Module B, environmental and nature-conservation assessment. Effects that are not monetized, such as land take and the fragmentation of undisturbed landscapes.
    • Module C, spatial-planning assessment. Deficits in connection quality between higher-order centers, and each region's accessibility to the nearest motorway junction, airport or long-distance station. The handbook says explicitly that this module covers distributive questions such as the minimum accessibility of regions. The cost–benefit analysis covers only the allocative sum of savings.
    • Module D, urban-development assessment. Relief of town centers from through traffic, for example by bypasses.

    Mapped onto \(W\): Module A estimates Term A as time and cost savings with equal weights for everyone. It prices part of Term C and counts Term E. Module B is a non-monetized stand-in for Term D and the land limit. Module C is a soft version of the equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\). Term B (health from active travel) does not appear, because the plan covers only federal long-distance infrastructure.

    What it assumes. The demand model treats land use as fixed. It includes primary induced traffic: more trips and new destinations when travel gets cheaper. It explicitly excludes secondary induced traffic, meaning new residents and jobs that settle along an improved corridor (translated passage in section 9). In the language of Module 1, the BVWP is \(W\) evaluated at the "months–years" timescale and blind to the "decades" timescale. That is where most of the long-run demand response to motorway building takes place.

    From ratio to law. The appraisal does not decide by itself. For roads, the Bundestag adopts the list of needed projects (Bedarfsplan) as an annex to a law, the Federal Trunk Road Expansion Act. The current list was adopted on 2 December 2016 (BMV). Critics note that the plan contains more "urgent need" projects than the budget can fund. Which of them are built first is then decided in budgets and politics, not by the ratio.

    The appraisal method is described in more detail, with a worked carbon example, in the box below. The short version: moving the carbon value from €145 to the UBA's current €350 per tonne adds about €59 million to the present cost of a road that generates 10,000 extra tonnes of CO₂ a year. Re-running BVWP appraisals with higher carbon prices changes some benefit–cost ratios materially (Martinsturner et al., 2024).

    Urban public transport: the standardized appraisal

    Cities and states build trams, light rail and suburban rail with federal co-funding under the Municipal Transport Financing Act (Gemeindeverkehrsfinanzierungsgesetz, GVFG). Each project must pass a common method, the Standardisierte Bewertung ("standardized appraisal"), currently in its version 2016+, in force since 1 July 2022 (BMV). A project whose benefits do not exceed its costs gets no federal money. Munich's second trunk line for its suburban rail, for example, reached a ratio of 1.05 in 2016, just above the threshold (Bundestag document 19/8511).

    The money behind this test has grown fast. Federal GVFG funds were about €665 million in 2020, €1 billion a year from 2021 and €2 billion from 2025, rising by 1.8 % a year after that (BMV). Funding rules decide what is appraised at all: a tram line competes for this money only if it passes the test.

    Under the hoodInside BVWP Module A: benefit components and what the carbon value does

    The cost–benefit module adds up these benefit components, each the difference between the "with project" and "without project" cases: changes in vehicle operating costs (NB), exhaust emissions including CO₂ (NA), road safety (NS), passenger travel time (NRZ), freight transport time (NTZ), noise (NG), urban severance (NT), the implicit benefit of induced trips (NI) and life-cycle greenhouse emissions of the infrastructure itself (NL). Costs are investment and maintenance. The benefit–cost ratio is the present value of benefits divided by the present value of costs, both discounted at 1.7 % a year.

    Worked example. Take a motorway widening that, after induced traffic, adds \(\Delta E_{\mathrm{CO_2}} = 10{,}000\) t of CO₂ per year. The BVWP 2030 values a tonne at €145. The UBA's current recommendation for emissions in 2026 is €350 (2025 prices) at a 1 % pure time preference rate, and €1,000 with equal weighting of generations. The annual climate cost in Term C is therefore

    $$p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\,\Delta E = \begin{cases} 1.45\ \text{M€/yr} & p=145\\ 3.50\ \text{M€/yr} & p=350\\ 10.0\ \text{M€/yr} & p=1000\end{cases}$$

    To turn a yearly cost into a present value, multiply it by the annuity factor: the present value of €1 a year. Over an illustrative 40-year horizon at 1.7 %, that factor is \((1-1.017^{-40})/0.017\approx 28.9\). Moving from €145 to €350 therefore adds about \(2.05\ \text{M€}\times 28.9\approx 59\) million euros to the present value of costs, holding emissions constant. Martinsturner et al. (2024, TU Berlin, see resources) re-ran BVWP project appraisals with higher CO₂ prices and found that some benefit–cost ratios change materially. This is the "report the vector" rule from Module 1 in action: a project whose ranking depends on a contested price should be flagged.

    Where it fails, and what others do. The fixed land-use assumption favors road capacity, because the long-run traffic it induces is left out. France's appraisal uses a much higher, rising carbon value; Sweden treats safety as a limit rather than a price. See Module 14 for a comparison of national appraisal frameworks.

    4. Design standards and rules

    The FGSV rulebook

    Most German design rules are written by the FGSV (Road and Transportation Research Association), a registered association whose committees of practitioners and academics produce the technical rules. They become binding when the federal government or the states adopt them. They are the German counterpart of the AASHTO Green Book and the Highway Capacity Manual, with one difference: they are consensus documents produced by volunteer committees. Most are sold, not free. The key ones for this course:

    • HBS (Handbuch für die Bemessung von Straßenverkehrsanlagen, 2015 edition). The capacity manual, with parts A (motorways), L (rural roads) and S (urban roads). It defines quality levels (Qualitätsstufen des Verkehrsablaufs, QSV) A–F, analogous to US level of service. The FGSV runs a free question-and-answer page on it. See Module 8.
    • RASt 06 (Richtlinien für die Anlage von Stadtstraßen, 2006, corrected reprint). Urban street design. RASt designs from the edges inward ("städtebauliche Bemessung"): space for pedestrians, cycling and frontage uses is set first, and the carriageway gets what remains. In terms of \(W\), that is an implicit positive \(r\) on street space. See Module 12.
    • RiLSA (traffic signal guidelines, 2010 edition with 2015 amendments). See Module 9.
    • ERA (cycle facility recommendations). The edition on sale is still the 2010 one. A revision has been under discussion for years, and cycling advocates criticize the slow pace.
    • EAR 23 (parking facility recommendations, FGSV 283), and the rural and motorway design guidelines RAL and RAA.

    The two most important rules point in different directions. The capacity manual grades roads by the flow of motor vehicles, as the US one does. The urban street guide starts from the space people need on foot. Which one governs a given street depends on the local authority and the engineers it hires.

    Road traffic law: the rule behind the rules

    Signs, speed limits and bus lanes are traffic orders under the Road Traffic Regulations (StVO). The Road Traffic Act (StVG) empowers the ministry to issue those regulations. Before 2024 the empowerment was framed around averting dangers to the "safety or ease of traffic" (Sicherheit oder Leichtigkeit des Verkehrs). In practice, "ease" meant the flow of motor traffic. Section 45(9) of the StVO also allowed restrictions on moving traffic only where a local danger "considerably exceeds" the general risk. This effectively ruled out many bus lanes, speed limits and cycle lanes. In the language of Module 1, it shrank the feasible set \(\Theta\) of plans a city could choose from.

    A first attempt at reform failed in the Bundesrat in 2023. After mediation, the Bundestag and Bundesrat adopted the StVG amendment on 14 June 2024. The amended StVO, approved by the Bundesrat on 5 July 2024, entered into force on 11 October 2024.

    The new §6(4a) StVG (full translation in section 9) and the new §45(1) sentence 2 no. 7 StVO allow traffic orders "to improve the protection of the environment, including climate protection, to protect health or to support urban development". This applies in particular to bus lanes, bus priority at signals and "appropriate space" for walking and cycling, provided the ease of traffic is taken into account and safety is not impaired. The reform also makes it easier to impose 30 km/h near pedestrian crossings, playgrounds and busy school routes, and to close gaps of up to 500 m between 30 km/h sections (previously 300 m).

    Read as a change to the objective: before 2024, local traffic orders were in effect constrained to maximize vehicle flow subject to safety. After 2024 they may also serve Terms B, C and D. But "ease of traffic" must still be "taken into account" and safety must not be impaired. The legal objective is therefore a weighted list, not a single function. How courts weigh the items will determine how much really changed.

    5. What gets built: observed outcomes

    • Emissions. Transport emitted about 146 million tonnes of greenhouse gases in 2023, roughly 22 % of the national total and only 10.9 % less than in 1990. Other sectors cut far more. The UBA's projections show transport staying far from its targets through 2030 (UBA). The gap to the 85-million-tonne budget for 2030 is about 60 million tonnes a year.
    • Cars. At the start of 2026, Germany had 593 cars per 1,000 residents, up from 588 two years earlier (Destatis).
    • Trips. Nationally, the share of trips made by car (as driver or passenger) fell from 57 % in 2017 to 53 % in 2023, and walking rose from 22 % to 26 % (MiD 2023). In Munich the car share among residents' trips fell from 34 % to 29 % over the same years, even though car ownership in the region rose.
    • Safety. About 3.4 deaths per 100,000 residents, a low rate by world standards and less than a third of the US rate, but higher than in Sweden, Japan or Singapore.
    • No general motorway speed limit. Germany is the only EU member without one. Only an advisory speed of 130 km/h applies. The UBA has repeatedly estimated the CO₂ savings from a limit.
    • Maintenance backlog. The BVWP itself put maintenance and replacement at €141.6 billion for 2016–2030, about half the plan. The A45 Rahmede viaduct was closed in December 2021 and demolished in 2023, and part of Dresden's Carolabrücke collapsed in September 2024. Term E includes the life-cycle cost of assets already built, not only new capital.
    • Rail reliability. In 2024 only about 62.5 % of DB long-distance trains arrived less than six minutes late, and reports put 2025 at about 60 %. Years of under-investment show up as low reliability in Term A for rail users. Unreliability does not appear in a mode-share figure until people give up on the train.

    6. Where the layers diverge, and why

    LayerFederalMunicipal
    Stated objectivesCarbon budget, Vision Zero aim, equal living conditionsAvoid, shift, improve; modal targets; Vision Zero in some cities
    AppraisalBVWP: benefit–cost ratio with €145/t CO₂ and fixed land use, plus three non-monetized modulesStandardized appraisal for rail and tram projects seeking federal money; no common method for most street projects
    Design rulesRoad traffic law (until 2024: flow and safety only); motorway design guidelinesFGSV capacity manual and street design guide, applied locally
    OutcomesTransport emissions 11 % below 1990; car ownership rising; maintenance backlogFalling car share of trips in large cities; little change in rural areas

    Five reasons explain most of the gap between the climate target and the outcome.

    • The target is not inside the appraisal. The carbon budget is a limit on the whole sector, and since 2024 on the whole economy. The appraisal that ranks federal projects uses an older carbon price and leaves out secondary induced traffic. Both choices favor road capacity. A project can pass the ratio while the sector misses its budget.
    • Funding rules. Federal trunk roads are paid for by the federal government once they are in the needs plan. Urban public transport needs co-funding, a local share and a passed standardized appraisal. The Deutschlandticket's funding is disputed every year between the federal government and the states (section 7). Money that is secure gets planned for; money that is uncertain does not.
    • Who decides. The federal level owns motorways and federal roads. The states execute road traffic law and approve traffic orders; the 2024 reform lets municipalities apply to the authorities designated under state law. Cities plan their streets but often cannot change the rules that govern them.
    • Standards by consensus. FGSV committees produce careful, tested rules, but slowly. The cycle facility guide still dates from 2010.
    • History and political power. Motorway building in Germany goes back to the 1930s, and a general speed limit has been debated for decades without being adopted. It is the clearest case where one reading of Term A, the speed preferences of motorway drivers, wins against Term C in national politics.

    Internal variety: sixteen states, many cities

    Berlin has 330 cars per 1,000 residents; Saarland has 649 (Destatis, 2026). In the largest cities, residents make 33 % of their trips by car, 31 % on foot, 21 % by public transport and 15 % by bicycle. In rural areas, more than 60 % of trips are by car (BMV, MiD 2023). Berlin passed a mobility law; Freiburg built a parking-free district; Munich created a dedicated mobility department. The federal rules are the same for all of them. The differences come from density, history, state politics and how each city uses the room the rules leave.

    The same problem, here

    A suburb 15 km from the city center is growing. Its one radial road into the city is jammed every morning. What would competent German planners typically propose, and why? This is a stylized judgment, not a documented case.

    • The likely answer depends on who owns the road. If it is a federal road, a widening or bypass can be entered into the federal plan and scored with the national benefit–cost ratio. In parallel, the region may extend or improve suburban rail or a tram line and seek federal co-funding through the standardized appraisal. Since 2024 the city can more easily add a bus lane on its own streets.
    • Objectives: the federal plan maximizes a benefit–cost ratio; the city plan aims to avoid and shift traffic. Both are official.
    • Constraints: budgets, and road traffic law that until 2024 made bus lanes hard to justify.
    • Models: the federal model holds land use fixed, which favors the road; the rail appraisal counts benefits differently.
    • Institutions: the level of government that owns the road decides which appraisal applies, and so, often, which answer wins.

    In Germany, the split between levels of government explains more of the answer than any difference in stated goals. Compare the US, Japan and Singapore.

    The German layers, read as \(W\)

    Federal appraisal: Term A as time and cost savings with equal weights, part of Term C priced (CO₂ at €145/t), Term E counted, land and nature assessed without money values, minimum regional accessibility tested separately, land use fixed. Municipal plans: Term A as access through proximity, with modal-split targets standing in for C. Road traffic law: since 2024, a weighted list of flow, safety, climate, health and urban development. Carbon budget: a limit on the whole economy since 2024. This is our interpretation; agencies do not state it this way.

    What other countries can borrow

    • A published, multi-module federal appraisal. Few countries publish a single handbook that values time, safety, CO₂, noise and severance for road, rail and waterway projects on a common basis and adds a separate test for regional accessibility. Module C is a practical model for the equity floor. See Module 14.
    • Integrated public transport through Verkehrsverbünde. Hamburg created the first transport association in 1965. Operators share one timetable and one fare system across a region. In terms of \(W\), integration removes transfer penalties and fare discontinuities from \(V_{nj}\). The Deutschlandticket extended the idea to the whole country.
    • Street design from the edges inward (RASt). Starting from the space needs of pedestrians and frontage uses, rather than from design traffic volumes, is a design-code version of putting a price on land.
    • Long, consistent travel-survey series. SrV (since 1972) and MiD let cities measure their objectives over decades.
    • Open engineering pedagogy. German universities contributed key traffic-flow and simulation tools: the Intelligent Driver Model (Treiber, Hennecke and Helbing, 2000; developed further in Dresden), MATSim (TU Berlin with ETH Zürich) and the commercial PTV Visum/Vissim suite from Karlsruhe. See Module 7 and Module 10.

    And two things to avoid: paywalled standards, which are harder to scrutinize in public and to teach outside Germany (this page links only to their catalog pages); and a cycle-facility standard left unrevised for more than fifteen years.

    7. Case studies

    Munich: strategy, measurement and the gap between them

    Munich created a dedicated Mobility Department (Mobilitätsreferat) in 2021 and adopted the Mobility Strategy 2035, with Vision Zero and an 80 % "exhaust-free" target for 2025. The MiD 2023 regional sample shows real movement: among residents, walking accounts for 29 % of trips, public transport 25 % and cycling 18 %. The car share fell five points to 29 %. In 2023, 40 % of Munich residents used a Deutschlandticket, against 16 % nationally. The 2025 target, however, was framed in a way that is hard to measure: it covers "traffic", and it counts exhaust-free cars. Lesson for \(W\): state targets in a measurable unit (trips, person-km, CO₂) and connect them to the welfare terms they serve. See Module 6.

    Freiburg-Vauban: parking-free by design

    Vauban was built on a 41-hectare former French army barracks site, vacated in 1991. Today about 5,500 people live there. Most streets are "parking-free" (stellplatzfrei): residents who own a car buy a space in a collective garage at the edge of the district, and households that sign up as car-free pay instead toward land held in reserve for a future garage. The city's information panels report 172 cars per 1,000 residents, based on 2013 statistics, against 593 nationally today. In terms of \(W\), Vauban unbundles the price of parking from the price of housing. This makes \(r\,L\) visible to each household, so households that do not own a car stop subsidizing those that do. See Module 5 and Module 13. One caveat: Vauban's residents are self-selected and relatively affluent, so its low car ownership is not a transferable elasticity.

    The Deutschlandticket: a national flat fare

    Since 1 May 2023, one monthly subscription valid on all local and regional public transport in Germany has replaced hundreds of regional tariffs. Its price was €49, rose to €58 in 2025 and to €63 from January 2026 (deutschlandticket.de, DB Regio). The ticket cuts the money cost \(c\) of public transport to zero at the margin for subscribers. It also removes the complexity cost of tariff boundaries, part of \(\mathbf{x}\) in \(V_{nj}\).

    To judge it with \(W\), count each effect once. For people who already rode, the lower price is a transfer: their gain in Term A equals the fare revenue the operators lose, which raises Term E. The two cancel, except that the lost revenue must be replaced by taxes, which costs \(\mu\) per euro. The real gains come from changed behavior: new trips that people value, and car trips diverted to public transport, which reduce crashes, emissions and noise (Term C) and delay for the drivers who remain (Term A). Against these stand \(\mu\) times the lost revenue and the cost of extra capacity on crowded lines. The answer depends heavily on how many car trips are actually diverted rather than new trips generated. The federal government and the Länder dispute the funding every year, which itself creates uncertainty for users.

    Berlin: a mobility law and its politics

    A citizens' initiative, Volksentscheid Fahrrad, collected over 100,000 signatures in 2016. In response, Berlin's parliament passed the Berlin Mobility Act (Mobilitätsgesetz) in June 2018, the first such law in Germany. It gives walking, cycling and public transport priority over private cars in planning. Implementation has been slower than the law's targets. A trial closure of Friedrichstraße to cars (2020–2023) ended in legal and political dispute, and in 2023 a new Senate put a number of cycle-lane projects on hold for review. Lesson: a statutory change to the objective does not deliver results by itself. Street space is reallocated project by project, and each project runs into the instruments, the courts and the electoral cycle.

    9. Translated key passages

    Road Traffic Act (StVG) §6(4a), as amended in 2024. Statutory text, translated in full

    Rechtsverordnungen nach Absatz 1 Satz 1 Nummer 2, 8, 15 Buchstabe b oder c, Nummer 16 oder 18 können auch erlassen werden zur Verbesserung des Schutzes der Umwelt, darunter des Klimaschutzes, zum Schutz der Gesundheit oder zur Unterstützung der städtebaulichen Entwicklung, soweit sie nicht bereits nach Absatz 4 erlassen werden können. […] Die nach Satz 1 erlassenen Rechtsverordnungen und auf ihnen beruhenden Anordnungen müssen neben der Verbesserung des Schutzes der Umwelt, des Schutzes der Gesundheit oder der Unterstützung der städtebaulichen Entwicklung die Leichtigkeit des Verkehrs berücksichtigen und dürfen die Sicherheit des Verkehrs nicht beeinträchtigen.

    (4a) Regulations under paragraph 1 sentence 1 nos. 2, 8, 15(b) or (c), 16 or 18 may also be issued to improve the protection of the environment, including climate protection, to protect health or to support urban development, insofar as they cannot already be issued under paragraph 4. These regulations shall in particular provide that municipalities may apply to the authorities designated under state law for the execution of the regulations for orders ensuring the safety and ease of traffic, improving the protection of the environment, protecting health or supporting urban development. Regulations issued under sentence 1, and orders based on them, must, in addition to improving the protection of the environment, protecting health or supporting urban development, take the ease of traffic into account and must not impair the safety of traffic.

    Source: gesetze-im-internet.de. The middle sentence is omitted from the original above for length; the translation covers all three sentences. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Only the German text is legally binding.

    German Environment Agency (UBA): "more mobility with less traffic"

    Gerade die Vermeidung von Verkehr wird oft missverstanden: Vorrangiges Ziel ist es dabei nicht, Verkehr zu unterbinden, sondern die Verkehrswege durch veränderte Siedlungs- und Produktionsstrukturen zu verkürzen oder die Auslastung der Fahrzeuge zu erhöhen […] Es geht also um mehr Mobilität mit weniger Verkehr!

    Avoiding traffic in particular is often misunderstood. The primary aim is not to prevent traffic, but to shorten the distances traveled through changed settlement and production structures, or to increase vehicle occupancy […]. So it is about more mobility with less traffic!

    Source: UBA, Nachhaltige Mobilität (topic page). Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). In course notation, "mobility" is the logsum of Term A, and "traffic" is vehicle-kilometers, the driver of Term C.

    BVWP 2030 method handbook: what induced traffic is left out

    Im BVWP 2030 wird daher davon ausgegangen, dass die nachfragebestimmenden sozioökonomischen und soziodemografischen Strukturdaten zwischen Planfall und Bezugsfall unverändert sind und sekundär induzierter Verkehr nicht Gegenstand der NKA ist.

    The BVWP 2030 therefore assumes that the socio-economic and socio-demographic structural data that determine demand are unchanged between the project case and the reference case, and that secondary induced traffic is not part of the cost–benefit analysis.

    Source: Methodenhandbuch zum Bundesverkehrswegeplan 2030, section III.2.3.1. "Secondary induced traffic" means traffic from new residents and jobs that settle near the improved infrastructure. Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Discussion questions
    1. BVWP Module C tests minimum accessibility separately from the cost–benefit ratio. Is this better or worse than putting distributional weights \(\omega_g\) inside Term A? Consider transparency, manipulability and how each handles a remote region with few residents.
    2. The 2024 StVG wording requires that the "ease of traffic" be "taken into account". Write it as a constrained optimization: which is the objective, and which are the constraints? Would a court reading it as "do not reduce motor-vehicle capacity" make the reform ineffective?
    3. Using the worked example in section 3, find the CO₂ price at which a road project with a benefit–cost ratio of 1.5 at €145/t falls below 1. What else would you need to know?
    4. The transport sector's carbon budget became part of an economy-wide total in 2024. Should a national appraisal then use the shadow price of the economy-wide target, a damage estimate, or something else? Which choice links the appraisal to the limit?
    5. Munich's 2025 target counted exhaust-free cars as part of the 80 %. Which terms of \(W\) does an electric car improve, and which does it leave unchanged (crashes, land, severance, delay to others)?
    6. Vauban's residents chose car-light living. How would you design a study that separates the effect of the parking rules from self-selection?
