    Role model country

    Singapore

    City-state · Reading time ≈ 50 minutes · Key terms: A C D E constraints

    Singapore is the clearest case in this course of a society that treats land as the binding constraint on transport. It has priced road congestion since 1975, has capped the number of vehicles by auction since 1990, and has held growth of the car fleet at zero since 2018. It plans housing, jobs and rail together under a single government. It is also the role model where what the government says it wants and what gets built line up most closely. The reason is institutional: one government owns most of the land, builds most of the homes, runs the roads and sets the prices. That same concentration of power explains the costs: high car prices, a rationing system many residents find unfair, appraisals that are not published, and a model few democracies can copy whole.

    In brief
    The question
    Why do Singapore's stated goals and its outcomes line up so much more closely than in other countries, and what does that alignment cost?
    Stated objectives
    "20-minute towns and a 45-minute city" by walking, cycling and public transport (Land Transport Master Plan 2040). Land is treated as the binding limit: roads already take about 12% of the island. Vision Zero is an aspiration, not a rule.
    How projects are appraised
    No published national appraisal manual, and project appraisals are generally not public. Decisions are explained in plans and in replies to Parliament.
    Design standards
    The main rules are instruments: a quota on vehicles, sold by auction, and road charges adjusted to keep traffic within a target speed band. Developers must assess walking and cycling, not only traffic.
    What gets built
    A rail network of 271 km (2025), planned to reach about 360 km in the early 2030s. Public transport carries 65% of peak motorized journeys (2023). The number of private cars has been flat for a decade. 2.35 road deaths per 100,000 residents, 60% of them motorcyclists.
    Lessons for a decision maker
    Prices and quantity limits work when one body controls land, roads and transit and keeps the rules stable for decades. Ask who in your city controls each of these, and how a decision can be checked if its appraisal is never published.
    Learning outcomes
    • Separate Singapore's stated objectives, appraisal practice, rules and instruments, and observed outcomes, and explain why they diverge less than elsewhere.
    • Map Singapore's instruments onto \(W\): ERP as a charge for the delay drivers impose on others, the Certificate of Entitlement (COE) as a quantity limit whose market price is its shadow price, and land as Term D and a hard limit.
    • Describe the institutional set-up (MOT, LTA, URA and HDB) and how integrated land-use and transport planning works in practice.
    • Identify where the model is weak: equity of rationing by price, forecasting and timing risk, road safety for motorcyclists and older pedestrians, and unpublished appraisals.
    • Use Singapore to answer the course's central question for one country: how much of what it builds follows from objectives, constraints, models and institutions?

    2. Stated objectives: what the laws and plans say

    The course framework in Module 1 summarizes Singapore as: maximize access subject to a binding land constraint; internalize congestion directly by price and quantity control. This section shows where that reading comes from, in the government's own words.

    Land as the binding limit D constraints

    The core argument appears in nearly every official document. When the Ministry of Transport defended the COE system in Parliament in September 2025, its reasoning was about land, not congestion: roads already take up 12% of the island, and more roads would mean less land for housing, schools and hospitals. "We do not have enough land for every person, or even every household, to own a car" (section 9). In the notation of the course, Singapore sets \(r\), the opportunity cost of land, very high. It also treats total road and parking land as close to fixed: a limit \(L\le\bar L\) in the objective.

    Access as the objective, with a floor for every town A constraints

    The positive objective in the current plan, the Land Transport Master Plan 2040 (LTMP 2040, published 2019), is phrased in travel time to opportunities, not in vehicle speed: "20-minute towns and a 45-minute city". All journeys to the nearest neighborhood center by walking, cycling or riding public transport should take less than 20 minutes. Nine in ten peak-period journeys by these modes should take less than 45 minutes. These are versions of the equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\), applied to every town.

    The plan is frank about the starting point. About 9 in 10 such trips to the nearest neighborhood center already took under 20 minutes, but only about two-thirds of peak-period journeys took under 45 minutes. LTMP 2040 also states that public transport journey times rose between 2008 and 2012 because average trip distances grew as new housing moved further from the center. This is the planners' own admission that Term A depends on where homes and jobs are, not just on network speed. The plan's response, "bringing jobs closer to homes", is a land-use lever. It does not mention road capacity.

    Health and safety B C

    LTMP 2040 adds a "Healthy Lives, Safer Journeys" strand: more space for public transport, active mobility and community uses, a cycling-path network growing from more than 440 km (2019) to more than 1,000 km by 2040, cleaner-energy bus and taxi fleets by 2040, and a "Vision Zero" aspiration for road deaths. Unlike Sweden, Singapore does not define Vision Zero as a binding limit. It is stated as an aspiration alongside education and enforcement.

    3. How projects are appraised

    Singapore does not publish a national cost–benefit manual comparable to the US benefit–cost guidance, Germany's federal transport plan method or the UK's appraisal guidance (TAG). Project-level appraisals of rail lines, roads or road-pricing changes are generally not public. For a course centered on \(W\) this is the biggest gap. The objective is visible in targets and instruments, not in a published appraisal.

    What is public:

    • Plans and their reasoning. LTMP 2040 and URA's Master Plan state objectives and targets. Rail alignments and stations are safeguarded in the Master Plan decades in advance.
    • Parliamentary replies. Major decisions on COE, ERP and fares appear as ministerial statements and replies to Parliament. For Singapore, these short texts are the main public source of reasoning and data.
    • Transport Impact Assessment for developers. LTA's TIA Guidelines for Developments (2019 edition), with the Walking and Cycling Plan (WCP) requirements, make developers assess pedestrians and cyclists as well as traffic. An addendum (2020) sets requirements for microsimulation, calibration and network-level assessment, relevant to Module 10. This is the one appraisal procedure that is fully published.
    • Histories after the fact. The Centre for Liveable Cities (CLC), a government think tank, publishes book-length studies of how past decisions were taken, including failures. They are a form of evaluation after the event, not before it.

    The vehicle quota and road charges are set administratively, by rules the government publishes, not by project appraisal. Whether the quota is too tight or too loose is answered by policy, not by a published comparison of costs and benefits (section 4).

    4. Rules and instruments

    In most countries, design manuals do the steering. In Singapore the strongest rules are two economic instruments, one for the number of vehicles and one for their use. They correspond to the two halves of the Module 1 box "Price or limit?".

    Quantity: the Certificate of Entitlement constraints

    Under the Vehicle Quota System (VQS), introduced on 1 May 1990, every new vehicle needs a Certificate of Entitlement (COE), valid for ten years. The quota for each period is set from the number of vehicles deregistered plus an allowed growth rate. Since February 2018 that growth rate has been 0% for cars and motorcycles, so the car fleet is in effect capped: one car out, one car in. COEs are sold in sealed-bid, uniform-price auctions. Bidding takes place twice a month, and every successful bidder in a category pays the same "quota premium" (LTA OneMotoring).

    This is the clearest real-world example of a limit whose shadow price you can observe. The shadow price, introduced in Module 1, is how much one more unit of a limited thing would be worth. Here it is the value of one more vehicle license, and the auction reveals it every fortnight as the COE premium. Premiums for cars have at times exceeded S$100,000. Wikipedia's compilation of LTA bidding results lists about S$150,000 for Category B in October 2023. Spread over the ten-year life of the certificate, S$100,000 is roughly S$35 a day, before any fuel, parking or road charges (the calculation is in the box below).

    Price: Electronic Road Pricing A E

    Electronic Road Pricing (ERP) charges vehicles for using congested roads at congested times. LTA's stated rule is to adjust charges "to keep traffic moving at an optimal speed" (OneMotoring). Rates are reviewed periodically. They are raised where measured speeds fall below a target band and lowered where speeds rise above it. The charge varies by vehicle type and, in peak hours, by half-hour.

    Read with \(W\), count each effect once. The delay that drivers cause each other is already in Term A, because Term A uses everyone's actual travel times. The charge is the instrument that reduces that delay: it makes each driver weigh the delay they impose on others (Module 1, "Where is congestion?"). The money itself is a transfer from drivers (Term A) to the government (revenue in Term E). The welfare gain comes from the trips that shift in time, route or mode, and from the faster journeys of everyone who remains.

    Other rules

    • Design standards. LTA's Code of Practice and street-design guidelines. Singapore borrows heavily from British and Australian practice for junction and capacity analysis.
    • Transport Impact Assessment (section 3): developers must plan for walking and cycling, not only for cars.
    • Public bus contracting. Since the Bus Contracting Model (2016) the government owns the bus fleet and depots and tenders route packages to operators, so service levels are set by LTA rather than by operators' profits.
    Under the hoodThe COE premium as a Lagrange multiplier, and ERP as an approximate congestion charge

    The land limit. In the notation of Module 1, Singapore's planning problem can be written as

    $$\max_\theta\; W(\theta)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad L_{\text{roads}}(\theta)+L_{\text{parking}}(\theta)\le \bar L .$$

    If road space cannot grow, the number of vehicles using it has to be managed. The vehicle cap is one way: write it as \(N(\theta)\le \bar N\). Its Lagrange multiplier \(\lambda\) is the marginal value of one more vehicle license, and the COE auction reveals it as the premium.

    Annualizing the shadow price. Suppose a car COE clears at S$100,000 for ten years and the buyer's discount rate is 5%. The annuity factor, the present value of S$1 a year for ten years, is \(\big(1-1.05^{-10}\big)/0.05 \approx 7.72\). So the annual shadow price of the right to keep one car on the road is about \(100{,}000/7.72\approx\) S$13,000, or roughly S$35 per day.

    Two interpretations follow. First, this is the private value of car ownership to the marginal bidder, and it puts an upper bound on what society forgoes by holding \(\bar N\) fixed. Second, it is not a measure of the external cost of a car. The cap is set by land and congestion policy, and the price adjusts to whatever demand is. If the cap were loosened, the premium would fall. Delay would rise (counted in Term A), and so would crashes, emissions (Term C) and land take (Term D). The welfare question is whether the marginal \(\lambda\) exceeds the marginal cost an extra car imposes on everyone else through these terms. Singapore has answered it administratively, not by appraisal.

    Why a speed band approximates the right charge. On a single road with travel time \(t(q)\) rising with flow \(q\), the delay one more vehicle imposes on all the others is \(q\,t'(q)\); its money value is the charge that would make each driver weigh it. ERP does not compute this. It targets a speed band instead. As Module 13 shows, for a link with a known speed–flow function, holding speed near the top of the stable range keeps flow near capacity without breakdown, which approximates the optimal charge. The rule is simple to explain and to audit, which may matter more for public acceptance than precision.

    5. What gets built: observed outcomes

    • Public transport share rising. Public transport's share of peak-period motorized journeys rose from 57% in 2012 to 65% in 2023 (MOT, 2025).
    • A flat car fleet. There were 524,312 private cars at the end of 2025, almost the same as the 519,645 of 2015. The growth in cars since then is mostly private-hire cars, from about 29,000 in 2015 to about 96,000 in 2025 (LTA, 2025).
    • A growing rail network: 271 km in 2025, with about 360 km planned for the early 2030s.
    • Road safety for vulnerable groups. Singapore's fatality rate (2.35 per 100,000 in 2024) is higher than that of Sweden and rose from 2.30 in 2023. Motorcyclists account for about 60% of deaths, and older people are overrepresented among pedestrian fatalities. Low car ownership does not by itself bring Vision Zero.
    • Forecasting and timing risk. Integrated planning can fail when demand forecasts do. The North East Line opened in 2003 with fewer than 150,000 daily passengers against forecasts of 200,000–250,000, because housing in Sengkang and Punggol was scaled back after the 1997 Asian financial crisis. Buangkok station stayed closed until 2006 because it had too few homes within 400 m (CLC 2018).
    • Technology and delivery risk. ERP 2, the satellite-based successor contracted in 2016, was delayed several times, partly by the global microchip shortage. On-board unit installation began only in November 2023, and gantry-based charging ends on 31 December 2026. Its satellite positioning also raises privacy questions that a gantry system did not.
    • Revenue is not earmarked. According to MOT, COE revenue averages about S$4–6 billion a year and goes into general revenue. Operating subsidies for bus and rail are about S$2 billion a year. The link between the charge and transport improvement is political, not legal, unlike the London or New York schemes.

    6. Where the layers diverge, and why

    LayerWhat it says or doesFit with the stated objective
    Stated objectivesAccess by walking, cycling and public transport; land as the limit; Vision Zero as aspiration—
    AppraisalNot published; reasoning given in plans and to ParliamentCannot be checked from outside
    Rules and instrumentsVehicle cap, road pricing, integrated new towns, bus contractingClose: the instruments serve the land limit directly
    OutcomesRising public transport share, flat private car fleet, growing railClose on access and land; weak on safety for motorcyclists and older pedestrians; costly for lower-income would-be drivers

    In the other role models, this course finds large gaps between what governments say and what gets built. Singapore's gaps are smaller. Four institutional features explain why.

    • One government, one budget. The national government is also the city government. Roads, rail, buses, vehicle registration, the quota and road pricing all sit with one agency, the Land Transport Authority, formed in 1995 by merging several road, rail and vehicle agencies. No state, county or private railway can pull in another direction.
    • Land in public hands. The government and statutory boards already owned about 60% of land in the early 1970s, according to CLC. Estimates by the property consultancy Knight Frank now put the state's share at about 90% (EdgeProp). Much of it was acquired under the Land Acquisition Act of 1966, which lets the state take private land for public purposes (National Library Board). A government that owns the land can put the homes where the rail is, and the rail where the homes will be.
    • Continuity. The same rules have been adjusted, not reversed, for fifty years: road pricing since 1975, the quota since 1990.
    • Limited contestation. Plans face less public challenge than in the other role-model countries, and appraisals are not published. That speeds decisions and also removes a check on them. (Qualitative judgment.)

    Where the layers do diverge, it is in the terms the instruments do not reach. The quota allocates cars to those with the highest willingness to pay, which tracks income. When COE prices reached record highs, academics and opposition politicians proposed needs-based allocation. The government acknowledges the system is "neither perfect nor popular" but argues that needs-based rules would be "subjective, divisive" and would benefit few people (MOT, 22 September 2025). In \(W\) terms, the COE is efficient with equal weights for everyone, but its distributional effects are addressed, if at all, through spending, not through the instrument. And neither the quota nor road pricing targets motorcycle safety.

    Internal variety: small, and across time more than space

    With one government, Singapore has less internal variety than the other role models. The variety it has is between generations of towns. Older new towns grew around bus interchanges and were connected to rail later. Punggol and Sengkang were planned with rail from the start, and Tengah puts roads underground (section 7). Access also differs between people: about 30% of peak motorized journeys are still made by private car or motorcycle, by the households that can afford a COE or a motorcycle.

    The same problem, here

    A suburb 15 km from the city center is growing. Its one radial road into the city is jammed every morning. What would competent Singapore planners typically propose, and why? This is a stylized judgment, not a documented case.

    • The likely answer: not a wider road. If speeds fall below the target band, the road charge at that point rises. More buses and bus priority come first. A rail line, if one is planned, is already safeguarded in the Master Plan. Over the longer run, the government moves jobs to a regional center closer to the suburb.
    • Objectives: access by public transport and active modes, stated in travel-time targets.
    • Constraints: land, treated as fixed; the vehicle quota.
    • Models: not public, so this layer cannot be compared.
    • Institutions: one agency owns the road, the buses, the rail plan and the price; the state owns most of the land.

    In Singapore, the constraint (land) and the institutions that act on it explain most of the answer. Compare the US, Germany and Japan.

    What other countries can borrow

    1. Price congestion where it occurs. Singapore's Area Licensing Scheme (1975) was the first urban congestion charge in the world. London (2003), Stockholm (2006) and New York (2025) followed decades later. The lesson is less the technology than the political continuity: charges have been adjusted for fifty years.
    2. Separate ownership from use, and price both. The COE limits the stock of vehicles. ERP prices the flow. Fuel and parking charges add a variable cost. This combination is unique in the world.
    3. Plan land use and transport as one system. New towns are designed around rail stations, with the town center, bus interchange and MRT station co-located. The CLC study of the first 50 years of integration attributes Singapore's avoidance of sprawl to this coordination.
    4. Targets in the right units. "20-minute towns, 45-minute city" measures access by all non-car modes, not vehicle speed, and is close to an accessibility floor in \(W\).
    5. Data openness for operations. LTA's DataMall publishes real-time bus arrival, traffic speed, ERP rates and other feeds.

    Transferability is limited. The model relies on a single tier of government, extensive state landownership, compulsory acquisition powers and limited public contestation of plans. Few other cities can copy it wholesale. (Qualitative judgment.)

    7. Case studies

    7.1 From paper licenses to satellites: the evolution of road pricing A E

    YearStepNote
    1975 (June)Area Licensing Scheme (ALS)Paper licenses to enter a Restricted Zone in the Central Area during morning peak hours; car pools of four or more exempt; park-and-ride from fringe parking lots. Vehicles entering the zone in restricted hours fell by 73% between March and September 1975 (CLC 2018).
    1990Vehicle Quota System / COEStock control added to flow pricing (1 May 1990).
    1998Electronic Road Pricing (ERP)In-vehicle units and gantries replace paper licenses. Charges vary by location, half-hour and vehicle type.
    2018Zero vehicle growthCars and motorcycles 0% per year from February 2018.
    2023–2026ERP 2 on-board unitsInstallation from November 2023. Charging continues at existing gantries during the transition.
    2027ERP 2 liveFrom 1 January 2027 charging uses satellite positioning at 33 charging locations marked by road signs and markings. All 95 gantries (62 not in use) are to be removed or modified by June 2027 (LTA, 28 September 2026).

    ERP 2 makes distance-based charging technically possible. In 2024 the Minister for Transport described it as an option "in future" and said it could allow a one-off increase in the vehicle population while keeping congestion in check (MOT, 8 May 2024). No date has been set. That remark is analytically important. It is the government saying that a better price on use could relax the quantity limit on ownership. This is the price-versus-quantity trade-off of Module 13 stated as policy.

    One honest caveat. Cordon and point charges move congestion in time and space. Studies of ERP report peak spreading and diversion to uncharged arterials, which is why the charge has had to follow the congestion around the network for decades.

    7.2 Holding the fleet at zero growth constraints

    The vehicle growth rate was cut in steps from 3% to 0.25% and finally to 0% for cars and motorcycles from February 2018. LTA's quota annex for the November 2026 – January 2027 bidding exercises confirms that this rate will apply until 31 January 2028, while goods vehicles and buses keep 0.25%. In October 2024 the government also announced a one-off injection of up to about 20,000 additional COEs over several years. Exercise: use the shadow-price logic of Module 1 to explain why an injection of quota would be expected to lower the COE premium without changing the long-run cap.

    7.3 Punggol and Sengkang: integrated new towns, and a forecasting lesson A E

    Sengkang and Punggol were planned from the outset with the North East Line and a local LRT loop. According to CLC, most Punggol residents live within 200–400 m of an LRT station. Punggol was later designated HDB's first "eco-town". The case shows both strengths and risks of the model. Access by design is excellent once the town is built. But the rail line was committed before demand existed, and when housing slowed after 1997, ridership and operator finances lagged for years. In \(W\) terms, a plan with a high long-run Term A can carry a large early Term E deficit. The decision rule must look across the full appraisal period and the risk of delay (see Module 5 and Module 14).

    7.4 Tengah: a "car-lite forest town" D B

    Tengah, HDB's 24th town in western Singapore, is planned for about 42,000 homes. It has the first "car-free" HDB town center: roads, parking and service traffic run below ground, freeing the surface for a park and a 100 m wide, 5 km long "Forest Corridor" for walking and cycling. Most bus stops are planned within 300 m of homes, and the town is served by the Jurong Region Line (CLC 2018; LTMP 2040). It moves land from Term D, surface road and parking space, to public space and active travel, Terms A and B, at higher construction cost, Term E. Critics note that cars are moved underground, not removed, so the town is "car-lite" rather than car-free.

    8. Where to study

    Singapore's working language of government and engineering is English, so primary documents are accessible without translation.

    Degree programs and research groups

    • National University of Singapore (NUS), Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering. Transportation engineering is one of the department's research and teaching areas. Graduate study is possible through the MEng (Civil and Environmental Engineering) and coursework MSc programs, and NUS also offers an interdisciplinary master's in transportation systems and management. Check the current catalog, as program names change.
    • Nanyang Technological University (NTU), School of Civil and Environmental Engineering. Transport and infrastructure research, including work with LTA.
    • LTA Academy, LTA's capability-building arm, founded in 2006. It runs executive courses for foreign officials, including the World Bank–ADB Leaders in Urban Transport Planning program (2020 brochure). These are fee-paying, mainly for officials.
    • Centre for Liveable Cities (CLC), a government think tank whose Urban Systems Studies are the most useful free histories of how decisions were taken.

    Free teaching material

    • CLC, Integrating Land Use & Mobility: Supporting Sustainable Growth (2018), full PDF. A book-length case history, with interviews of former planners, of the 1971 Concept Plan, the ALS, the MRT decision, new towns, the NEL and Tengah. It is the best single reading for this page. The earlier volume in the same series, Transport: Overcoming Constraints, Sustaining Mobility (CLC and LTA, 2013), is widely held in libraries, but we did not find a stable free online copy.
    • LTA, LTMP 2040 report (PDF), the primary statement of objectives and targets.
    • LTA TIA Guidelines and simulation addendum (linked above), practitioner documents that show how a developer's transport assessment is done in Singapore.
    • Parliamentary replies on mot.gov.sg. The main public source of reasoning and data on COE, ERP and mode share. Use them as primary sources.

    9. Key passages from official documents

    Because Singapore's sources are in English, no translation is needed. The passages below are the short texts that best summarize the objective. Read them with the \(W\) terms in mind.

    Key passage 1 · LTMP 2040 (LTA, 2019), the access objective

    "We want a Singapore that is a 45-Minute City with 20-Minute Towns in 2040. All journeys to the nearest neighbourhood centre using Walk-Cycle-Ride modes of transport will take less than 20 minutes."

    The plan continues: 9 in 10 peak-period Walk-Cycle-Ride journeys are to take less than 45 minutes.

    Reading: The objective is stated in travel time to opportunities by non-car modes, an accessibility floor on Term A for every town. Private cars are deliberately excluded from the measured set.

    Key passage 2 · CLC, Integrating Land Use & Mobility (2018), land as the constraint

    "With 12% of Singapore's land space already dedicated to roads—a hefty proportion compared with 14% for housing—subsequent increases in road space needed to be made in a controlled and sustainable manner, with viable alternatives to driving being provided."

    Reading: Term D is stated as a comparison with housing, the most valuable alternative land use. Road capacity is not ruled out, but every expansion must be paired with alternatives, an implicit rejection of the fixed-demand logic of \(\min\sum q\,t\).

    Key passage 3 · Ministry of Transport, parliamentary response on "COE 2.0" (22 September 2025)

    "We do not have enough land for every person, or even every household, to own a car."

    Reading: The quantity limit justified directly by land, not by congestion or emissions. The same speech calls the COE "neither perfect nor popular", a frank statement of the equity cost.

    Discussion questions
    1. The COE premium is a market-revealed shadow price on the vehicle cap. Under what conditions would it equal the marginal cost an extra car imposes on others (delay, crashes, emissions, land), so that the cap is optimal? What evidence would you need?
    2. If ERP 2 introduced distance-based charging that fully priced the delay each driver imposes on others, should the COE cap be relaxed? Use the price-versus-quantity reasoning of Module 13, and consider uncertainty about demand.
    3. LTMP 2040 measures access only by Walk-Cycle-Ride modes. Is it legitimate for a welfare objective to ignore the access that private cars provide? How would including it change the 45-minute-city indicator?
    4. Singapore does not publish project appraisals. Which parts of \(W\) can an outside analyst still estimate from public data (DataMall, parliamentary replies, the Master Plan), and which cannot be estimated? What would you ask the government to publish first?
    5. Road deaths rose in 2024 despite low car ownership. What does this suggest about the relationship between mode share and the safety limit \(F(\theta)\le\bar F\)?
    6. Which elements of the Singapore model could a European or US city with fragmented landownership and elected local government adopt, and which could it not? Sort them by whether they depend on objectives, constraints, models or institutions.
