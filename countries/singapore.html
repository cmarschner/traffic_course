    Role model country

    Singapore

    City-state · Reading of \(W\): maximize access subject to a binding land constraint · Key terms: A C D E constraints

    Singapore is the clearest case in this course of a society that treats land as the binding constraint on transport. It has priced road congestion since 1975, has capped the number of vehicles by auction since 1990, and has held growth of the car fleet at zero since 2018. It plans housing, jobs and rail together under a single government. The result is a high share of public transport and short commutes for a city of six million. The costs are high car prices, a rationing system many residents find unfair, and a planning model that depends on a degree of state control few other democracies have.

    What to take from this page
    • How Singapore's instruments map onto \(W\): ERP as a price on external congestion (Term C), the Certificate of Entitlement (COE) as a quantity constraint whose market price is its shadow price, and land as Term D and a hard constraint.
    • Why the COE premium can be read as the Lagrange multiplier on the vehicle constraint, and what it says about the value of road space.
    • The institutional set-up: MOT, LTA, URA and HDB, and how integrated land-use and transport planning works in practice.
    • Where the model is weak: equity of rationing by price, land-use and transport timing risk, and road safety for motorcyclists and older pedestrians.

    2. Singapore's reading of \(W\)

    The course framework in Module 1 summarizes Singapore as: maximise access subject to a binding land constraint; internalise congestion directly by price and quantity control. This section shows where that reading comes from.

    Land as a hard constraint and a high price D constraints

    The core argument appears in nearly every official document. When the Ministry of Transport defended the COE system in Parliament in September 2025, its reasoning was about land, not congestion: roads already take up 12% of the island, and more roads would mean less land for housing, schools and hospitals. In the notation of the course, Singapore sets \(r\), the opportunity cost of land, very high. It also treats total road and parking land as close to fixed:

    $$\max_\theta\; W(\theta)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad L_{\text{roads}}(\theta)+L_{\text{parking}}(\theta)\le \bar L .$$

    If road space cannot grow, the number of vehicles using it has to be managed. Singapore does this with two instruments that correspond neatly to the two halves of the m01 box "Constraint or price?".

    Quantity: the COE as a constraint with a market-revealed shadow price constraints

    Under the Vehicle Quota System (VQS), introduced on 1 May 1990, every new vehicle needs a Certificate of Entitlement, valid for ten years. The quota for each quarter is set from the number of vehicles deregistered plus an allowed growth rate. Since February 2018 that growth rate has been 0% for cars and motorcycles, so the car fleet is in effect capped: one car out, one car in. COEs are sold in sealed-bid, uniform-price auctions. Bidding takes place twice a month, and every successful bidder in a category pays the same "quota premium" (LTA OneMotoring).

    This is the clearest real-world example of a constraint whose shadow price you can observe. Write the vehicle cap as \(N(\theta)\le \bar N\). Its Lagrange multiplier is the marginal value of one more vehicle license, and the auction reveals it every fortnight as the COE premium. Premiums for cars have at times exceeded S$100,000. Wikipedia's compilation of LTA bidding results lists about S$150,000 for Category B in October 2023.

    Worked example: annualising the shadow price

    Suppose a car COE clears at S$100,000 for ten years and the buyer's discount rate is 5%. The annuity factor is \(\big(1-1.05^{-10}\big)/0.05 \approx 7.72\), so the annual shadow price of the right to keep one car on the road is about \(100{,}000/7.72\approx\) S$13,000, or roughly S$35 per day, before any fuel, parking or ERP charges.

    Two interpretations follow. First, this is the private value of car ownership to the marginal bidder, and it puts an upper bound on what society forgoes by holding \(\bar N\) fixed. Second, it is not a measure of the external cost of a car. The cap is set by land and congestion policy, and the price adjusts to whatever demand is. If the cap were loosened, the premium would fall, and congestion and land take, priced in Terms C and D, would rise. The welfare question is whether the marginal \(\lambda\) (the premium) exceeds the marginal external and land cost of an extra car. Singapore has answered it administratively, not by appraisal.

    Price: ERP as a Pigouvian charge on external congestion C

    Electronic Road Pricing charges vehicles for using congested roads at congested times. LTA's stated rule is to adjust charges "to keep traffic moving at an optimal speed" (OneMotoring): rates are reviewed periodically and raised where measured speeds fall below a target band, lowered where they rise above it. The charge varies by vehicle type and, in peak hours, by half-hour. This is a speed-targeting rule, not a computed marginal external cost. As Module 13 shows, for a link with a known speed–flow function, holding speed near the top of the stable range approximates the optimal Pigouvian toll. Revenue \(R\) enters Term E as a transfer.

    Access as the objective, with an equity floor A constraints

    The positive objective in the current plan, the Land Transport Master Plan 2040 (LTMP 2040, published 2019), is phrased in travel time to opportunities, not in vehicle speed: "20-minute towns and a 45-minute city". Formally these are two accessibility floors applied to every town:

    $$\Pr\big[t_{\text{home}\to\text{nearest center}}^{\text{WCR}}\le 20\text{ min}\big]\approx 1,\qquad \Pr\big[t_{\text{peak}}^{\text{WCR}}\le 45\text{ min}\big]\ge 0.9 .$$

    These are versions of the equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\). The baseline in the plan was that about 9 in 10 WCR trips to the nearest neighborhood center already took under 20 minutes, but only about two-thirds of peak-period WCR journeys took under 45 minutes. LTMP 2040 also states that public transport journey times rose between 2008 and 2012 because average trip distances grew as new housing moved further from the center. This is the planners' own admission that Term A depends on the location of homes and jobs, not just on network speed. The plan's response, "bringing jobs closer to homes", is a land-use lever. It does not mention road capacity.

    Health and externalities B C

    LTMP 2040 adds a "Healthy Lives, Safer Journeys" strand: more space for public transport, active mobility and community uses, a cycling-path network growing from more than 440 km (2019) to more than 1,000 km by 2040, cleaner-energy bus and taxi fleets by 2040, and a "Vision Zero" aspiration for road deaths. Unlike Sweden, Singapore does not define Vision Zero as a binding constraint. It is stated as an aspiration alongside education and enforcement.

    3. Institutions and standards

    Singapore's institutions are unusually consolidated. One national government is also the city government, the main landowner and, through HDB, the builder of most homes.

    • Ministry of Transport (MOT) sets policy and answers to Parliament. Major decisions on COE, ERP and fares appear as ministerial statements and parliamentary replies, which are the best primary sources for policy reasoning.
    • Land Transport Authority (LTA), formed in 1995 by merging several road, rail and vehicle agencies, plans, builds and regulates the whole land transport system: roads, rail, buses, vehicle registration, COE and ERP. Since the Bus Contracting Model (2016) the government owns the bus fleet and depots and tenders route packages to operators.
    • Urban Redevelopment Authority (URA) prepares the long-range Concept Plan (now the Long-Term Plan Review) and the statutory Master Plan. Rail alignments and stations are safeguarded in these plans decades in advance.
    • Housing & Development Board (HDB) plans and builds the new towns. Their structure (town center, neighborhood centers, MRT/LRT stations, bus interchanges) is the physical form of Term A.
    • Public Transport Council regulates fares; the Traffic Police publish road-safety statistics.
    • Household Travel Survey (historically HITS, the Household Interview Travel Survey). LTA's large household survey is the basis of mode-share statistics and of demand models. Results are released selectively, often through parliamentary replies, not as a full public dataset.

    Technical guidance

    • LTMP 2040, the strategic plan. Its predecessors are LTMP 2008 and LTMP 2013.
    • Transport Impact Assessment (TIA) Guidelines for Developments (2019 edition), with the Walking and Cycling Plan (WCP) requirements. Developers must assess pedestrians and cyclists as well as traffic. An addendum (2020) sets requirements for microsimulation, calibration and network-level assessment, relevant to Module 10.
    • Design standards. LTA's Code of Practice and street-design guidelines; Singapore borrows heavily from British and Australian practice for junction and capacity analysis.
    • Appraisal. Singapore does not publish a national cost–benefit manual comparable to the US BCA Guidance, Germany's BVWP method or the UK's TAG, and project-level appraisals are generally not public. For a course centered on \(W\) this is the biggest gap. The objective is visible in targets and instruments, not in a published appraisal.

    4. Signature ideas and their weaknesses

    What the world can learn

    1. Price congestion where it occurs. Singapore's Area Licensing Scheme (1975) was the first urban congestion charge in the world. London (2003), Stockholm (2006) and New York (2025) followed decades later. The lesson is less the technology than the political continuity: charges have been adjusted for fifty years.
    2. Separate ownership from use, and price both. The COE limits the stock of vehicles. ERP prices the flow. Fuel and parking charges add a variable cost. This combination is unique in the world.
    3. Plan land use and transport as one system. New towns are designed around rail stations, with the town center, bus interchange and MRT station co-located. The CLC study of the first 50 years of integration attributes Singapore's avoidance of sprawl to this co-ordination.
    4. Targets in the right units. "20-minute towns, 45-minute city" measures access by all modes, not vehicle speed, and is close to an accessibility floor in \(W\).
    5. Data openness for operations. LTA's DataMall publishes real-time bus arrival, traffic speed, ERP rates and other feeds.

    Documented weaknesses and criticisms

    1. Equity of rationing by price. The COE allocates cars to those with the highest willingness to pay, which tracks income. When COE prices reached record highs, academics and opposition politicians proposed needs-based allocation. The government acknowledges the system is "neither perfect nor popular" but argues that needs-based rules would be "subjective, divisive" and would benefit few people (MOT, 22 September 2025). In \(W\) terms, the COE is efficient for \(\omega_g=1\) but has distributional consequences that are addressed, if at all, through spending, not the instrument.
    2. Revenue is not hypothecated. According to MOT, COE revenue averages about S$4–6 billion a year and goes into general revenue. Operating subsidies for bus and rail are about S$2 billion a year. The link between the charge and transport improvement is political, not legal, unlike the London or New York schemes.
    3. Land-use and transport timing risk. Integrated planning can fail when demand forecasts do. The North East Line opened in 2003 with fewer than 150,000 daily passengers against forecasts of 200,000–250,000, because housing in Sengkang and Punggol was scaled back after the 1997 Asian financial crisis. Buangkok station stayed closed until 2006 because it had too few homes within 400 m (CLC 2018).
    4. Road safety for vulnerable groups. Singapore's fatality rate (2.35 per 100,000 in 2024) is higher than that of the Netherlands or Sweden and rose from 2.30 in 2023. Motorcyclists account for about 60% of deaths, and older people are overrepresented among pedestrian fatalities. The data contradict any idea that low car ownership by itself brings Vision Zero.
    5. Technology and delivery risk. ERP 2, the satellite-based successor contracted in 2016, was delayed several times, partly by the global microchip shortage. On-board unit installation began only in November 2023, and gantry-based charging ends on 31 December 2026. Its satellite positioning also raises privacy questions that a gantry system did not.
    6. Transferability. The model relies on a single tier of government, extensive state landownership (the government and statutory boards already owned about 60% of land in the early 1970s, according to CLC), compulsory acquisition powers and limited public contestation of plans. Few other cities can copy it wholesale. (Qualitative judgement.)

    5. Case studies

    5.1 From paper licenses to satellites: the evolution of road pricing C

    YearStepNote
    1975 (June)Area Licensing Scheme (ALS)Paper licenses to enter a Restricted Zone in the Central Area during morning peak hours; car pools of four or more exempt; park-and-ride from fringe parking lots. Vehicles entering the zone in restricted hours fell by 73% between March and September 1975 (CLC 2018).
    1990Vehicle Quota System / COEStock control added to flow pricing (1 May 1990).
    1998Electronic Road Pricing (ERP)In-vehicle units and gantries replace paper licenses. Charges vary by location, half-hour and vehicle type.
    2018Zero vehicle growthCars and motorcycles 0% per year from February 2018.
    2023–2026ERP 2 on-board unitsInstallation from November 2023. Charging continues at existing gantries during the transition.
    2027ERP 2 liveFrom 1 January 2027 charging uses satellite positioning at 33 charging locations marked by road signs and markings. All 95 gantries (62 not in use) are to be removed or modified by June 2027 (LTA, 28 September 2026).

    ERP 2 makes distance-based charging technically possible. In 2024 the Minister for Transport described it as an option "in future" and said it could allow a one-off increase in the vehicle population while keeping congestion in check (MOT, 8 May 2024). No date has been set. That remark is analytically important. It is the government saying that a better price on use (Term C) could relax the quantity constraint on ownership. This is the price-versus-quantity trade-off of Module 13 stated as policy.

    One honest caveat. Cordon and point charges move congestion in time and space. Studies of ERP report peak spreading and diversion to uncharged arterials, which is why the charge has had to follow the congestion around the network for decades.

    5.2 Holding the fleet at zero growth constraints

    The vehicle growth rate was cut in steps from 3% to 0.25% and finally to 0% for cars and motorcycles from February 2018. LTA's quota annex for the November 2026 – January 2027 bidding exercises confirms that this rate will apply until 31 January 2028, while goods vehicles and buses keep 0.25%. In October 2024 the government also announced a one-off injection of up to about 20,000 additional COEs over several years. Exercise: use the m01 constraint logic to explain why an injection of quota would be expected to lower the COE premium without changing the long-run cap.

    5.3 Punggol and Sengkang: integrated new towns, and a forecasting lesson A E

    Sengkang and Punggol were planned from the outset with the North East Line and a local LRT loop. According to CLC, most Punggol residents live within 200–400 m of an LRT station. Punggol was later designated HDB's first "eco-town". The case shows both strengths and risks of the model. Access by design is excellent once the town is built. But the rail line was committed before demand existed, and when housing slowed after 1997, ridership and operator finances lagged for years. In \(W\) terms, a plan with a high long-run Term A can carry a large early Term E deficit. The decision rule must look across the full appraisal period and the risk of delay (see Module 5 and Module 14).

    5.4 Tengah: a "car-lite forest town" D B

    Tengah, HDB's 24th town in western Singapore, is planned for about 42,000 homes. It has the first "car-free" HDB town center: roads, parking and service traffic run below ground, freeing the surface for a park and a 100 m wide, 5 km long "Forest Corridor" for walking and cycling. Most bus stops are planned within 300 m of homes, and the town is served by the Jurong Region Line (CLC 2018; LTMP 2040). It moves land from Term D, surface road and parking space, to public space and active travel, Terms A and B, at higher construction cost, Term E. Critics note that cars are moved underground, not removed, so the town is "car-lite" rather than car-free.

    6. Where to study

    Singapore's working language of government and engineering is English, so primary documents are accessible without translation.

    Degree programs and research groups

    • National University of Singapore (NUS), Department of Civil and Environmental Engineering. Transportation engineering is one of the department's research and teaching areas. Graduate study is possible through the MEng (Civil and Environmental Engineering) and coursework MSc programs, and NUS also offers an interdisciplinary master's in transportation systems and management. Check the current catalog, as program names change.
    • Nanyang Technological University (NTU), School of Civil and Environmental Engineering. Transport and infrastructure research, including work with LTA.
    • LTA Academy, LTA's capability-building arm, founded in 2006. It runs executive courses for foreign officials, including the World Bank–ADB Leaders in Urban Transport Planning program (2020 brochure). These are fee-paying, mainly for officials.
    • Centre for Liveable Cities (CLC), a government think tank whose Urban Systems Studies are the most useful free histories of how decisions were taken.

    Free teaching material

    • CLC, Integrating Land Use & Mobility: Supporting Sustainable Growth (2018), full PDF. A book-length case history, with interviews of former planners, of the 1971 Concept Plan, the ALS, the MRT decision, new towns, the NEL and Tengah. It is the best single reading for this page. The earlier volume in the same series, Transport: Overcoming Constraints, Sustaining Mobility (CLC and LTA, 2013), is widely held in libraries, but we did not find a stable free online copy.
    • LTA, LTMP 2040 report (PDF), the primary statement of objectives and targets.
    • LTA TIA Guidelines and simulation addendum (linked above), practitioner documents that show how a developer's transport assessment is done in Singapore.
    • Parliamentary replies on mot.gov.sg. For Singapore, these short texts are the main public source of reasoning and data on COE, ERP and mode share. Use them as primary sources.

    7. Key passages from official documents

    Because Singapore's sources are in English, no translation is needed. The passages below are the short texts that best summarize the objective. Read them with the \(W\) terms in mind.

    Key passage 1 · LTMP 2040 (LTA, 2019), the access objective

    "We want a Singapore that is a 45-Minute City with 20-Minute Towns in 2040. All journeys to the nearest neighbourhood centre using Walk-Cycle-Ride modes of transport will take less than 20 minutes."

    The plan continues: 9 in 10 peak-period Walk-Cycle-Ride journeys are to take less than 45 minutes.

    Reading: The objective is stated in travel time to opportunities by non-car modes, an accessibility floor on Term A for every town. Private cars are deliberately excluded from the measured set.

    Key passage 2 · CLC, Integrating Land Use & Mobility (2018), land as the constraint

    "With 12% of Singapore's land space already dedicated to roads—a hefty proportion compared with 14% for housing—subsequent increases in road space needed to be made in a controlled and sustainable manner, with viable alternatives to driving being provided."

    Reading: Term D is stated as a comparison with housing, the most valuable alternative land use. Road capacity is not ruled out, but every expansion must be paired with alternatives, an implicit rejection of the fixed-demand logic of \(\min\sum q\,t\).

    Key passage 3 · Ministry of Transport, parliamentary response on "COE 2.0" (22 September 2025)

    "We do not have enough land for every person, or even every household, to own a car."

    Reading: The quantity constraint justified directly by land, not by congestion or emissions. The same speech calls the COE "neither perfect nor popular", a frank statement of the equity cost.

    Discussion questions
    1. The COE premium is a market-revealed shadow price on the vehicle cap. Under what conditions would it equal the marginal external plus land cost of an extra car, so that the cap is optimal? What evidence would you need?
    2. If ERP 2 introduced distance-based charging that fully internalized congestion, should the COE cap be relaxed? Use the price-versus-quantity reasoning of Module 13, and consider uncertainty about demand.
    3. LTMP 2040 measures access only by Walk-Cycle-Ride modes. Is it legitimate for a welfare objective to ignore the access that private cars provide? How would including it change the 45-minute-city indicator?
    4. Singapore does not publish project appraisals. Which parts of \(W\) can an outside analyst still estimate from public data (DataMall, parliamentary replies, the Master Plan), and which cannot be estimated?
    5. Road deaths rose in 2024 (and, by press reports, again in 2025) despite low car ownership. What does this suggest about the relationship between mode share and the safety constraint \(F(\theta)\le\bar F\)?
    6. Which elements of the Singapore model could a European or US city with fragmented landownership and elected local government adopt, and which could it not?
