    Country study

    France: statutory appraisal, a rising carbon value and passive design

    Key terms: C E A constraints · Related modules: 3, 6, 9, 12, 13, 14

    France has some of the most ambitious transport appraisal on paper. Its engineers invented consumer surplus in 1844. Its law has required cost–benefit evaluation of large projects since 1982. Its official carbon value is among the highest in the world. Yet transport emissions in 2024 were the same as in 1990, the car carries almost two-thirds of all trips, and a carbon tax set far below the official value still triggered the largest protest movement in decades. Meanwhile French cities rebuilt their streets around tramways and 30 km/h limits. This page separates four layers (stated objectives, appraisal, design standards and outcomes) and asks why, in France, they so often point in different directions.

    In brief
    The question
    Why does a country with statutory appraisal and a high official carbon value still build projects with low returns and keep its carbon tax frozen?
    Stated objectives
    A right to mobility for everyone (2019 mobility law). Carbon budgets that require transport emissions to fall by roughly a third within a decade. Fewer road deaths.
    How projects are appraised
    Socio-economic evaluation by law since 1982, with an independent counter-appraisal for large projects since 2012. An official carbon value derived from the national climate target, about €256 per tonne in 2025 and rising.
    Design standards
    Street categories written into the Highway Code (zone 30, meeting zone at 20 km/h with pedestrian priority). Tens of thousands of roundabouts. 30 km/h as the default in many large cities.
    What gets built
    Tramways in dozens of cities, funded by a payroll levy on employers. A large high-speed rail network that the national audit court called "a model pushed beyond its relevance". Transport emissions of 125 Mt CO₂e in 2024, the same as in 1990.
    Lessons for a decision maker
    A price used in appraisal does not change behavior; only a price people pay does. Dedicated local funding can matter more than national guidance. Ask your analysts: which carbon price did the forecast assume travelers face, and which did the appraisal use to value the result?
    Learning outcomes
    • Separate the four layers of French practice (stated objectives, appraisal, design standards, outcomes) and name a primary source for each.
    • Explain the French appraisal framework and the valeur de l'action pour le climat (the Quinet reports), and why an appraisal price differs from a tax.
    • Describe the institutions (CEREMA, DGITM, Université Gustave Eiffel, mobility authorities) and the legal frame (LOM 2019, plans de mobilité, versement mobilité).
    • Read the legal definitions of zone de rencontre and zone 30 and explain why they are passive instruments.
    • Use the French case to answer the course's central question: why competent planners facing the same problem produce different solutions.

    2. Four layers: objectives, appraisal, standards, outcomes

    A country does not have one transport philosophy. It has laws and plans that state objectives. It has procedures that decide which projects pass. It has standards that decide what a project looks like. And it has budgets and politics that decide what is built. In France:

    Stated objectives

    Access for all. French transport law has long spoken of a droit au transport, now a droit à la mobilité: a right of access for every user. In this course it works as an equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\). The 2019 mobility law (Loi n° 2019-1428 d'orientation des mobilités, LOM) set out to cover the whole territory with mobility authorities, including rural areas that previously had none. It lists reducing territorial inequality among its four objectives.

    Climate. France's national low-carbon strategy sets carbon budgets, ceilings on average yearly emissions for successive five-year periods. For transport, the ministry's figures show 125 Mt CO₂e emitted in 2024 and budgets that fall to about 86 Mt a year for 2029–2033 (Ministry, SNBC). That is a cut of roughly a third in under a decade.

    Safety. Road safety is a national priority with its own observatory (ONISR) and interministerial committees, but France has not adopted a Vision Zero-style limit in law.

    How projects are appraised

    Appraisal by law. Since the 1982 transport framework law (LOTI), large transport infrastructure projects must undergo a socio-economic evaluation. The ministry's evaluation framework (an instruction of 2014 with technical fiches-outils) sets out values of time, crash values, environmental values and the discount rate. Since a 2012 law, projects above a public-funding threshold also require an independent counter-appraisal (contre-expertise), organized by the government's investment secretariat. France also applies a premium for the opportunity cost of public funds, which corresponds to \(\mu\) in Term E.

    A carbon value from a budget. Most countries value a tonne of CO₂ by the damage it causes. France derives its value from the national target instead, as Module 1 explains: if emissions must stay under a budget, the cost of the last tonne needed to meet it is the shadow price of the budget. The 2019 Quinet commission (France Stratégie, La valeur de l'action pour le climat, February 2019) recalibrated the value for the net-zero target:

That distinction is the key to section 3.

    Under the hoodWhen the appraisal price and the price travelers pay differ

    An appraisal has two parts that use prices in different ways. The forecast predicts behavior: how many people drive, take the train or stay home. It must use the prices people actually pay, because those drive their choices. The valuation turns the predicted effects into money. For carbon, France values each tonne at the Quinet value \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\).

    Suppose the carbon component of fuel tax stays at €44.60 per tonne while the appraisal values CO₂ at €250. The forecast then predicts how people behave under weak carbon pricing, which means more driving than in a world that meets the budget. The valuation charges that driving at a high price. Both steps are correct on their own. Together they describe a world that misses the target, and they value the miss at the price of meeting it.

    Two consequences follow. First, a project that cuts emissions looks very good, because each avoided tonne is worth €250. Second, the appraisal no longer tells you whether the national budget \(\bar E_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) will be met; it only ranks projects within a world that may not meet it. A consistent alternative is to forecast with the policies that would meet the budget (for example a higher carbon price or regulation), and then value emissions at the resulting shadow price. In the notation of Module 1, the first approach treats \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) as a fixed weight in Term C; the second treats \(E_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\le\bar E_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) as a limit and lets the price follow from it.

    Design standards

    French practice prefers passive instruments: designs that make the safe behavior the easy one, without enforcement technology or active control. Three examples:

    • Street categories in the Highway Code. Since the 2008 reforms known as the code de la rue, the law defines the zone 30, the zone de rencontre (meeting zone: 20 km/h, pedestrian priority) and the aire piétonne (pedestrian area). Each ties a speed limit to a coherent street design (translations in section 9).
    • Roundabouts. France has built very large numbers since the 1980s, supported by national capacity tools such as CEREMA's GIRABASE (Module 9).
    • 30 km/h cities. The mayor sets the limit by order. Grenoble moved from 2016, Paris in 2021 and Lyon in 2022, with CEREMA supplying the design and evaluation guidance (section 7).

    These standards reduce crash severity and noise in Term C at low cost in Term E. They are mostly local choices made within a national legal menu.

    3. Where the layers diverge, and why

    Appraisal prices are not the prices people pay

    France's appraisal values carbon at a price consistent with its climate target. The price drivers actually pay through the carbon tax is a fraction of it. A price used only in appraisal can change which projects are chosen. It cannot change how millions of people drive every day. That is one reason transport emissions in 2024 were back at their 1990 level despite the high official value. The gap is not an oversight. When the government tried to close part of it in 2018, it met the gilets jaunes (section 6).

    Political power: projects chosen for other reasons

    Statutory appraisal requires that the evaluation be done and published. It does not require that the project with the best result be chosen. The Cour des comptes summed up its 2014 review of high-speed rail in its title: a model "pushed beyond its relevance". It asked for stronger guarantees of relevance and profitability before any new line is chosen. Promises of national integration, such as the 1995 aim of a motorway or high-speed rail station within reach of every part of the country (Module 1), carry weight that the appraisal does not capture.

    Funding rules: a local tax base built the tramways

    The versement mobilité is a payroll levy on employers with at least 11 employees in the area of a mobility authority. It was created in the Paris region in 1971 and extended to other cities from 1973. The rate is set locally within ceilings fixed by law. It gave French cities a reliable source of money that only public transport could spend. That rule, more than any national guidance, explains why medium-sized French cities could afford tramways that comparable cities elsewhere could not. It also explains a gap: rural areas without such a tax base have had little to spend on alternatives to the car.

    Institutions: who sets the speed limit

    In 2018 the national government cut the limit on two-lane rural roads from 90 to 80 km/h. It was the right measure for Term C on France's deadliest roads. But it was imposed from Paris on roads managed by departments. It became a symbol of rural grievance, and from 2019 departments were allowed to restore 90 km/h on some roads, which many did. Urban 30 km/h limits, set by mayors, met far less resistance. The same safety logic succeeded or failed depending on who decided.

    History: the engineer-economist tradition

    The appraisal tradition comes from the state engineering corps of the Ponts et Chaussées. Jules Dupuit, one of its engineers, asked in 1844 how to measure the usefulness of public works and in doing so invented consumer surplus (Module 1). The tradition gave France strong national appraisal. It came from a centralized state, while many of the most visible changes since the 1980s, from tramways to Ville 30, were made by cities.

    The same problem, here

    Take a problem every country on this site faces. A suburb 15 km from the city center is growing. Its one radial road into the city is jammed every morning. What would competent French planners typically propose, and why? This is a stylized judgment, not a documented case.

    • The likely answer: the city region's mobility authority extends a tram line or a high-quality bus line toward the suburb, with park-and-ride at the edge and protected cycle tracks. If the road is a national road, the state may also study a widening, appraised with the official carbon value.
    • Objectives: a legal right to mobility for all, and carbon budgets that require transport emissions to fall by about a third within a decade.
    • Constraints: the local payroll levy has a ceiling set by law; political consent for restricting cars is weak outside the cities.
    • Models: the appraisal values CO₂ at a high official price, while the traffic forecast assumes the fuel prices people pay today, so car traffic keeps growing in the forecast.
    • Institutions: the mobility authority has its own tax base that only public transport can spend; roads belong to the state or the departments, with separate budgets.

    In France, a dedicated local funding rule explains more of the urban answer than national guidance does. Compare the United States, Germany, Japan, Singapore, the Netherlands and Sweden.

    4. Reading the choices in \(W\)

    Module 1 summarizes the French reading as "statutory socio-economic appraisal with a high, rising carbon value; behavior shaped passively through geometry rather than technology". The four layers refine this:

    • Term C, carbon C constraints. In appraisal, \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) is the shadow price of the carbon budget. In behavior, travelers face a much lower price, so the budget does not bind on choices.
    • Term E E. Appraisal is required by law and includes \(\mu\). The versement mobilité funds \(K-R\) for public transport from a tax base linked to employers, who capture part of the access benefits.
    • Term A and the equity floor A. The right to mobility is stated in law. Outside the cities, Term A for most households is almost entirely car-based, which makes fuel prices an equity question.
    • Term C, crashes and noise C. Attacked mainly through passive design: street categories, roundabouts and lower urban limits.

    The versement mobilité is worth a moment. It is a tax on employment, not on travel. So it does not change the cost of any trip and does not correct any external cost. Its justification is that employers capture part of the access benefits (Term A) that public transport creates, through larger labor markets. In welfare terms it funds \(K-R\) from a tax base linked to the beneficiaries. Whether it distorts less than general taxation, and so has a lower \(\mu\), is an open question.

    6. Successes and documented failures

    What works

    1. Carbon value from a budget. Deriving \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) from the national limit makes the appraisal value consistent with the target by construction.
    2. Appraisal as law, with independent counter-appraisal of large projects.
    3. The tramway renaissance. Since Nantes (1985), Grenoble (1987) and Strasbourg (1994), French cities have built tramways as tools of urban redesign, rebuilding streets from façade to façade rather than just laying tracks.
    4. Street categories in the Highway Code and the 30 km/h city.
    5. Roundabouts as passive control. They replace signals with a geometry that forces low speeds and removes the most dangerous conflicts.
    The other France
    • Periurban car dependence. Decades of low-density housing growth around cities, out-of-town retail and the closure of local services have left many households with no realistic alternative to the car. For them Term A is almost entirely car-based, and higher fuel prices are a direct loss of access.
    • The gilets jaunes and the carbon tax. France's carbon component in fuel taxes (contribution climat-énergie) was planned to rise steeply. The scheduled increase for 2019 helped trigger the gilets jaunes protests from November 2018. The government canceled the increase, and the rate has been frozen since at €44.60 per tonne, far below the appraisal values above. The lesson for Module 13: a price that is efficient in Term C can violate a felt equity floor, and losing political consent can cost more than the welfare gain.
    • The 80 km/h controversy. See section 3. A Vision-Zero-type measure aimed at France's most dangerous roads was partly reversed within two years.
    • Low-emission zones under pressure. The LOM and the 2021 Climate and Resilience Law required zones à faibles émissions (ZFE) in larger cities. They were criticized as penalizing lower-income car owners who cannot afford newer vehicles, and in 2025 Parliament voted to scrap the ZFE obligation. Check the ministry's ZFE page for the current legal position.
    • Road safety lags its neighbors. At about 4.8 deaths per 100,000, France is well behind Sweden, the Netherlands and Germany, and the rural road network accounts for most deaths.
    • Paris is not France. The Paris figures in section 1 reflect one of the densest, best-served cities in the world. Exporting "the Paris model" to small towns without the transit network misreads what produced it.

    7. Case studies

    Paris: Plan Vélo, Rue de Rivoli, 30 km/h and parking prices

    Paris has transformed its streets in about fifteen years. Key steps:

    • The banks of the Seine were closed to through traffic on the right bank in 2016 and turned into a park and promenade. The decision went through years of litigation.
    • Plan Vélo. Two successive plans (2015–2020 and 2021–2026) built protected cycle tracks on main axes. The diagnostic of the city's mobility plan reports more than 1,000 km of cycle facilities.
    • Rue de Rivoli was closed to general car traffic in May 2020, leaving it to buses, taxis, deliveries and a very wide two-way cycle track. It quickly became one of the busiest cycle routes in Europe.
    • 30 km/h city. From 30 August 2021 the limit on most Paris streets became 30 km/h, with a few main axes kept at 50.
    • Pricing by vehicle mass. After a local vote in February 2024, Paris tripled on-street parking charges for heavy cars (SUVs) belonging to visitors. This is a rare explicit price on vehicle mass, which drives crash severity and the use of space (Terms C and D).
    • School streets (rues aux écoles) are closed to cars in front of hundreds of schools.

    In \(W\) terms, Paris has reallocated land (Term D) from moving and parked cars to walking, cycling and green space, accepting a loss in the car part of the logsum. It has done so in a city where car trips within the city were already a small minority. The main open questions concern the suburbs: who loses access, and whether the Grand Paris Express rail network will compensate them.

    Strasbourg: tram and traffic plan

    Strasbourg chose a tramway over an automated metro and opened its first line in 1994. The tram came with a traffic plan that stopped cars crossing the city center. They must go around it, as in Groningen (see the Netherlands page). The tram was used to rebuild the streets it runs on, and the city combined it with park-and-ride and an extensive cycling network. Strasbourg is often cited as having the highest cycling share among large French cities. The CTS operator site shows today's network.

    Ville 30: Grenoble, Lyon and others

    Grenoble and many communes of its metropolitan area moved to 30 km/h as the general limit from 2016 ("métropole apaisée"). Paris followed in 2021 and Lyon on 30 March 2022, keeping some main roads at 50 km/h. Dozens of other cities have done the same. The legal frame is simple: the mayor sets the limit by order. CEREMA's role has been to supply the design and evaluation guidance, for example on how to make a zone 30 self-explaining with entry treatments, narrower carriageways and two-way cycling on one-way streets. The welfare logic is the same as in Sweden and Spain: lower impact speeds cut crash severity sharply and lower noise, and they cost little travel time, because average urban speeds are already low.

    Exercise FR.1 · Is 30 km/h worth it?

    A city of 500,000 has 1,200 km of streets. Moving from 50 to 30 km/h applies to 900 km. Car traffic on those streets is 2 million vehicle-km per day, with an average speed today of 24 km/h. Assume the new average speed is 22 km/h. (a) Compute the annual time loss in vehicle-hours and value it at €12/h with 1.3 occupants per car. (b) Assume road deaths fall from 12 to 8 and serious injuries from 150 to 115 per year. Value them with the French appraisal values (use the ministry's fiches-outils). (c) Add a noise benefit of your choice and justify it. (d) Is the sign of \(\Delta W\) robust? What would change it? (e) Why might the same arithmetic be accepted when a mayor decides, and rejected when the national government decides for rural roads?

    Small towns: priority to walking

    The most famous small-town case, Pontevedra, is Spanish (see Spain). French small towns have contributed in a quieter way. Chambéry is credited with early experiments with shared-space streets in the 1980s and 1990s that fed into the 2008 legal creation of the zone de rencontre. Today many town centers combine a zone de rencontre core, a 30 km/h belt and parking at the edge. The lesson is that the legal category made these designs routine: a mayor does not need to invent a new instrument, only to apply one.

    9. Translated key passages

    Code de la route, art. R110-2 (version in force since 25 April 2022): zone de rencontre

    "zone de rencontre : section ou ensemble de sections de voies en agglomération constituant une zone affectée à la circulation de tous les usagers. Dans cette zone, les piétons sont autorisés à circuler sur la chaussée sans y stationner et bénéficient de la priorité sur les véhicules. La vitesse des véhicules y est limitée à 20 km/h."

    "Meeting zone: a section or set of sections of roads in a built-up area forming a zone assigned to traffic by all users. In this zone, pedestrians may walk on the carriageway without stopping there, and have priority over vehicles. Vehicle speed is limited to 20 km/h."

    The article goes on to say that all carriageways in the zone are two-way for cyclists unless the mayor decides otherwise, and that the entries and exits are signed and the whole zone is laid out consistently with the speed limit. The zone 30 is defined in the same article in parallel terms, with a 30 km/h limit and no pedestrian priority on the carriageway. Source: Légifrance, art. R110-2.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Official legal text.

    Code de la route, art. R412-6: the duty of care toward vulnerable users

    "Celui-ci [le conducteur] doit, à tout moment, adopter un comportement prudent et respectueux envers les autres usagers des voies ouvertes à la circulation. Il doit notamment faire preuve d'une prudence accrue à l'égard des usagers les plus vulnérables."

    "The driver must at all times behave prudently and considerately toward other users of roads open to traffic. In particular, they must take increased care toward the most vulnerable users."

    Reading: this "principle of prudence" was added in 2008 with the code de la rue reforms that also created the zone de rencontre. It writes the asymmetry of crash energy into the rules of conduct. Source: Légifrance, art. R412-6.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Official legal text.

    Quinet commission (2019): what the carbon value is

    "La valeur de l'action pour le climat – ou valeur tutélaire du carbone – vient combler cette défaillance de marché"

    "The value of climate action, or official ('tutelary') carbon value, fills this market failure."

    The commission's point is that the market gives CO₂ no price, so public decisions need a reference value. The commission also cautioned that a 2030 value of €250 does not mean a carbon tax of that level should be introduced. In this course's notation, it is \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) in Term C, derived as the shadow price of the carbon budget. Source: France Stratégie (2019).

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Exercise FR.2 · A tramway with a carbon value

    A tram line costs €400 million and is expected to remove 60 million car-km per year from 2030, at 150 g CO₂ per car-km and falling 4% per year as the fleet electrifies. Using the Quinet trajectory above (interpolate linearly between the given years, and keep the 2050 value after 2050), compute the present value of the CO₂ benefit over 2030–2069 at a 2.5% discount rate. What share of the cost does it cover? Repeat with a constant €44.60 per tonne (the frozen carbon-tax rate). Which number belongs in \(W\), and why? Then ask the forecasting question from the "Under the hood" box in section 2: at which carbon price did your forecast of 60 million car-km assume people drive?

    Discussion questions
    1. The Quinet value is a shadow price of a carbon budget, but the carbon tax is frozen far below it. Is France's \(W\) "consistent"? What does it mean for appraisal when the prices used in \(W\) differ from the prices travelers face?
    2. The versement mobilité taxes jobs, not trips. Under what conditions is it a better way to fund transit than fares, fuel taxes or land-value capture?
    3. Was the 80 km/h decision a mistake of substance, of process, or neither? Use the limit-versus-price framing of Module 1, and ask who should decide.
    4. Statutory appraisal did not stop high-speed lines with low returns. Should a negative counter-appraisal be able to block a project? Who would gain and who would lose that power?
    5. Paris has removed road space for cars in a city where only 4% of internal trips are by car. What would the same policy cost, in Term A, in a city where cars carry 60% of trips?
