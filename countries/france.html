    Role model country

    France: statutory appraisal, a rising carbon value and passive design

    Key terms: C E A constraints · Related modules: 3, 6, 9, 12, 14

    France combines two traditions that are rarely found together. The first is the engineer-economist tradition of the Ponts et Chaussées, going back to Jules Dupuit's 1844 essay on the utility of public works, which today produces a statutory socio-economic appraisal with an official, rising carbon value. The second is a preference for passive instruments: tens of thousands of roundabouts, the zone de rencontre and zone 30 in the Highway Code, and whole cities at 30 km/h. Add a payroll levy that funds urban public transport and you have a distinctive reading of \(W\). France also shows how fragile this can be. A carbon tax that followed the logic of Term C helped trigger the gilets jaunes protests, and much of the country outside the big cities remains deeply car-dependent.

    What to take from this page
    • Explain the French appraisal framework and the valeur de l'action pour le climat (Quinet reports), and use them to price Term C.
    • Describe the institutions (CEREMA, DGITM, Université Gustave Eiffel, AOMs) and the legal frame (LOM 2019, plans de mobilité, versement mobilité).
    • Read the legal definitions of zone de rencontre and zone 30 and explain why they are passive instruments.
    • Analyze Paris, Strasbourg and the Ville 30 movement as case studies.
    • Give a balanced account of French weaknesses, including the political economy of carbon pricing.

    2. Reading of \(W\)

    Module 1 summarizes the French reading as "statutory socio-economic appraisal with a high, rising carbon value; behavior shaped passively through geometry (roundabouts, Ville 30) rather than technology". The evidence follows.

    Term C: an official, rising carbon value C constraints

    France derives its carbon value from a budget, exactly as Module 1 describes: a binding emissions constraint \(E_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\le \bar E_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) implies a shadow price, and the official value is that shadow price. The 2019 Quinet commission (France Stratégie, La valeur de l'action pour le climat, February 2019) recalibrated the value for the net-zero target:

    Year2018203020402050
    Value of climate action (€2018 per tonne CO₂e)54250500775

    Values from Quinet (2019), Économie et Statistique 510–512. A new commission revised the trajectory in March 2025 ("The new trajectory for the value of climate action") to reflect the more ambitious 2030 target of the third national low-carbon strategy (SNBC 3). Check the current values before using them.

    The commission was careful to say that the value is an appraisal and abatement benchmark, not a recommended tax rate. That distinction matters in section 4.

    Term E: appraisal by law E

    Since the 1982 transport framework law (LOTI), large transport infrastructure projects must undergo a socio-economic evaluation. The ministry's evaluation framework (an instruction of 2014 with technical fiches-outils) sets out values of time, crash values, environmental values and the discount rate. Since a 2012 law, projects above a public-funding threshold also require an independent counter-appraisal (contre-expertise) organized by the government's investment secretariat. France also applies a premium for the opportunity cost of public funds, corresponding to \(\mu\) in Term E.

    Term A as access for all A constraints

    French transport law has long spoken of a droit au transport (now a droit à la mobilité), a right of access for every user. It works as an equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\). The 2019 mobility law (Loi n° 2019-1428 d'orientation des mobilités, LOM) set out to cover the whole territory with mobility authorities, including rural areas that previously had none, and lists reducing territorial inequality among its four objectives.

    Instruments: passive design C

    France has built very large numbers of roundabouts since the 1980s, supported by national capacity tools (CEREMA's GIRABASE). It uses Highway Code categories (zone 30, zone de rencontre, aire piétonne; section 7) that tie a speed limit to a coherent street design. And since 2021 many large cities have adopted 30 km/h as the default speed. These instruments reduce crash severity and noise in Term C without enforcement technology or active control. Module 1, section 6 explains why this matters for Term E.

    The versement mobilité is worth a moment. It is a tax on employment, not on travel, so it does not change \(c_{nj}\) and does not correct any externality. Its justification is that employers capture part of the access benefits (Term A) that public transport creates, through larger labor markets. In welfare terms it is a way to fund \(K-R\) with a tax base linked to the beneficiaries, which may have a lower \(\mu\) than general taxation. It is also one reason French cities could afford tramways.

    4. Signature ideas and documented weaknesses

    Signature ideas

    1. Carbon value from a budget. Deriving \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) from the constraint, rather than from damage estimates, makes the carbon value consistent with the national target by construction.
    2. Appraisal as law, with independent counter-appraisal of large projects.
    3. The tramway renaissance. Since Nantes (1985), Grenoble (1987) and Strasbourg (1994), French cities have built tramways as tools of urban redesign, rebuilding streets façade to façade rather than just laying tracks.
    4. Street categories in the Highway Code and the 30 km/h city.
    5. Roundabouts as passive control. They replace signals with a geometry that forces low speeds and removes the most dangerous conflicts.

    Documented weaknesses and criticisms

    The other France
    • Periurban car dependence. Decades of low-density housing growth around cities, out-of-town retail and the closure of local services have left many households with no realistic alternative to the car. For them Term A is almost entirely car-based, and increases in fuel prices are a direct loss of access.
    • The gilets jaunes and carbon tax. France's carbon component in fuel taxes (contribution climat-énergie) was planned to rise steeply. The scheduled increase for 2019 helped trigger the gilets jaunes protests from November 2018. The government canceled the increase, and the rate has been frozen since at €44.60 per tonne, far below the appraisal values above. The lesson for Module 13: a price that is efficient in Term C can violate a felt equity floor, and losing political consent can cost more than the welfare gain.
    • The 80 km/h controversy. In July 2018 the national speed limit on two-lane rural roads without a median was cut from 90 to 80 km/h, a classic Vision-Zero-type measure aimed at France's most dangerous roads. It became a symbol of rural grievance, and from 2019 departments were allowed to restore 90 km/h on some roads, which many did.
    • Low-emission zones under pressure. The LOM and the 2021 Climate and Resilience Law required zones à faibles émissions (ZFE) in larger cities. They were criticized as penalizing lower-income car owners who cannot afford newer vehicles, and in 2025 Parliament voted to scrap the ZFE obligation. Check the ministry's ZFE page for the current legal position.
    • Road safety lags its neighbors. At about 4.8 deaths per 100,000, France is well behind Sweden, the Netherlands and Germany, and the rural road network accounts for most deaths.
    • Paris is not France. The Paris figures in section 1 reflect one of the densest, best-served cities in the world. Exporting "the Paris model" to small towns without the transit network misreads what produced it.

    5. Case studies

    Paris: Plan Vélo, Rue de Rivoli, 30 km/h and parking prices

    Paris has transformed its streets in about fifteen years. Key steps:

    • The banks of the Seine were closed to through traffic on the right bank in 2016 and turned into a park and promenade. The decision went through years of litigation.
    • Plan Vélo. Two successive plans (2015–2020 and 2021–2026) built protected cycle tracks on main axes. The diagnostic of the city's mobility plan reports more than 1,000 km of cycle facilities.
    • Rue de Rivoli was closed to general car traffic in May 2020, leaving it to buses, taxis, deliveries and a very wide two-way cycle track. It quickly became one of the busiest cycle routes in Europe.
    • 30 km/h city. From 30 August 2021 the limit on most Paris streets became 30 km/h, with a few main axes kept at 50.
    • Pricing by vehicle mass. After a local vote in February 2024, Paris tripled on-street parking charges for heavy cars (SUVs) belonging to visitors. This is a rare explicit price on the mass difference that drives crash severity and land use (Terms C and D).
    • School streets (rues aux écoles) are closed to cars in front of hundreds of schools.

    In \(W\) terms, Paris has reallocated land (Term D) from moving and parked cars to walking, cycling and green space, accepting a loss in car logsum. It has done so in a city where car trips within the city were already a small minority. The main open questions concern the suburbs: who loses access, and whether the Grand Paris Express rail network will compensate them.

    Strasbourg: tram and traffic plan

    Strasbourg chose a tramway over an automated metro and opened its first line in 1994. The tram came with a traffic plan that stopped cars crossing the city center. They must go around it, as in Groningen (see the Netherlands page). The tram was used to rebuild the streets it runs on, and the city combined it with park-and-ride and an extensive cycling network. Strasbourg is often cited as having the highest cycling share among large French cities. The CTS operator site shows today's network.

    Ville 30: Grenoble, Lyon and others

    Grenoble and many communes of its metropolitan area moved to 30 km/h as the general limit from 2016 ("métropole apaisée"). Paris followed in 2021 and Lyon on 30 March 2022, keeping some main roads at 50 km/h. Dozens of other cities have done the same. The legal frame is simple: the mayor sets the limit by order. CEREMA's role has been to supply the design and evaluation guidance, for example on how to make a zone 30 self-explaining with entry treatments, narrower carriageways and two-way cycling on one-way streets. The welfare logic is the same as in Sweden and Spain: lower impact speeds cut crash severity sharply, lower noise, and cost little in travel time, because average urban speeds are already low.

    Exercise FR.1 · Is 30 km/h worth it?

    A city of 500,000 has 1,200 km of streets. Moving from 50 to 30 km/h applies to 900 km. Car traffic on those streets is 2 million vehicle-km per day, with an average speed today of 24 km/h. Assume the new average speed is 22 km/h. (a) Compute the annual time loss in vehicle-hours and value it at €12/h with 1.3 occupants per car. (b) Assume road deaths fall from 12 to 8 and serious injuries from 150 to 115 per year. Value them with the French appraisal values (use the ministry's fiches-outils). (c) Add a noise benefit of your choice and justify it. (d) Is the sign of \(\Delta W\) robust? What would change it?

    Small towns: priority to walking

    The most famous small-town case, Pontevedra, is Spanish (see Spain). French small towns have contributed in a quieter way. Chambéry is credited with early experiments with shared-space streets in the 1980s and 1990s that fed into the 2008 legal creation of the zone de rencontre. Today many town centers combine a zone de rencontre core, a 30 km/h belt and parking at the edge. The lesson is that the legal category made these designs routine: a mayor does not need to invent a new instrument, only to apply one.

    7. Translated key passages

    Code de la route, art. R110-2 (version in force since 25 April 2022): zone de rencontre

    "zone de rencontre : section ou ensemble de sections de voies en agglomération constituant une zone affectée à la circulation de tous les usagers. Dans cette zone, les piétons sont autorisés à circuler sur la chaussée sans y stationner et bénéficient de la priorité sur les véhicules. La vitesse des véhicules y est limitée à 20 km/h."

    "Meeting zone: a section or set of sections of roads in a built-up area forming a zone assigned to traffic by all users. In this zone, pedestrians may walk on the carriageway without stopping there, and have priority over vehicles. Vehicle speed is limited to 20 km/h."

    The article goes on to say that all carriageways in the zone are two-way for cyclists unless the mayor decides otherwise, and that the entries and exits are signed and the whole zone is laid out consistently with the speed limit. The zone 30 is defined in the same article in parallel terms, with a 30 km/h limit and no pedestrian priority on the carriageway. Source: Légifrance, art. R110-2.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Official legal text.

    Code de la route, art. R412-6: the duty of care toward vulnerable users

    "Celui-ci [le conducteur] doit, à tout moment, adopter un comportement prudent et respectueux envers les autres usagers des voies ouvertes à la circulation. Il doit notamment faire preuve d'une prudence accrue à l'égard des usagers les plus vulnérables."

    "The driver must at all times behave prudently and considerately toward other users of roads open to traffic. In particular, they must take increased care toward the most vulnerable users."

    Reading: this "principle of prudence" was added in 2008 with the code de la rue reforms that also created the zone de rencontre. It writes the asymmetry of crash energy into the rules of conduct. Source: Légifrance, art. R412-6.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Official legal text.

    Quinet commission (2019): what the carbon value is

    "La valeur de l'action pour le climat – ou valeur tutélaire du carbone – vient combler cette défaillance de marché"

    "The value of climate action, or official ('tutelary') carbon value, fills this market failure."

    The commission's point is that the market gives CO₂ no price, so public decisions need a reference value. The commission also cautioned that a 2030 value of €250 does not mean a carbon tax of that level should be introduced. In this course's notation, it is \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) in Term C, derived as the shadow price of the carbon budget. Source: France Stratégie (2019).

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Exercise FR.2 · A tramway with a carbon value

    A tram line costs €400 million and is expected to remove 60 million car-km per year from 2030, at 150 g CO₂ per car-km and falling 4% per year as the fleet electrifies. Using the Quinet trajectory above (interpolate linearly between the given years, and keep the 2050 value after 2050), compute the present value of the CO₂ benefit over 2030–2069 at a 2.5% discount rate. What share of the cost does it cover? Repeat with a constant €44.60 per tonne (the frozen carbon-tax rate). Which number belongs in \(W\), and why?

    Discussion questions
    1. The Quinet value is a shadow price of a carbon budget, but the carbon tax is frozen far below it. Is France's \(W\) "consistent"? What does it mean for appraisal when the prices used in \(W\) differ from the prices travelers face?
    2. The versement mobilité taxes jobs, not trips. Under what conditions is it a better way to fund transit than fares, fuel taxes or land-value capture?
    3. Was the 80 km/h decision a mistake of substance, of process, or neither? Use the constraint-versus-price framing of Module 1.
    4. Paris has removed road space for cars in a city where only 4% of internal trips are by car. What would the same policy cost, in Term A, in a city where cars carry 60% of trips?
    5. Roundabouts and Ville 30 are passive instruments. Name one French problem they cannot solve, and say which instrument family (rules and prices, or active control) would.
