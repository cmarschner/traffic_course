    The Netherlands: safety by design and the cycling city

    Key terms: A B C constraints · Related modules: 6, 9, 11, 12, 14

    The Netherlands is the country most often held up as proof that a rich, car-owning society can make walking and cycling ordinary. Its distinctive contribution to this course is not a single policy but a design philosophy: classify every road by function, then shape it so that speed and mass differences between road users stay small wherever they meet. Combined with a formal social cost–benefit tradition, this gives a reading of \(W\) in which Terms A and B are large, the crash part of Term C is attacked by geometry rather than by price, and appraisal is taken seriously. The Netherlands also has real weaknesses: rising cyclist deaths, a large and still-growing motorway and aviation sector, and decades of failure to price car use.

    What to take from this page
    • Explain Sustainable Safety (Duurzaam Veilig) and its five principles, and show how each changes a term or constraint of \(W\).
    • Describe the Dutch functional road hierarchy and why it rules out the "stroad".
    • Name the institutions (Rijkswaterstaat, CROW, SWOV, KiM, PBL, CPB) and say which part of \(W\) each one estimates or governs.
    • Give a balanced account: signature successes and documented weaknesses.
    • Find free Dutch-language and English teaching material and read a central Dutch source critically.

    2. Reading of \(W\)

    Module 1 summarizes the Dutch reading as "safety by design (minimize speed and mass differences); cycling as a primary mode, so Terms A and B dominate; formal social cost–benefit analysis (MKBA)". Here is the evidence behind each part, and its limits.

    Term A: access, with the bicycle inside the choice set A

    In most countries the logsum in Term A is computed over car and public transport, and cycling is an afterthought. In Dutch practice the bicycle is a full alternative \(j\) in \(C_n\) for nearly every trip under about 7.5 km. The national cycling statistics (KiM, Fietsfeiten 2023) report that for trips up to 7.5 km the bicycle is used about as often as the car. Because \(V_{\text{bike}}\) is high for so many people, network improvements for cycling create large logsum gains, and the bicycle also widens the catchment of rail stations (bike-and-ride). This is the main reason the Dutch case is about access rather than "green" transport.

    Term B: health B

    Dutch cost–benefit studies of cycling schemes routinely monetize health effects, and these often make up a large share of the benefits. In the logic of Module 1, Term B is large because the exposure (kilometers cycled per person per year) is large, not because the Dutch value health more than others.

    Term C, crash part: priced in appraisal, attacked by geometry C

    Crash costs appear as a priced externality in MKBA. But the main Dutch tool against crashes is not a price. It is the Sustainable Safety vision, which tries to change the physics of every encounter: separate road users where speeds differ, and slow motor traffic to the speed of the most vulnerable user where they must mix. In the notation of Module 1, Sustainable Safety acts on \(E_{\text{crash}}(\theta)\) directly through \(\theta\) (geometry), not through \(p_{\text{crash}}\).

    Safety constraint: an aspiration rather than a hard limit constraints

    The third edition of Sustainable Safety (SWOV, 2018) describes a casualty-free road system as the ultimate ambition, and the national Strategic Road Safety Plan 2030 adopts a "zero" goal. Unlike Sweden, however, the Netherlands has not given the target the status of a parliamentary ethical principle. In practice it behaves like a strong preference with an implicit shadow price, not like the hard constraint \(F(\theta)\le\bar F\).

    Terms C (CO₂, nitrogen) and D: constraints imposed by courts and land C D

    The most binding environmental constraint in recent Dutch practice has been legal rather than economic. In May 2019 the Council of State (Raad van State) struck down the national nitrogen permitting program (PAS). Road and other construction projects that raised nitrogen deposition on protected nature areas then needed new legal grounds, and several were delayed. This is a hard constraint of the form \(E_k \le B_k\) entering through the courts, not through prices. Land (Term D) is scarce in a very dense country, which shows up in tight design standards rather than in an explicit \(r\).

    Term E: careful appraisal, weak pricing E

    The Netherlands has one of the most developed social cost–benefit traditions in Europe (MKBA, section 3). Yet for decades it did not price road use. A national kilometer charge for cars was legislated for and then shelved around 2010. A distance-based truck charge (vrachtwagenheffing) only started on 1 July 2026. In terms of Module 13, the Netherlands has measured the externalities in C carefully but has mostly left them unpriced for cars.

    Sustainable Safety

    SWOV first published Duurzaam Veilig in 1992. It was implemented from 1997 with a national "start-up program" that converted large numbers of residential streets to 30 km/h zones and many rural access roads to 60 km/h. The second edition (2005) set out five principles: functionality, homogeneity, physical forgiveness, recognisability/predictability and state awareness. The third edition (2018, for 2018–2030) regroups them into three design principles and two organizational principles:

    DV3 principleMeaningEffect in \(W\)

    (Bio)mechanicsLimit differences in speed, direction, mass and protection. Where they cannot be limited, separate users.Lowers crash severity in C; justifies 30 km/h where cyclists mix with cars
    PsychologicaMatch the road to what road users can perceive and do; make the correct behavior obvious.Passive design instead of enforcement (cheaper E)
    ResponsibilityClear allocation of tasks and responsibilities among authorities.Governance
    Learning and innovatingSystematic monitoring and learning from crashes.Updates the estimates of \(E_{\text{crash}}\)

    The functional hierarchy is the key engineering idea. A stroad, the North American street–road hybrid that combines property access, parking, turning traffic and 60–70 km/h through-traffic, cannot exist in a strictly categorized Dutch network. It must be split into a distributor (with separated cycle tracks and few accesses) or calmed into an access street. The cost is detours for motor traffic. That shows up in Term A as longer car routes and is a deliberate trade-off.

    Design standards worth studying

    • Turbo-roundabouts with raised lane dividers, which force lane choice before entry and remove weaving on two-lane roundabouts (see Module 9).
    • Protected intersections, in which cycle tracks keep their own corner islands and drivers turn at a near-right angle with good sight lines.
    • Cycle streets (fietsstraten), where cars are "guests", and filtered permeability, where bollards or bus gates stop through car traffic but let bicycles pass.
    • 30 km/h as the urban default. After a 2020 motion in parliament, national and local government have been working toward 30 km/h as the normal limit in built-up areas. Amsterdam applied it to most of its streets in December 2023.

    4. Signature ideas and documented weaknesses

    Signature ideas

    1. Design for the road user who makes a mistake. Sustainable Safety accepts human error as given and changes the system. It is a close cousin of Vision Zero (see the Sweden page), but more explicitly about geometry.
    2. The bicycle as a network, not a facility. Dutch cycling policy aims at a continuous, direct, safe, comfortable and attractive network (the CROW requirements). Isolated bike lanes would add little to the logsum.
    3. Appraisal culture. Major infrastructure decisions are expected to come with an MKBA reviewed against national guidance, and independent agencies (CPB, PBL, KiM) can and do publish unwelcome results.
    4. Bicycle–rail integration. Very large station bicycle parks (Utrecht Centraal's underground facility has about 12,500 places) turn rail into a door-to-door mode.

    Documented weaknesses and criticisms

    The Netherlands is not a car-free utopia
    • Cyclist deaths are rising. CBS counts 246 cyclist deaths in 2024 and 281 in 2025, more than a third of all road deaths, with at least 41% of the 2025 cyclist victims on e-bikes. Most are older people, often in single-bicycle crashes. Total road deaths in 2025 (759) were the highest in nearly two decades. Infrastructure built for 15–20 km/h conventional bicycles is now used by older riders on faster, heavier e-bikes and by mopeds, which strains the (bio)mechanics principle.
    • Motorway expansion continues. Rijkswaterstaat continues to widen motorways. Some projects, such as the A27 widening through the Amelisweerd woods near Utrecht, have been strongly contested. The national models used to justify them have been criticized for under-representing induced demand (Module 5).
    • Aviation. Schiphol is one of Europe's largest airports. In 2022 the government announced a cut in its flight cap to reduce noise. The plan was later suspended amid legal challenges and objections from airlines and foreign governments. The aviation externalities in C are large and politically hard to constrain.
    • Pricing failure. As noted in section 2, a car kilometer charge was shelved around 2010. Car use outside the cities is high and largely unpriced at the margin, apart from fuel tax.
    • Nitrogen deadlock. The 2019 nitrogen ruling showed that environmental limits left unpriced for decades can suddenly bind, halting projects of every kind at once.
    • Transferability. Dutch cities are flat, dense and compact. Their cycling shares were partly inherited: the 1970s protests (Stop de Kindermoord) and oil crisis came before car dependence was fully locked in. Planners elsewhere should copy the principles, not the numbers.

    5. Case studies

    Groningen: the 1977 traffic circulation plan

    Groningen's verkeerscirculatieplan (VCP), approved in the mid-1970s and implemented in 1977 under the Labor alderman Max van den Berg, divided the city center into four sectors. Cars could not drive directly from one sector to another. They had to go out to the ring road and back in. Pedestrians, cyclists and buses could cross freely. This is filtered permeability at city scale. It changes generalized costs in \(V_{nj}\) asymmetrically: car times between sectors rise, while walking and cycling times fall because streets become quieter and more direct. The plan was fiercely opposed at first, especially by retailers, and is now treated as a founding case of the car-limited center. Ghent (2017) used the same logic.

    Exercise NL.1 · Sector plan arithmetic

    A center has four sectors. Before the plan, a car trip between adjacent sectors takes 6 minutes. After the plan it takes 11 minutes via the ring road, and a cycle trip falls from 9 to 8 minutes. Use a binary logit with \(V_{\text{car}}=-0.10\,t_{\text{car}}-0.5\) and \(V_{\text{bike}}=-0.12\,t_{\text{bike}}\) (\(t\) in minutes), and \(\lambda=0.2\) per euro. Compute the car share and the logsum change per trip. Now add an external crash and noise cost of €0.30 per car trip (Term C). Does the plan raise \(W\) for this trip type? What is missing from the calculation, for example time savings for buses, or longer-run changes in where people shop?

    Houten: a town designed around the bicycle

    Houten, south of Utrecht, was expanded from a village into a planned town from the late 1970s onwards. Its plan reverses the usual hierarchy. A ring road around the town gives car access to each neighborhood from the outside. Neighborhoods are not connected to each other by car through the center, while a dense grid of cycle paths runs directly to the central railway station and the town center. For many internal trips the bicycle is faster than the car. Houten shows that the Groningen principle can be designed in from the start, at much lower cost than retrofitting.

    Utrecht: undoing the 1970s

    Utrecht filled in part of its canal ring in the 1970s to build an urban motorway-like road (Catharijnebaan). Around 2020 the city reopened the water as the Catharijnesingel, now lined with walking and cycling routes. Together with the redevelopment of the station area, including the bicycle parking mentioned above, this is a clear case of reallocating land: Term D (land) is given back to non-traffic uses, and the logsum loss for through car traffic is accepted.

    From stroads to roads and streets

    Dutch municipalities categorize their networks and then rebuild roads that do not fit their category. A typical rebuild converts a 50 km/h four-lane urban arterial with direct property access into either (a) a distributor road with separated cycle tracks, few side accesses and roundabouts or signal-controlled junctions, or (b) a 30 km/h access street with mixed traffic. The engineering literature calls this the sustainable safety categorisation. Mark Wagenbuur's BicycleDutch blog and YouTube channel document many before-and-after cases on video. They are an advocacy source but a very useful visual one.

    7. Translated key passages

    Sustainable Safety: the functionality principle (SWOV, Duurzaam Veilig)

    "Monofunctionaliteit van wegen als ofwel stroomweg, ofwel gebiedsontsluitingsweg, ofwel erftoegangsweg, in een hiërarchisch opgebouwd wegennet."

    "Mono-functionality of roads: each road is either a through-road, a distributor road or an access road, in a hierarchically structured road network."

    Context: this is the wording of the functionality principle in the second edition (2005), as it is still quoted in many Dutch municipal and provincial plans. A through-road (stroomweg) carries flow at high speed with no slow traffic. A distributor (gebiedsontsluitingsweg) carries flow on its links and lets traffic exchange only at junctions. An access road (erftoegangsweg) gives access to homes and destinations, with mixed traffic at low speed.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Sustainable Safety, third edition (SWOV, 2018): lineage of the principles

    "…de eerdere ontwerpprincipes van Duurzaam Veilig: functionaliteit van wegen, de (bio)mechanische principes homogeniteit en fysieke vergevingsgezindheid en de psychologische principes herkenbaarheid en statusonderkenning."

    "…the earlier design principles of Sustainable Safety: functionality of roads, the (bio)mechanical principles of homogeneity and physical forgiveness, and the psychological principles of recognisability and state awareness."

    Reading: "homogeneity" is the key idea for Term C. Crash energy rises with the square of the speed difference and with mass differences, so the design goal is to make the users who meet each other similar in speed and mass, or to keep them apart.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Exercise NL.2 · Homogeneity as physics

    A car (1,500 kg) hits a cyclist (90 kg with bicycle) from behind. Compare the cyclist's velocity change, assuming a perfectly inelastic collision, at closing speeds of 20 km/h and 50 km/h. Then look up a pedestrian or cyclist fatality-risk curve as a function of impact speed (see Module 11). Explain in one paragraph why the (bio)mechanics principle implies either separation or 30 km/h, and what it implies for e-bikes on cycle tracks shared with conventional bicycles.

    Discussion questions
    1. Sustainable Safety acts on \(E_{\text{crash}}\) through geometry, Vision Zero through a hard constraint. Can you tell the two apart in practice? Where would they recommend different designs?
    2. The Netherlands measures externalities carefully but did not price car use for decades. Using Modules 3 and 13, explain why careful appraisal and weak pricing can coexist. Which is more important for \(W\)?
    3. Is the Groningen sector plan a Pareto improvement? Who loses, and could the losers be compensated?
    4. Rising cyclist deaths among older e-bike riders: is this a failure of Sustainable Safety or a side effect of its success (more exposure)? What data would you need to decide?
    5. Which Dutch practices depend on flat, compact cities, and which would transfer to a hilly, sprawling metropolis?
