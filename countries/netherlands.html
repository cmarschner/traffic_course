    Country study

    The Netherlands: safety by design, the cycling city and the motorway program

    Key terms: A B C constraints · Related modules: 6, 9, 11, 12, 14

    The Netherlands is the country most often held up as proof that a rich, car-owning society can make cycling ordinary. It is also a country that widens motorways every year and measures the minutes lost in motorway jams against official targets. Both are Dutch. They come from different parts of the state, which follow different rules. This page does not treat the Netherlands as one philosophy. It separates four layers: what laws and plans say, how projects are appraised, what the design standards require, and what is actually built. Then it asks where the layers pull apart, and why.

    In brief
    The question
    Why does one country build some of the world's safest cycling streets and, at the same time, a large motorway-widening program?
    Stated objectives
    Zero road deaths as a long-term ambition. Motorway travel-time targets: in the rush hour, a trip between cities should take at most 1.5 times as long as outside it. A national cycling ambition, with little national money attached.
    How projects are appraised
    National projects need a social cost–benefit analysis, mandatory for regular national projects since 2006. Road effects come from a prescribed national traffic model. The official guidance says that estimating effects on cycling is "still in development". The results hardly predict which option politicians choose.
    Design standards
    Sustainable Safety: give every road one function; where speeds and masses differ, separate road users; where they must mix, slow cars to 30 km/h. Written by research and standards bodies, applied mostly by municipalities.
    What gets built
    Cycling networks, built by municipalities and provinces. Motorway widening, built by the national road agency, which spent about €4 billion on national main roads in 2025. Road deaths rose to 759 in 2025, the highest in nearly two decades.
    Lessons for a decision maker
    Who owns a network, who pays for it and which model it must be judged with shape outcomes more than any national vision. Ask your analysts: which objective defined the "problem"? Which model produced the benefits? Which modes can that model see?
    Learning outcomes
    • Separate the four layers of Dutch practice (stated objectives, appraisal, design standards, outcomes) and name a primary source for each.
    • Explain Sustainable Safety (Duurzaam Veilig) and the functional road hierarchy, and why the hierarchy rules out the "stroad".
    • Describe how a national road project is found and appraised, from a travel-time target to a cost–benefit analysis, and why this process sees motorway bottlenecks more easily than gaps in a cycling network.
    • Name the institutions (Rijkswaterstaat, CROW, SWOV, KiM, PBL, CPB) and say which layer, and which part of \(W\), each one governs.
    • Use the Dutch case to answer the course's central question: why competent planners facing the same problem produce different solutions.

    2. Four layers: objectives, appraisal, standards, outcomes

    A country does not have one transport philosophy. It has laws and plans that state objectives. It has procedures that decide which projects pass. It has standards that decide what a project looks like on the ground. And it has budgets and politics that decide what is finally built. In the Netherlands, different bodies run each layer:

    LayerWho sets itMain documents

    Stated objectives

    Safety. The third edition of Sustainable Safety (SWOV, 2018) describes a road system without deaths or serious injuries as the ultimate ambition. The national Strategic Road Safety Plan 2030 adopts the same "zero" goal. It is an ambition, not a legal limit (section 4).

    Motorways. The national mobility policy of 2004, the Nota Mobiliteit, set explicit targets for motorway travel times in the rush hour. In the words of Rijkswaterstaat's 2011 national road analysis, a peak trip should take:

    • between cities, at most one and a half times as long as outside the peak;
    • around the five large cities, at most twice as long.

    The 2011 analysis calls these targets "the yardstick" for how the mobility system is doing (Rijkswaterstaat, NMCA weganalyse, 9 June 2011, p. 7). Notice what the target measures. It is a ratio of car travel times on one network. It says nothing about cycling, about safety, or about how many destinations people can reach.

    Cycling. National government, provinces and municipalities have agreed shared ambitions for cycling, including a national network of fast cycle routes (Nationaal Toekomstbeeld Fiets). The money is mostly local. In 2023 the associations of municipalities and provinces (VNG and IPO) told parliament that the state's proposed structural contribution to fast cycle routes was €6 million a year from 2024. That came on top of a one-off €780 million for cycling links to new housing areas (VNG and IPO, February 2023).

    How projects are appraised

    The Dutch word for social cost–benefit analysis is MKBA (maatschappelijke kosten-batenanalyse). An MKBA adds up all effects of a project that can be expressed in money, for everyone in the country, and compares them with the costs. The General MKBA Guidance (CPB/PBL, 2013) sets the method for all policy fields. The course's digest of it is on the Sources page.

    National transport projects pass through a program called MIRT, the multi-year program for infrastructure, spatial planning and transport. An MKBA was first required for special large projects, under guidance from 2000. Since 2006 it has also been mandatory for regular MIRT projects. It is made once, to support the minister's choice of a preferred option (Rijkswaterstaat, Werkwijzer MKBA bij MIRT-verkenningen, 2018).

    Where do projects come from in the first place? National analyses run the national traffic model forward to a future year. Wherever the predicted rush-hour travel time breaks the targets above, the analysis reports a bottleneck (knelpunt). A bottleneck can become an exploration in the MIRT, and an exploration ends with an MKBA. So the stated objective decides which problems enter the pipeline at all.

    The same 2018 work guide shows an asymmetry in the tools. For a road project, effects "are determined with the NRM", the regional version of the national model, using a prescribed framework and software. For a cycling project, the guide says that estimating the effects on cycling "is still in development", and so is valuing cyclists' travel time. The analyst must find their own method. The guide does list health effects for cycling projects, which is Term B in this course.

    How much does the MKBA matter? Jan Anne Annema and colleagues studied 106 Dutch MKBA reports from 2000 to 2012, covering 454 project variants. They found no significant link between a variant's net present value and the variant politicians chose, after controlling for other factors. A positive result did keep a variant "pending", which protected it from rejection (Annema, Frenken, Koopmans & Kroesen, 2017).

    Under the hoodFrom travel-time ratio to cost–benefit analysis: the steps and the models

    The national model. The Ministry uses two linked models. The Landelijk Model Systeem (LMS) forecasts mobility for the whole country. The Nederlands Regionaal Model (NRM) does the same in more detail for four regions, and is prescribed in planning studies (NMCA weganalyse 2011, p. 16).

    The bottleneck test. For each motorway route, the analysis computes the travel-time factor: predicted peak travel time divided by off-peak travel time. It compares this factor with the target of 1.5 or 2.0. The 2011 analysis even colored routes that came within 0.1 of the target, to show near-misses. The successor analysis, the Integral Mobility Analysis of 2021 (updated in 2023), takes a broader view: it looks for potential accessibility problems on roads, railways, waterways and urban public transport.

    The appraisal. In the MKBA of a road project, NRM output is fed into a mandatory cost–benefit tool that extracts changes in travel time, distance and reliability. These are turned into money with standard unit values per trip purpose. External effects (emissions, noise, safety) and costs are added. In the notation of Module 1, the result is close to \(\Delta W\) with all equity weights \(\omega_g=1\), measured against a reference case \(\theta_0\) that includes committed policy.

    What the chain cannot see. A congested cycle route, a dangerous junction for children or a missing link in a town's cycling network does not break a motorway travel-time target. It therefore never appears as a national bottleneck, whatever its value in \(W\). Such projects reach the agenda through local plans and local budgets instead.

    Design standards

    Design standards decide what a road looks like once someone has decided to build or rebuild it. The central Dutch standard is a vision, not a law. SWOV, the Institute for Road Safety Research, first published Duurzaam Veilig (Sustainable Safety) in 1992. It was implemented from 1997 with a national start-up program, which converted large numbers of residential streets to 30 km/h zones and many rural access roads to 60 km/h. The second edition (2005) set out five principles: functionality, homogeneity, physical forgiveness, recognisability and predictability, and state awareness. The third edition (2018, for 2018–2030) regroups them into three design principles and two organizational principles:

    DV3 principleMeaningEffect in \(W\)
    Functionality of roadsEvery road has one function: through-road (stroomweg), distributor (gebiedsontsluitingsweg) or access road (erftoegangsweg), in a hierarchical network.Reduces conflicts per vehicle-km (C) while keeping through-traffic fast (A)
    (Bio)mechanicsLimit differences in speed, direction, mass and protection. Where they cannot be limited, separate users.Lowers crash severity in C; justifies 30 km/h where cyclists mix with cars

    ResponsibilityClear allocation of tasks and responsibilities among authorities.Governance

    The functional hierarchy is the key engineering idea. A stroad is the North American street–road hybrid. It combines property access, parking, turning traffic and through-traffic at 60–70 km/h. In a strictly categorized Dutch network a stroad cannot exist. It must be split into a distributor (with separated cycle tracks and few accesses) or calmed into an access street. The cost is detours for motor traffic. That shows up in Term A as longer car routes, and it is a deliberate trade-off.

    CROW, a non-profit knowledge platform owned jointly by public authorities and industry, turns these principles into the design manuals that municipalities use. Standards worth studying:

    • Turbo-roundabouts with raised lane dividers, which force lane choice before entry and remove weaving on two-lane roundabouts (see Module 9).
    • Protected intersections, in which cycle tracks keep their own corner islands and drivers turn at a near-right angle with good sight lines.
    • Cycle streets (fietsstraten), where cars are "guests", and filtered permeability, where bollards or bus gates stop through car traffic but let bicycles pass.
    • 30 km/h as the urban default. After a 2020 motion in parliament, national and local government have been working toward 30 km/h as the normal limit in built-up areas. Amsterdam applied it to most of its streets in December 2023.

    3. Where the layers diverge, and why

    Read the four layers side by side and the puzzle dissolves. Dutch cycling streets and Dutch motorway widening are not contradictions inside one philosophy. They are the products of two different institutional systems that share a flag.

    Institutions: two systems in one country

    The national government owns the motorways. It also owns the national traffic model, the bottleneck analysis and the appraisal procedure. Municipalities and provinces own the streets and most cycling infrastructure. They design with SWOV's vision and CROW's manuals. Each system is coherent on its own terms. Dutch highway appraisal is therefore not cycling planning. It is a careful, conventional appraisal of road capacity, done in a country whose cities happen to be very good at cycling.

    Funding rules: money follows the network owner

    In 2025 about €4 billion flowed through the national Mobility Fund to the main road network. The structural national contribution to regional fast cycle routes, as proposed in 2023, was €6 million a year. This comparison is not a fair measure of cycling spending, because most cycling money is local. It is a fair measure of what the national level can and cannot do. A national cycling ambition with no national budget line depends on whether each municipality chooses to pay.

    Incentives: the objective defines the problem

    The motorway travel-time target turns car delay into an official "bottleneck" with a place in the national program. No equivalent target turns a dangerous junction or a missing cycle link into a national problem. Planners respond to the problems their institution can see. That is not bad faith. It is what a stated objective does.

    History: inherited city form

    Dutch cities are flat, dense and compact. Their cycling shares were partly inherited. The 1970s protests against child deaths in traffic (Stop de Kindermoord) and the oil crisis came before car dependence was fully locked in. Many cities then chose to protect their centers (section 7). These choices were local and political before they became national standards.

    Political power and the courts

    Two forces cut across all four layers. Politicians choose project variants largely independently of the MKBA result, as Annema and colleagues found. And courts can turn an unpriced environmental effect into a hard limit overnight, as the 2019 nitrogen ruling did. In the language of Module 1, that ruling made a constraint \(E_k \le B_k\) bind, without anyone having chosen \(B_k\) through appraisal.

    The same problem, here

    Take a problem every country on this site faces. A suburb 15 km from the city center is growing. Its one radial road into the city is jammed every morning. What would competent Dutch planners typically propose, and why? This is a stylized judgment, not a documented case.

    • The likely answer depends on who owns the road. If it is a national motorway, the rush-hour travel time breaks the 1.5 target, the national analysis reports a bottleneck, and a MIRT exploration studies a widening, judged by an MKBA. If it is a provincial or municipal road, the likely answer is a fast cycle route, a better bus or train link with large bicycle parking at the station, and filtered streets in the suburb. Often both happen at once.
    • Objectives: the national program aims at motorway travel-time ratios; the region and city aim at safe, direct cycling networks and liveable streets. Both are official.
    • Constraints: nitrogen permits, which can halt a widening; budgets; and scarce land.
    • Models: the road option is scored with the prescribed regional model and cost–benefit tool; for the cycle route, the official guidance says estimating effects is "still in development".
    • Institutions: about €4 billion a year of national money for main roads, against mostly local money for cycling.

    In the Netherlands, the level of government that owns the road decides which objective and which model apply, and so, often, which answer wins. Compare the United States, Germany, Japan, Singapore, Sweden and France.

    4. Reading the choices in \(W\)

    Module 1 summarizes the Dutch reading as "safety by design; cycling as a primary mode, so Terms A and B dominate; formal social cost–benefit analysis". The four layers show that this describes the cities and the design standards better than the national road program. Term by term:

    • Term A A. In Dutch cities the bicycle is a full option in the choice set for nearly every trip under about 7.5 km. Because many people find cycling attractive, improvements to the cycling network create large gains in the logsum, and cycling widens the catchment of rail stations. The national appraisal, however, measures Term A mainly through motorway travel times.
    • Term B B. Dutch appraisals of cycling schemes value health effects, and these often make up a large share of the benefits. Term B is large because the exposure (kilometers cycled per person) is large, not because the Dutch value health more than others.
    • Term C, crashes C. Crash costs appear as a price in the MKBA. But the main Dutch tool against crashes is geometry, not a price. In Module 1's notation, Sustainable Safety acts on \(E_{\text{crash}}(\theta)\) through the design \(\theta\), not through \(p_{\text{crash}}\).
    • The safety limit constraints. The zero goal has not been given the status of a binding rule. It behaves like a strong preference with an implicit price, not like a hard limit \(F(\theta)\le\bar F\).
    • Terms C (nitrogen, CO₂) and D D. The binding environmental limit came from the courts, not from prices. Land is scarce, which shows up in tight design standards rather than in an explicit rent \(r\).
    • Term E E. Careful appraisal sits next to weak pricing. The Netherlands measured the external costs of driving in C carefully but, for decades, left them unpriced for cars (Module 13).

    6. Successes and documented failures

    What works

    1. Design for the road user who makes a mistake. Sustainable Safety accepts human error as given and changes the system. It is a close cousin of Vision Zero (see the Sweden page), but more explicitly about geometry.
    2. The bicycle as a network, not a facility. Dutch cycling policy aims at a continuous, direct, safe, comfortable and attractive network (the CROW requirements). Isolated bike lanes would add little to the logsum.
    3. Appraisal culture. Major national decisions come with an MKBA made to national guidance, and independent agencies (CPB, PBL, KiM) can and do publish unwelcome results. Even if the MKBA rarely decides the choice, it makes the trade-offs public.
    4. Bicycle–rail integration. Very large station bicycle parks (Utrecht Centraal's underground facility has about 12,500 places) turn rail into a door-to-door mode.
    The Netherlands is not a car-free utopia
    • Cyclist deaths are rising. CBS counts 246 cyclist deaths in 2024 and 281 in 2025, more than a third of all road deaths, with at least 41% of the 2025 cyclist victims on e-bikes. Most are older people, often in crashes involving only their own bicycle. Infrastructure built for conventional bicycles at 15–20 km/h now carries older riders on faster, heavier e-bikes, and mopeds. That strains the (bio)mechanics principle.
    • Motorway expansion continues. Rijkswaterstaat continues to widen motorways. Some projects, such as the A27 widening through the Amelisweerd woods near Utrecht, have been strongly contested. Critics argue that the national models under-represent induced demand (Module 5).
    • Aviation. Schiphol is one of Europe's largest airports. In 2022 the government announced a cut in its flight cap to reduce noise. The plan was later suspended amid legal challenges and objections from airlines and foreign governments. The aviation harms in C are large and politically hard to limit.
    • Pricing failure. The car kilometer charge was shelved around 2010. Car use outside the cities is high and largely unpriced at the margin, apart from fuel tax.
    • Nitrogen deadlock. The 2019 nitrogen ruling showed that environmental limits left unpriced for decades can suddenly bind, halting projects of every kind at once.
    • Transferability. Planners elsewhere should copy the principles, not the numbers. The numbers depend on flat, compact cities and on choices made fifty years ago.

    7. Case studies

    Groningen: the 1977 traffic circulation plan

    Groningen's verkeerscirculatieplan (VCP) was approved in the mid-1970s and implemented in 1977 under the Labor alderman Max van den Berg. It divided the city center into four sectors. Cars could not drive directly from one sector to another. They had to go out to the ring road and back in. Pedestrians, cyclists and buses could cross freely. This is filtered permeability at city scale. It changes generalized costs in \(V_{nj}\) unevenly: car times between sectors rise, while walking and cycling times fall because streets become quieter and more direct. The plan was fiercely opposed at first, especially by retailers. It is now treated as a founding case of the car-limited center. Ghent (2017) used the same logic. Note the layer: this was a municipal decision, decades before any national standard asked for it.

    Exercise NL.1 · Sector plan arithmetic

    A center has four sectors. Before the plan, a car trip between adjacent sectors takes 6 minutes. After the plan it takes 11 minutes via the ring road, and a cycle trip falls from 9 to 8 minutes. Use a binary logit with \(V_{\text{car}}=-0.10\,t_{\text{car}}-0.5\) and \(V_{\text{bike}}=-0.12\,t_{\text{bike}}\) (\(t\) in minutes), and \(\lambda=0.2\) per euro. Compute the car share and the logsum change per trip. Now add an external crash and noise cost of €0.30 per car trip (Term C). Does the plan raise \(W\) for this trip type? What is missing from the calculation, for example time savings for buses, or longer-run changes in where people shop? Finally: would this plan ever be found by a national bottleneck analysis based on motorway travel times? Why not?

    Houten: a town designed around the bicycle

    Houten, south of Utrecht, was expanded from a village into a planned town from the late 1970s onwards. Its plan reverses the usual hierarchy. A ring road around the town gives car access to each neighborhood from the outside. Neighborhoods are not connected to each other by car through the center. Meanwhile a dense grid of cycle paths runs directly to the central railway station and the town center. For many internal trips the bicycle is faster than the car. Houten shows that the Groningen principle can be designed in from the start, at much lower cost than retrofitting.

    Utrecht: undoing the 1970s

    Utrecht filled in part of its canal ring in the 1970s to build an urban motorway-like road, the Catharijnebaan. Around 2020 the city reopened the water as the Catharijnesingel, now lined with walking and cycling routes. The introduction tells the story with photographs. Together with the redevelopment of the station area, including the bicycle parking mentioned above, this is a clear case of reallocating land. Term D (land) is given back to non-traffic uses, and the logsum loss for through car traffic is accepted.

    From stroads to roads and streets

    Dutch municipalities categorize their networks and then rebuild roads that do not fit their category. A typical rebuild converts a 50 km/h four-lane urban arterial with direct property access into one of two things: (a) a distributor road with separated cycle tracks, few side accesses and roundabouts or signal-controlled junctions, or (b) a 30 km/h access street with mixed traffic. Mark Wagenbuur's BicycleDutch blog and YouTube channel document many before-and-after cases on video. They are an advocacy source, but a very useful visual one.

    9. Translated key passages

    Sustainable Safety: the functionality principle (SWOV, Duurzaam Veilig)

    "Monofunctionaliteit van wegen als ofwel stroomweg, ofwel gebiedsontsluitingsweg, ofwel erftoegangsweg, in een hiërarchisch opgebouwd wegennet."

    "Mono-functionality of roads: each road is either a through-road, a distributor road or an access road, in a hierarchically structured road network."

    Context: this is the wording of the functionality principle in the second edition (2005), as it is still quoted in many Dutch municipal and provincial plans. A through-road (stroomweg) carries flow at high speed with no slow traffic. A distributor (gebiedsontsluitingsweg) carries flow on its links and lets traffic exchange only at junctions. An access road (erftoegangsweg) gives access to homes and destinations, with mixed traffic at low speed.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Sustainable Safety, third edition (SWOV, 2018): lineage of the principles

    "…de eerdere ontwerpprincipes van Duurzaam Veilig: functionaliteit van wegen, de (bio)mechanische principes homogeniteit en fysieke vergevingsgezindheid en de psychologische principes herkenbaarheid en statusonderkenning."

    "…the earlier design principles of Sustainable Safety: functionality of roads, the (bio)mechanical principles of homogeneity and physical forgiveness, and the psychological principles of recognisability and state awareness."

    Reading: "homogeneity" is the key idea for Term C. Crash energy rises with the square of the speed difference and with mass differences. So the design goal is to make the users who meet each other similar in speed and mass, or to keep them apart.

    Exercise NL.2 · Homogeneity as physics

    A car (1,500 kg) hits a cyclist (90 kg with bicycle) from behind. Compare the cyclist's velocity change, assuming a perfectly inelastic collision, at closing speeds of 20 km/h and 50 km/h. Then look up a pedestrian or cyclist fatality-risk curve as a function of impact speed (see Module 11). Explain in one paragraph why the (bio)mechanics principle implies either separation or 30 km/h, and what it implies for e-bikes on cycle tracks shared with conventional bicycles.

    Exercise NL.3 · Rewrite the target

    The motorway travel-time target defines a bottleneck as a route where the peak trip takes more than 1.5 (or 2) times the off-peak trip. (a) Name two projects that would raise \(W\) on a congested corridor but would never be proposed under this target. (b) Write an alternative national target based on access, for example the number of jobs reachable within 45 minutes by any mode. (c) Which institution would have to change its model, and which its budget, for your target to change what gets built?

    Discussion questions
    1. Sustainable Safety acts on \(E_{\text{crash}}\) through geometry, Vision Zero through a stated limit. Can you tell the two apart in practice? Where would they recommend different designs?
    2. The Netherlands measures externalities carefully but did not price car use for decades. Using Modules 3 and 13, explain why careful appraisal and weak pricing can coexist. Which matters more for \(W\)?
    3. Annema and colleagues found that MKBA results hardly predict the variant politicians choose. Is that a failure of the MKBA, or a sign that politicians weigh things the MKBA leaves out? What would you want to see before deciding?
    4. Is the Groningen sector plan a Pareto improvement? Who loses, and could the losers be compensated?
    5. Rising cyclist deaths among older e-bike riders: is this a failure of Sustainable Safety or a side effect of its success (more exposure)? What data would you need to decide?
