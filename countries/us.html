    Role model country

    United States

    ≈ 2 hours · Read after Module 1 · Key terms: A C D E constraints

    The United States built the toolkit the rest of the world uses to measure traffic: the Highway Capacity Manual, level of service, the four-step model, and federal benefit–cost analysis with a value of a statistical life. It is also the clearest case of a gap between what a country says it wants and what it builds. Federal law puts safety first among its goals, yet US roads kill three to five times as many people per resident as roads in the other role-model countries. And no other country in this course varies as much inside its own borders. Houston and Manhattan work under the same federal rules and have produced almost opposite cities.

    In brief
    The question
    Why does the country that invented the tools for measuring traffic, and that names safety as its first national goal, still build mostly for cars and lose about 37,000 to 40,000 people a year on its roads?
    Stated objectives
    Federal law lists seven goals for the highway program, from safety to environmental sustainability. It does not rank them, and each state sets its own targets. Many cities, and California, add zero-death and climate goals of their own.
    How projects are appraised
    Most federal highway money reaches the states by formula, without a benefit–cost test. Benefit–cost analysis decides competitive grants. Its carbon value and its discount rate changed with the administrations of 2021 and 2025.
    Design standards
    Level of service grades roads A to F by vehicle delay. National design and signing manuals are defaults with legal weight: a city that wants narrower lanes must justify the exception.
    What gets built
    Wide arterials and freeways in most regions, and transit concentrated in a few old cores. About 5% of workers commuted by transit in 2019, and almost two-fifths of them lived in the New York area.
    Lessons for a decision maker
    Ask which layer drives a project: the stated goal, the funding rule, the design manual or the model. In the US, changing the metric in law (California) and changing the default speed and street layout (Hoboken) moved outcomes. Stating a goal alone did not.
    Learning outcomes
    • Separate four layers of US practice (stated objectives, appraisal, design standards, observed outcomes) and give an example of each pair that diverges.
    • Explain why funding rules, such as formula grants and the 90% federal share for the Interstate, shape what is built more than appraisal does.
    • Explain California's SB 743 as a change of metric written into law, and work out a VMT threshold.
    • Assess three US cases (Katy Freeway, New York congestion pricing, Hoboken) in terms of \(W\).
    • Use the US to answer the course's central question for one country: how much of what is built is explained by objectives, constraints, models and institutions?

    2. Stated objectives: what the laws and plans say

    There is no single US transport objective. Each level of government states its own, and they do not always agree.

    Federal law: seven goals, no ranking

    Since 2012, federal law has named seven national goals for the federal highway program (23 U.S.C. §150(b)):

    1. Safety: "a significant reduction in traffic fatalities and serious injuries on all public roads".
    2. Infrastructure condition: keep highways "in a state of good repair".
    3. Congestion reduction on the National Highway System.
    4. System reliability.
    5. Freight movement and economic vitality.
    6. Environmental sustainability.
    7. Reduced project delivery delays.

    Read against \(W\), the list covers most of the terms: access (A), crashes and environment (C), and cost (E). Two things are missing. The first is any rule for trading the goals off: when less congestion means more traffic and more emissions, the law does not say which wins. The second is any limit. Each state sets its own numeric targets under the same section, and the law attaches no limit to them.

    Safety and climate: goals without force

    The 2022 National Roadway Safety Strategy adopted the Safe System approach and a long-term goal of zero deaths. The 2021 Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act created the Safe Streets and Roads for All grant program (US$5 billion over five years). Many cities, beginning with New York in 2014, have adopted Vision Zero. In Module 1's language that is a safety limit \(F\le\bar F\), but imposed locally and without legal force over state roads.

    Climate goals have changed with administrations. Between 2021 and 2024, federal programs added climate and equity criteria (the Justice40 initiative). A Secretarial order of January 2025 (Ensuring Reliance Upon Sound Economic Analysis) removed these, required rigorous benefit–cost analysis for grants, and introduced other selection priorities, including communities with higher marriage and birth rates and "user-pays" funding models. In \(W\)'s terms, the equity weights \(\omega_g\) and the carbon price were changed by executive order, twice in four years.

    States and cities: very different objectives

    • California set regional greenhouse-gas targets for its MPOs (SB 375, 2008), and in 2013 replaced vehicle delay with vehicle-miles traveled as the measure of transport impact in environmental review (SB 743, section 7).
    • Cities such as New York, Hoboken and Washington have adopted Vision Zero, lower speed limits and street-redesign programs.
    • Parking reform: Buffalo (2017), Minneapolis (2021) and California near major transit stops (AB 2097, 2022) abolished minimum parking requirements. The critique behind this came from an American, Donald Shoup of UCLA (Module 1).

    Other states and cities state congestion relief and economic growth as their main aims. The country therefore does not have one reading of \(W\). It has a federal frame that allows many.

    3. How projects are appraised

    An appraisal is the formal comparison of a project's benefits and costs before money is committed. In the US, whether a project is appraised at all depends on which pot of money pays for it.

    Formula money: no benefit–cost test

    Most federal highway money reaches the states by formula. The 2021 infrastructure law provides about US$348 billion for highways and bridges over fiscal years 2022 to 2026. About US$303 billion of it, 87%, is distributed by formula (ARTBA analysis of FHWA data, 2025). The states then choose projects. Federal law does not require a benefit–cost analysis for them.

    What it does require is a planning process. Every urbanized area over 50,000 people has an MPO, a regional board of local governments. The MPO writes a long-range plan for at least 20 years and a short-term program of projects (FHWA overview). Both must be fiscally constrained: they may only include what reasonably expected revenue can pay for. This is the one hard constraint in US planning, and it is a constraint on Term E, not on safety or carbon.

    The plans are usually tested with a regional four-step travel model (Module 4). These models often hold land use fixed and respond weakly to new road capacity. A widening therefore tends to show time savings that induced traffic later erodes (Module 5).

    Competitive grants: benefit–cost analysis

    Benefit–cost analysis (BCA) decides discretionary grants, where projects compete for a fixed sum. USDOT's Benefit–Cost Analysis Guidance for Discretionary Grant Programs, updated almost every year, prices travel-time savings, vehicle operating costs, crashes, emissions and the residual value of assets. It is a consumer-surplus framework, so in principle it can handle induced demand through the rule of a half (Module 2).

    Three of its parameters have moved with politics:

    • Crashes are priced with a value of a statistical life (VSL), revised every year for inflation and income. This one is stable, and it is a US invention that much of the world adopted (Module 3).
    • Carbon. The 2024–25 guidance valued CO₂ with the federal social cost of greenhouse gases. In 2025 the administration directed agencies to stop monetizing climate damages. In November 2025 FTA announced that it "will no longer consider the social cost of carbon" when rating transit projects for its Capital Investment Grants. At the federal level, \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) is currently zero.
    • The discount rate. In May 2025 the guidance returned to 7% a year in real terms, from the 3.1% used in 2024. A higher rate shrinks benefits that arrive late. It therefore hurts long-lived investments such as rail more than short-lived ones (see the box below).

    Transit projects that seek the largest federal grants go through a separate FTA rating of mobility, cost-effectiveness, environment, land use, economic development and congestion relief. A highway widening paid from formula money faces no comparable test. This asymmetry matters more for what gets built than any single number in the BCA guidance.

    Environmental review

    The National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA, 1970) requires federal agencies to state a project's purpose and need, compare alternatives and disclose impacts (nepa.gov). It is a procedure, not a test: it forces disclosure but does not forbid a harmful project. In 2025 the Council on Environmental Quality withdrew its government-wide NEPA regulations, and the Supreme Court's Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v. Eagle County decision narrowed the scope of review. California's own environmental law (CEQA) goes further, and it is there that SB 743 changed the metric.

    Under the hoodWhat a 7% discount rate does to a rail project, in numbers

    Take a rail project that is built over six years (years 0 to 5) with equal spending each year. It then yields constant benefits from year 6 to year 50. Each future amount is divided by \((1+i)^t\), where \(i\) is the discount rate and \(t\) the year.

    The present value of one unit of benefit per year, from year 6 to year 50, is

    $$\sum_{t=6}^{50}(1+i)^{-t} = \begin{cases} 20.7 & i = 3.1\%\\ 9.7 & i = 7\%.\end{cases}$$

    The present value of the construction cost changes much less, because it is spent early. One sixth of the cost in each of years 0 to 5 is worth 0.93 of the nominal cost at 3.1% and 0.85 at 7%.

    The benefit–cost ratio is the ratio of the two present values. Going from 3.1% to 7% multiplies it by \((9.7/0.85)/(20.7/0.93)\approx 0.51\). The same project's ratio roughly halves. A resurfacing project whose benefits come in the first ten years would lose much less. The discount rate therefore decides not only how many projects pass, but which kind.

    The other default settings, in the notation of Module 1: land already owned by a DOT carries no opportunity cost (\(r=0\)), and USDOT applies no marginal cost of public funds (\(\mu=0\)).

    4. Design standards and rules

    Most of what a driver, walker or cyclist experiences is decided not by appraisal but by design standards: lane widths, design speeds, signal timings and the definition of a "failing" junction.

    Level of service: where it came from and what it assumes

    The Highway Capacity Manual (HCM) was first published in 1950 by the federal Bureau of Public Roads. Its second edition, of 1965, organized the analysis around a single idea: level of service (LOS), a grade from A (free flow) to F (breakdown) based on delay or density (TR News, 1997). The manual is now in its 7th edition (TRB, 2022).

    LOS assumes that the goal of a road is to move the vehicles that will come anyway, with little delay. It serves one part of Term A: drivers' travel time. Local general plans, state DOT policies and, until 2020 in California, environmental review treated a drop in LOS as an "impact" to be mitigated, usually by adding lanes or turn pockets. That is the classic objective \(\min\sum_a q_a t_a\) of Module 1, applied junction by junction with demand held fixed.

    Where it fails: LOS counts vehicles, not people, so a bus with 50 passengers weighs as much as a car. A pedestrian's wait at a junction does not enter the grade. And because demand is fixed, a junction "fixed" by widening attracts more traffic over the years. Other traditions answer differently. Germany's capacity manual (HBS) uses similar grades, but its street-design guide sets space for pedestrians first. Swedish and Dutch practice starts from safety. California dropped vehicle delay from environmental review altogether. The HCM itself now has multimodal chapters, but the vehicle grade is still the one most plans use (Module 8).

    Design manuals with legal weight

    The geometric design guide of AASHTO, the association of state highway agencies, is known as the Green Book (7th edition, 2018). It sets design speeds, lane widths and sight distances. The Manual on Uniform Traffic Control Devices (MUTCD, 11th edition, effective January 2024) is the legally binding standard for signs, signals and markings. Deviating from either requires a documented design exception, so a city that wants narrower lanes carries the burden of proof. This is the opposite of the Swedish logic in Module 1, where a design must first show it is safe.

    Two features of these standards matter most for outcomes:

    • Design speed. A road designed for higher speeds than its posted limit invites drivers to go faster.
    • Speed limits from observed speeds. Limits were long set near the 85th-percentile speed of free-flowing traffic, the speed that 85% of drivers do not exceed. This ratchets limits upward on roads designed for speed. The 2023 MUTCD gives engineers more latitude to consider other factors, such as pedestrian activity.

    Change is under way. The 8th edition of the Green Book, in preparation, is organized around the context of a road (rural, suburban, urban core) and multimodal design (NCHRP vision and roadmap). Large cities wrote their own guides through NACTO, the National Association of City Transportation Officials: the Urban Street Design Guide (2013) and the Transit Street Design Guide (Module 12).

    Local zoning: land as a free input

    Minimum parking requirements in local zoning codes force developers to build a set number of spaces per home or per square foot of floor space. In the notation of the course, they set \(r=0\) for land used for car storage and pass its cost into rents and prices. They are a design standard in all but name, and they shape cities more than most road projects.

    Under the hoodHow LOS is graded at a signalized junction

    For a junction with traffic signals, the HCM grades level of service by control delay: the average extra time a vehicle spends because of the signal, compared with driving through at free-flow speed. The thresholds, in seconds per vehicle:

    LOSABCDEF
    Control delay (s/veh)≤ 1010–2020–3535–5555–80> 80

    The delay is an average over vehicles, not people, and only over motor vehicles. Adding a pedestrian phase raises control delay and can lower the grade, although it may raise welfare. Many local plans required LOS D or better at peak hours. A development that pushed a junction from D to E then had to pay for more lanes. California's SB 743 removed this test from state environmental review. Some cities still apply it through their own general plans. See Module 8 and Module 9.

    5. What gets built: observed outcomes

    The outcomes below are the test of the layers above.

    • Road deaths. 40,901 people died in 2023, or 12.2 per 100,000 residents. That is several times the rates of the other role models on this site: about 3.4 in Germany, 2.6 in Japan and 2.35 in Singapore. Pedestrians made up about 18% of deaths (7,314 in 2023). Deaths fell to 39,254 in 2024 and, by NHTSA's early estimate, to 36,640 in 2025, still far above the levels of the early 2010s. Analyses such as Smart Growth America's Dangerous by Design link pedestrian deaths to wide, fast arterials and larger vehicles.
    • Roads grew faster than people, and delay grew faster still. Between 1993 and 2017, freeway lane-miles in the 100 largest urbanized areas grew by 42%, population by 32%, and annual hours of delay by 144% (Transportation for America, 2020). This is an advocacy report, and part of all three numbers reflects growing urban boundaries. It agrees with the peer-reviewed finding that vehicle-miles in US metro areas rise roughly one-for-one with lane-miles (Duranton and Turner, 2011).
    • Transit is concentrated. In 2019 about 5% of US workers commuted by public transport. About 3 million of the 7.8 million transit commuters lived in the New York metro area (US Census Bureau, 2021). In Houston, 3.2% of workers commute by transit.
    • Very high transit construction costs. The NYU Transit Costs Project found US urban rail among the most expensive per kilometer in the world, which inflates \(K\) in Term E and makes each new line harder to justify.
    • Underfunded maintenance. The federal fuel tax (18.4 cents per gallon for gasoline) has not changed since 1993. The Highway Trust Fund has relied on transfers from general revenue since 2008, so fares and user taxes cover a shrinking share of costs.

    None of these outcomes is what a single stated goal asked for. Safety is the first national goal, yet the death rate is the highest of any role model. The explanation lies in the layers between the goal and the street.

    6. Where the layers diverge, and why

    LayerWhat it saysWhat it rewards in practice
    Stated objectivesSeven national goals, safety first; zero deaths as a long-term aimNothing directly: no ranking, no limits, targets set by each state
    Funding rulesFormula money to states; competitive grants; fiscal constraint on regional plansCapital projects on state highways; projects that fit the formula programs
    AppraisalBenefit–cost analysis with VSL; carbon and discount rate set by each administrationRanking within competitive grants only
    Design standardsLOS, design speed, 85th-percentile speed limits, parking minimumsVehicle throughput and speed; free car storage
    Outcomes—High road deaths, growing lane-miles, transit concentrated in a few cores

    Five reasons explain most of the gap. Each is an institutional choice that could be made differently.

    • Funding rules. The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 set the federal share of Interstate construction at 90% and created the Highway Trust Fund, fed by fuel taxes, to pay for it (FHWA history). A state paid ten cents of each dollar for a new freeway. Federal money for transit capital came later, from 1964, and the largest new lines must win competitive grants and bring a local match. Money that is cheap for the decision maker gets spent, whatever its benefit–cost ratio.
    • Who owns what. The state DOT owns the highways and many arterials through towns. The city owns local streets. A county or regional agency runs transit, and the municipality controls land use. Nobody owns the whole objective. A city that wants a safe main street may not control the road.
    • Models and metrics. LOS and fixed-demand models made widening look like a solution for fifty years. The time savings are visible in the model; the induced traffic, the crashes and the land are not.
    • History. The Interstate and the suburbs grew together from the 1950s. Homes, jobs and shops located for the car, and that pattern now generates the demand that roads must carry. Choices made once fix the demand for decades (Module 1, timescales).
    • Political power and volatility. Federal prices for carbon and equity changed with each administration. A project's ranking can depend on the year it was submitted. Local politics, meanwhile, decide whether a city adopts Vision Zero or a parking reform.

    Internal variety: one country, many answers

    The same federal rules produced Manhattan and Houston. Most New York City workers commute without a car; in Houston, about 70% drive alone. California measures transport impact in vehicle-miles and sets regional climate targets; other states still treat vehicle delay as the main impact. Hoboken has had no traffic death since 2017, while many of the metro areas with the highest pedestrian death rates are in the fast-growing South (Dangerous by Design). This variety is itself evidence. Federal goals and appraisal rules are the same everywhere, so the differences come from density, land-use rules, state law, local politics and history.

    The same problem, here

    Take a problem every country on this site faces. A suburb 15 km from the city center is growing. Its one radial road into the city is jammed every morning. What would competent US planners typically propose, and why? This is a stylized judgment, not a documented case.

    • The likely answer: widen the road or add express lanes, paid largely from formula money through the state DOT. In California, a widening must now report its induced vehicle-miles and mitigate them, so express lanes, transit or housing near jobs become more likely.
    • Objectives: congestion relief is a named national goal and the easiest to measure.
    • Constraints: only the fiscal constraint binds. There is no carbon or safety limit on the project.
    • Models: a four-step model with fixed land use shows large time savings.
    • Institutions: the state DOT owns the road and the money; the suburb controls land use; neither controls the other.

    In the US case, institutions and funding rules explain more of the answer than stated objectives do. Compare the same problem in Germany, Japan and Singapore.

    The US layers, read as \(W\)

    Federal appraisal: time savings minus crash costs minus public cost, with \(r=0\), \(\mu=0\), a 7% discount rate and currently \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}=0\). Design standards: vehicle time only, with demand held fixed. California: the same appraisal, plus a quasi-limit on vehicle-miles per resident and binding regional greenhouse-gas targets. New York: a charge on driving into the core. Hoboken: a local safety limit \(F\le\bar F\) met through design. This is our interpretation; agencies do not state it this way.

    What other countries can borrow

    • Quantification. The HCM, the four-step model, the national crash database (FARS) and the National Household Travel Survey made traffic measurable. Germany's HBS and many national manuals descend from the HCM.
    • Monetizing safety. The VSL approach, developed largely by US economists and agencies, makes crash reduction comparable with time savings.
    • Evidence on induced demand, and a tool built on it: the California Induced Travel Calculator (UC Davis), which Caltrans recommends for estimating the vehicle-miles induced by highway expansions.
    • Changing the metric in law. SB 743 is the clearest case anywhere of a legislature replacing a congestion metric with a vehicle-miles metric.
    • Pricing: express (HOT) lanes, first on California's SR 91 (1995), and New York's cordon charge (2025).
    • Bottom-up experiments. Cities such as New York, Hoboken and Washington tested low-cost street redesigns faster than many national systems would allow.

    7. Case studies

    Katy Freeway, Houston (2003–2008)

    Interstate 10 west of downtown was rebuilt to as many as 26 lanes, counting frontage roads, for about US$2.8 billion. Travel times fell immediately after the 2008 opening. Using Houston TranStar data, City Observatory reported that between 2011 and 2014 the morning peak trip on two corridor segments became 25 minutes (30%) longer and the afternoon trip 23 minutes (55%) longer (Cortright, 2015). Defenders note the corridor's rapid population and job growth and the much larger number of people it now carries. In \(W\) terms: Term A for new trips (more people living and working in west Houston) is positive. The claimed time savings for existing trips vanished. Terms C and D were never counted. See Module 5 for the induced-demand mechanics.

    New York congestion pricing (from 5 January 2025)

    The MTA's Congestion Relief Zone charges most cars US$9 once per day at peak times to enter Manhattan south of 60th Street. The scheme was authorized by state law in 2019, paused in June 2024 at a planned US$15, relaunched at US$9, and challenged by USDOT in 2025. According to the New York State Governor's office, the first year brought about 73,000 fewer vehicles per day entering the zone (−11%), morning peak speeds 23% higher, crashes 7% and injuries 8% lower, transit ridership up 7%, and net revenue of US$518 million by November 2025 (Governor's office, January 2026). These are official figures from the scheme's promoters; independent evaluations are still appearing.

    Read with \(W\), count each effect once. The charge itself is a transfer: drivers pay it, which lowers their surplus in Term A, and the MTA receives it as revenue in Term E. The two cancel, except through the marginal cost of public funds \(\mu\). The welfare gain comes from the behavior the charge changes. Drivers who stay save time, because the trips priced off the road no longer delay them; that gain appears in Term A. Fewer crashes reduce Term C. Against this stands the loss of the people who no longer make the trip, or switch to a mode they like less. See Module 13.

    Hoboken, New Jersey: zero traffic deaths

    Hoboken (about 60,000 residents on two square miles) has recorded no traffic death since early 2017, a streak reported as nine years in March 2026 (OPB, 2026). The tools were cheap and passive: daylighting (removing parking next to crossings so drivers and pedestrians can see each other), curb extensions, leading pedestrian intervals, no right turn on red at some junctions, and a citywide 20 mph limit (City of Hoboken Vision Zero). Its target is zero deaths and serious injuries by 2030. It shows that the safety limit \(F\le\bar F\) can be met in a US setting, at least in a small, dense, grid city that controls its own streets. See Module 11 and Module 12.

    California SB 743: a worked example

    Under the state's technical advisory, a residential project has a significant transport impact if its home-based vehicle-miles traveled (VMT) per resident exceed 85% of the existing regional (or city) average. Suppose the regional average is 20 VMT per resident per day.

    $$\text{threshold}=0.85\times 20=17\ \text{VMT/resident/day}.$$

    A greenfield subdivision modeled at 23 VMT must mitigate (for example, with transit passes, unbundled parking or more housing near jobs) or document a significant impact. An infill project near a rail station at 12 VMT is presumed to have none. Under LOS, the infill project could have been the one that failed, because it adds turning traffic at a congested downtown junction. The reversal is the point: the law changed which term of \(W\) the review protects.

    To put a rough value on it: suppose each vehicle-mile causes US$0.10 of crash, air-pollution, noise and climate damage to others (an illustrative figure; congestion delay is not included, because it already sits in Term A). Then avoiding 6 VMT per resident per day saves about US$220 per resident per year in Term C.

    9. Key passages from primary documents

    The central US documents are in English, so no translation is needed. Three short passages show the change of objective in California law.

    California Public Resources Code §21099(b)(1), via the OPR Technical Advisory

    The criteria for determining the significance of transportation impacts must "promote the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions, the development of multimodal transportation networks, and a diversity of land uses."

    OPR Technical Advisory on Evaluating Transportation Impacts in CEQA (December 2018)

    "…automobile delay, as measured by 'level of service' and other similar metrics, generally no longer constitutes a significant environmental effect under CEQA."

    "…OPR recommends that a per capita or per employee VMT that is fifteen percent below that of existing development may be a reasonable threshold."

    Source: OPR (now Governor's Office of Land Use and Climate Innovation), Technical Advisory, PDF.

    Read against \(W\): the first passage names three outcomes (carbon, multimodal choice, land-use mix) that live in Terms C and A. The second deletes the old metric. The third turns Term C into a quasi-limit, a per-capita VMT ceiling that projects must meet or mitigate.

    Discussion questions
    1. The seven national goals in 23 U.S.C. §150(b) carry no ranking. Rewrite them as one objective with limits, in the form of \(W\). Which goals become terms, which become limits, and which drop out as instruments?
    2. The USDOT discount rate moved from 7% to 3.1% and back to 7% between 2023 and 2025. The box in section 3 shows that this roughly halves a rail project's benefit–cost ratio. Is the discount rate a technical parameter or a value judgment? Who should set it, and how often should it change?
    3. Was the Katy Freeway widening a failure? State the counterfactual you would need, the terms of \(W\) you would estimate, and which of them the original appraisal set to zero.
    4. Hoboken met \(F=0\) for nine years with paint, posts and a 20 mph limit. Which features of Hoboken make this transferable, or not, to a Sun Belt city whose 45 mph arterials belong to the state DOT?
    5. SB 743 is sometimes criticized for discouraging rural and exurban housing. Write the VMT rule as a limit, then as a price. Which formulation would you defend, and why?
    6. New York's cordon revenue is earmarked for transit capital. In Module 1's notation, does earmarking change \(W\)? (Hint: think about \(\mu\) and about what the money would otherwise fund.)
