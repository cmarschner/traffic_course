    Sweden: Vision Zero and two co-equal goals

    Key terms: constraints A C E · Related modules: 3, 11, 13, 14

    Sweden gave the world Vision Zero: the principle, adopted by parliament in 1997, that no one should be killed or seriously injured in road traffic, and that the road system must be designed to make this possible. In the language of this course, Sweden turned the crash term of \(W\) from a price into a constraint. Sweden also runs a mature national appraisal system (ASEK), introduced one of the best-documented congestion charges in the world, and frames transport policy with two goals of equal rank: accessibility, and safety, environment and health. Its weaknesses are just as instructive: car dependence outside the big cities, a large motorway tunnel project in Stockholm, and a road-death count that fell sharply for two decades and then flattened.

    What to take from this page
    • State the Vision Zero decision of 1997 in its own words and explain what changes when safety becomes a constraint rather than a priced externality.
    • Describe Sweden's two co-equal transport-policy goals and the "four-step principle" for choosing measures.
    • Know the institutions (Trafikverket, Transportstyrelsen, VTI, Trafikanalys, the ASEK group) and what part of \(W\) each produces.
    • Analyze the Stockholm congestion tax and 2+1 roads as case studies with measured effects.
    • Give a balanced account of Swedish weaknesses.

    2. Reading of \(W\)

    Module 1 summarizes the Swedish reading as "safety as a hard constraint (Vision Zero); two co-equal transport-policy goals: accessibility, and safety–environment–health". Here is the evidence.

    The safety constraint constraints

    Government bill Prop. 1996/97:137, Nollvisionen och det trafiksäkra samhället ("Vision Zero and the traffic-safe society", May 1997) proposed that the long-term goal of road safety be that no one is killed or seriously injured, and that the design and function of the road transport system be adapted to this. Parliament adopted it in autumn 1997 (committee report 1997/98:TU4). Three features make it a constraint rather than a price:

    1. The target is zero for deaths and serious injuries, not an "optimal" level where marginal safety cost equals marginal benefit.
    2. Human tolerance is the design norm. The bill states that people's physical ability to withstand external violence in crashes should set the standard for designing the road transport system (see the translation in section 7). This gives \(\bar F\) an engineering meaning: design so that impact energies stay below what the body can survive.
    3. Responsibility is shifted to system designers (road authorities, vehicle makers, commercial transport operators), while road users remain responsible for following the rules.

    Module 1 points out that a binding constraint has a shadow price. Swedish appraisal still uses a monetary value for crashes in ASEK, so in practice both coexist. The constraint guides which designs are acceptable at all, for example median barriers on fast two-way roads or 30–40 km/h where pedestrians cross. The price is used to rank projects within that set.

    Two co-equal goals A C

    The transport-policy goals adopted with Prop. 2008/09:93, Mål för framtidens resor och transporter keep one overarching goal ("to ensure a socio-economically efficient and long-term sustainable transport supply for citizens and businesses throughout the country") and replace six sub-goals with two goals of equal rank: a functional goal, accessibility, and a consideration goal, safety, environment and health. In terms of \(W\), the functional goal is Term A, and the consideration goal combines the crash, air and CO₂ parts of Term C with Term B and the constraints. Interestingly, the government explicitly declined a proposal by its analysis agency (SIKA) to call some of the goals conditions (villkor): "the goals should be co-equal" (målen bör vara jämbördiga), and the bill accepts that goal conflicts are unavoidable. So in Swedish policy only safety, through Vision Zero, has the status of a constraint. Environment and accessibility are weighed against each other. The same bill also stated that Sweden should have a vehicle fleet independent of fossil fuels by 2030.

    Term E: appraisal with ASEK E

    Trafikverket's appraisal values and methods are set by the ASEK report (Analysmetod och samhällsekonomiska kalkylvärden för transportsektorn; version 8.0 dated 2 April 2024). An ASEK working group recommends methods and values, and Trafikverket decides. Since ASEK 8.0 the unit values (value of time, crash values, emissions, noise, and so on) are published in a separate spreadsheet annex rather than in the report text. Sweden historically used a comparatively high carbon value, which gave CO₂ a large weight in Term C.

    The four-step principle: choosing \(\theta\)

    Trafikverket plans with the fyrstegsprincipen: before building, consider (1) measures that influence transport demand and mode choice, (2) measures that use existing infrastructure more efficiently, (3) limited rebuilding, and only then (4) new construction or major rebuilding. This is a procedural rule about which alternatives \(\theta\) must be in the comparison set. It keeps pricing, management and small fixes on the table. A "minimize vehicle-hours" objective with fixed demand would never generate step-1 alternatives.

    Two other Swedish standards are worth knowing. In 2008 Sweden introduced a speed-limit scale in steps of 10 km/h (30, 40, 50 … 120 km/h), which let authorities match limits to crash-tolerance thresholds, for example 40 km/h on urban streets where pedestrians cross and 80 km/h on rural roads without median barriers. And municipalities plan with a Vision Zero-based method for urban speed limits that checks each street against what an unprotected road user can survive.

    4. Signature ideas and documented weaknesses

    Signature ideas

    1. Safety as an ethical constraint. The burden of proof is on the design: show that the system is forgiving, rather than show that the deaths are "worth it". This idea spread to Norway, the Netherlands (as part of Sustainable Safety), Australia and New Zealand (Safe System), and to Vision Zero programs in many US cities.
    2. Human tolerance as the engineering norm. This is the root of today's "kinetic energy management": median barriers on fast rural roads, roundabouts instead of crossroads, 30–40 km/h where unprotected road users mix with cars.
    3. Congestion pricing that survived a referendum (section 5).
    4. Two co-equal goals and the four-step principle, which together make the comparison set wider than "which road to build".

    Documented weaknesses and criticisms

    Sweden is not uniformly sustainable
    • Car dependence outside the cores. Even in Sweden's most transit-rich region, the outer suburban municipalities make 63% of trips by car (Region Stockholm, 2025). In sparsely populated Norrland the car is the only realistic mode for most trips. Term A for rural households depends almost entirely on car generalized cost, so fuel taxes and carbon policy hit equity (the floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\)) directly.
    • Climate policy has weakened. Transport is the largest source of Swedish domestic CO₂. In 2024 the government sharply lowered the biofuel reduction obligation (reduktionsplikt) for gasoline and diesel to cut pump prices, a move that was expected to raise emissions and that critics, including the government's own climate policy council, saw as moving away from the national transport-climate target.
    • Förbifart Stockholm. The E4 Stockholm Bypass, a motorway largely in tunnel west of the city, is one of the largest road projects in Swedish history. Critics argue that it will induce car traffic and undercut the effect of the congestion tax and the region's climate goals. Supporters point to regional accessibility. It is a good exercise in Term A versus Term C with variable demand (Module 5).
    • Progress has slowed. Road deaths fell sharply after 1997, but over the last decade they have stayed broadly around 200–270 per year. Single-vehicle crashes, older road users, and pedestrian and cyclist injuries in towns (many of them falls, which are poorly captured by police statistics) are now the main problems. Vision Zero has also been criticized for focusing on deaths more than on serious injuries among unprotected road users.
    • Constraint versus appraisal. Economists have pointed out that a zero target, taken literally, could justify measures with very low benefit–cost ratios. Swedish practice resolves this informally, by using the constraint as a direction and ASEK values for ranking. That compromise deserves explicit discussion.

    5. Case studies

    The Stockholm congestion tax

    Stockholm ran a seven-month congestion-charging trial in the first half of 2006, followed by a referendum in September 2006 in which 53% of valid votes in the city were in favor. The charge was reintroduced permanently in August 2007. It is a time-differentiated cordon charge around the inner city, collected automatically by number-plate recognition. Gothenburg introduced a similar tax in 2013. According to Eliasson's overview (KTH CTS Working Paper 2014:7):

    • traffic across the cordon fell by about 20% during the trial and stabilized at about 22% below 2005 levels during charged hours after reintroduction;
    • this produced substantial reductions in congestion and travel-time variability in and around the inner city;
    • public support, initially hostile, rose to more than two-thirds of the population, and the charges came to be accepted by all political parties.

    In the terms of Module 1: the charge enters \(c_{nj}\), so some trips switch time, route, mode or destination, or are not made. The congestion part of Term C falls. Charge revenue is a transfer: it lowers traveler surplus in A and returns as \(R\) in Term E. Welfare rises through the behavior change and through \(\mu\). Module 13 works through the numbers. Transportstyrelsen's congestion tax page (Swedish) gives current charge levels and times.

    Exercise SE.1 · A cordon charge in \(W\)

    Before charging, 100,000 car trips per peak period cross a cordon. A charge of 30 SEK reduces them by 20%. Assume a linear demand curve between these points, a value of time of 150 SEK/h, and that the remaining drivers save 6 minutes each because congestion falls. (a) Compute the change in consumer surplus for drivers who keep driving, those who are priced off (rule of a half), and the revenue. (b) Compute the external congestion benefit and add €0 or a positive value for CO₂ and crash reduction. (c) With \(\mu=0.2\) and annual collection costs equal to 15% of revenue, does the charge raise \(W\)? (d) Which of these numbers would the referendum voters have seen?

    2+1 roads with cable median barriers

    Sweden's best-known engineering response to Vision Zero is the 2+1 road: a road of normal rural width rebuilt with one lane in each direction plus a middle lane that alternates between directions every few kilometers to provide safe overtaking, with a steel-cable barrier separating opposing traffic. Introduced in the late 1990s on existing 13 m roads, it was much cheaper than building a motorway. It removed most head-on crashes, which were the main source of rural deaths on such roads. Evaluations by VTI and others have reported large reductions in deaths and serious injuries; international reviews often cite reductions of roughly half or more. The barriers have costs: frequent minor collisions with the cable, maintenance work in live traffic, risks for motorcyclists, and loss of the ability to turn left at every access. These belong in Terms C and E.

    The 2+1 road is the canonical example of the Vision Zero logic in Module 11. It does not try to make drivers perfect. It makes the most likely fatal error, drifting into oncoming traffic, survivable.

    Exercise SE.2 · Shadow price of a barrier

    A 20 km rural road carries 8,000 vehicles per day and has averaged 1.2 deaths and 4 serious injuries per year. Converting it to 2+1 costs SEK 200 million, and you may assume deaths and serious injuries fall by 50%. Use the current ASEK values (from the spreadsheet annex) for a death and a serious injury, a 40-year life and the ASEK discount rate. Compute the benefit–cost ratio of the safety benefits alone. If it is below 1, what is the implied shadow price of the safety constraint \(F\le\bar F\) for this project?

    Urban speed management

    Swedish municipalities have widely adopted 40 km/h as the general urban limit, with 30 km/h near schools and in residential areas, using the 2008 speed-limit scale. The reasoning is again Vision Zero: an unprotected road user's survival probability falls steeply between 30 and 50 km/h impact speed. Compare this with the French and Spanish choice of 30 km/h as the default (see France and Spain).

    6. Where to study, and free teaching material

    Universities

    • KTH Royal Institute of Technology, Stockholm: transport planning, transport economics and traffic simulation; home of the Centre for Transport Studies (CTS), whose working papers (including Eliasson's) are free.
    • Chalmers University of Technology, Gothenburg: vehicle safety, active safety and traffic safety research, hosting the SAFER vehicle and traffic safety center.
    • Lund University: the traffic planning group at LTH (Faculty of Engineering) developed the Swedish Traffic Conflict Technique and is a center for surrogate safety research.
    • Linköping University (with VTI) also teaches traffic engineering and simulation.

    Free material

    • Laureshyn & Várhelyi (2018): The Swedish Traffic Conflict Technique: observer's manual (Lund University, English): how to measure near-misses (time to accident and conflicting speed) instead of waiting for crashes. Directly useful for Module 10 and Module 11.
    • Trafikverket Vision Zero Academy (English): background and publication list.
    • ASEK 8.0 (Swedish): the full Swedish appraisal method.
    • VTI publications (Swedish and English): free reports on safety, speed, road design and economics.
    • Trafikanalys (Swedish): official statistics on travel and injuries.

    7. Translated key passages

    The following are from the Vision Zero bill, Prop. 1996/97:137 (full text at riksdagen.se and lagen.nu). Government bills are official documents and may be quoted freely.

    The goal (summary of the bill)

    "Det långsiktiga målet för trafiksäkerheten föreslås vara att ingen skall dödas eller skadas allvarligt till följd av trafikolyckor inom vägtransportsystemet. För att nå detta mål föreslås att vägtransportsystemets utformning och funktion anpassas till de krav som följer av nollvisionen."

    "The long-term goal for road safety is proposed to be that no one shall be killed or seriously injured as a result of traffic crashes in the road transport system. To reach this goal, it is proposed that the design and function of the road transport system be adapted to the requirements that follow from Vision Zero."

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Human tolerance as the design norm

    "Människans fysiska förutsättningar att tåla yttre våld vid trafikolyckor bör vara normgivande vid utformningen av vägtransportsystemet."

    "People's physical capacity to withstand external violence in traffic crashes should set the norm for the design of the road transport system."

    Reading: this sentence is what turns Vision Zero into engineering. It defines the constraint \(F(\theta)\le\bar F\) not as a count but as a design rule. Every likely collision type must have impact energies below human tolerance.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Shared responsibility

    "Ansvaret för trafiksäkerheten bör vara delat mellan trafikanten och de s.k. systemutformarna." … "Trafikanterna har ansvar för att följa gällande trafikregler och visa hänsyn, omdöme och ansvar."

    "Responsibility for road safety should be shared between the road user and the so-called system designers." … "Road users are responsible for following the traffic rules in force and for showing consideration, judgment and responsibility."

    The bill adds that the system designers (road authorities, vehicle manufacturers, commercial transport operators) bear the ultimate responsibility for the design and function of the system.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Discussion questions
    1. Module 1 argues that every binding constraint has an implied price. Estimate the shadow price of Vision Zero from a Swedish measure with a low benefit–cost ratio (for example Exercise SE.2). Is it higher or lower than the ASEK value of a statistical life? What does that tell you?
    2. "Co-equal goals" cannot be added up. How would you rank two plans if one is better on accessibility and the other on safety–environment–health? Does Pareto comparison (Module 1, section 7) help?
    3. Stockholm's congestion tax was unpopular before the trial and popular after it. What does this imply for how cities should introduce pricing (Module 13)?
    4. Rural car dependence makes fuel and carbon taxes regressive in space. Design a policy package that keeps the carbon budget and an equity floor for rural Sweden.
    5. Is Förbifart Stockholm compatible with the four-step principle? Argue both sides.
