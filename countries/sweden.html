    Country study

    Sweden: Vision Zero and two co-equal goals

    Key terms: constraints A C E · Related modules: 3, 11, 13, 14

    In 1997 the Swedish parliament adopted Vision Zero: the principle that no one should be killed or seriously injured in road traffic, and that the road system must be designed to make this possible. Sweden is now among the safest countries in the world. It is tempting to read this as a country that turned safety into a hard limit and built everything around it. The record is more interesting. Vision Zero is an ethical framework that shapes design standards. It is not a mathematical rule that every Swedish investment must pass. Projects are still ranked with money values for crashes, politicians still choose projects for their own reasons, and the revenue from Stockholm's pioneering congestion charge was earmarked for a large motorway tunnel. This page separates the four layers (stated objectives, appraisal, design standards and outcomes) and asks where and why they diverge.

    In brief
    The question
    What did Vision Zero actually change in Swedish decisions, and what did it leave to ordinary appraisal and politics?
    Stated objectives
    Vision Zero (1997): no one killed or seriously injured. Two transport-policy goals of equal rank (2009): accessibility, and safety, environment and health. A climate target: domestic transport emissions at least 70% below 2010 by 2030.
    How projects are appraised
    Cost–benefit analysis with national unit values (ASEK), including a money value for each death and injury. Studies find that planners' rankings follow the benefit–cost ratio, while politicians' final choices do not.
    Design standards
    Human tolerance to crash forces sets the design norm: median barriers on fast two-way roads (the "2+1 road"), roundabouts, 30–40 km/h where people on foot or bike mix with cars.
    What gets built
    Road deaths fell from 541 in 1997 to 203 in 2025 (counted on a narrower definition since 2010). A congestion charge cut traffic across the Stockholm cordon by about 20%. Its revenue was earmarked for a motorway bypass.
    Lessons for a decision maker
    A stated limit changes design standards fastest, because engineers can apply it directly. It changes investment priorities only if the appraisal and the budget process are told how. Ask your analysts: does this plan meet the limit, or only rank well? Who decided the use of the revenue?
    Learning outcomes
    • State the Vision Zero decision of 1997 in its own words, and explain why it is an ethical framework rather than a binding constraint on every investment.
    • Separate the four layers of Swedish practice (stated objectives, appraisal, design standards, outcomes) and name a primary source for each.
    • Know the institutions (Trafikverket, Transportstyrelsen, VTI, Trafikanalys, the ASEK group) and which layer, and which part of \(W\), each one governs.
    • Analyze the Stockholm congestion tax and 2+1 roads as case studies with measured effects, counting each effect once.
    • Use the Swedish case to answer the course's central question: why competent planners facing the same problem produce different solutions.

    2. Four layers: objectives, appraisal, standards, outcomes

    A country does not have one transport philosophy. It has laws and plans that state objectives. It has procedures that decide which projects pass. It has standards that decide what a project looks like. And it has budgets and politics that decide what is built. In Sweden:

    Stated objectives

    Vision Zero. Government bill Prop. 1996/97:137, Nollvisionen och det trafiksäkra samhället ("Vision Zero and the traffic-safe society", May 1997) proposed a long-term goal: no one killed or seriously injured, with the road system's design and function adapted to that aim. Parliament adopted it in autumn 1997. Three features matter:

    1. The target is zero for deaths and serious injuries. It is not an "optimal" level where the marginal cost of safety equals its marginal benefit.
    2. Human tolerance is the design norm. People's physical ability to withstand crash forces should set the standard for designing roads and vehicles (see the translation in section 9).
    3. Responsibility moves to the system designers: road authorities, vehicle makers and commercial transport operators. Road users remain responsible for following the rules.

    The bill is explicit about economics. Some consultees argued that welfare economics implies an optimal number of deaths. The government replied that, from an ethical standpoint, it could not accept that people are killed or seriously injured in road traffic. It said cost–benefit analysis should not be used to define an acceptable death toll. It should be used instead to find cost-effective safety measures (section 5.1 of the bill, translated in the course's digest). The bill left the costs, and the trade-off with other goals, to a later transport-policy bill.

    Two co-equal goals. Prop. 2008/09:93, Mål för framtidens resor och transporter keeps one overarching goal: "to ensure a socio-economically efficient and long-term sustainable transport supply for citizens and businesses throughout the country". Under it sit two goals of equal rank. The functional goal is accessibility. The consideration goal is safety, environment and health. The government explicitly declined a proposal by its analysis agency (SIKA) to call some goals conditions (villkor). "The goals should be co-equal" (målen bör vara jämbördiga), and the bill accepts that goal conflicts are unavoidable.

    Climate. The 2017 climate policy framework set a target for domestic transport (excluding domestic aviation): emissions "shall fall by at least 70 percent by 2030 compared with 2010" (Prop. 2016/17:146).

    How projects are appraised

    Trafikverket's appraisal values and methods are set out in the ASEK report (Analysmetod och samhällsekonomiska kalkylvärden för transportsektorn, "analysis method and socio-economic values for the transport sector"; version 8.0, 2 April 2024). An ASEK working group recommends methods and values, and Trafikverket decides. Since ASEK 8.0 the unit values, such as the value of time, crash values, emissions and noise, are published in a separate spreadsheet annex.

    Two points matter for this page. First, ASEK puts a money value on deaths and serious injuries, exactly like appraisal manuals in countries without Vision Zero. So safety enters the ranking of projects as a price, not as a limit. Second, Trafikverket plans with the four-step principle (fyrstegsprincipen). Before building, it asks whether a problem can be solved by (1) influencing demand and mode choice, (2) using existing infrastructure more efficiently, or (3) limited rebuilding. Only then comes (4) new construction. This is a rule about which alternatives must be compared. A study that minimizes vehicle-hours with fixed demand would never generate step-1 alternatives.

    How much do the appraisals matter? Jonas Eliasson and Mattias Lundberg examined the national investment plan for 2010–2021. Planners' rankings of investments followed the benefit–cost ratio, especially for projects with low and moderate ratios. Politicians' final selections showed no sign of being affected by it. The appraisals also helped planners redesign projects to be cheaper (Eliasson & Lundberg, 2012). A later review reached a similar conclusion: the rate of return does not predict inclusion in the final program, which is decided on political grounds. It also warned that construction costs tend to be underestimated (Andersson, Hultkrantz, Lindberg & Nilsson, 2018).

    Under the hoodLimit or price? How the two coexist in Swedish appraisal

    Module 1 shows that a binding limit \(F(\theta)\le\bar F\) has a shadow price \(\nu\): the gain in \(W\) if the limit were relaxed by one unit. If Vision Zero were a strict constraint on the national plan, the plan would be chosen by maximizing \(W\) subject to the death and injury limit, and every project would face the same \(\nu\).

    Swedish practice works differently. ASEK values each avoided death at a fixed price \(p_{\text{crash}}\). Projects are ranked by benefit–cost ratio with that price. Vision Zero enters elsewhere: in the design rules that decide which variants of a project are acceptable at all, for example a median barrier on a fast two-way road. In optimization language, Vision Zero restricts the feasible set \(\Theta\) of designs. It does not set the objective of the national plan.

    This split has a consequence. Two safety measures in the same plan can carry very different implied values per life saved. A barrier required by the design rules may cost far more per death avoided than a speed camera that was not selected because its ratio looked modest. Exercise SE.2 asks you to compute such an implied value. Economists who criticize Vision Zero usually point to this inconsistency. Supporters reply that the bill never meant appraisal to decide the death toll, only to find the cheapest ways to reduce it.

    Design standards

    This is where Vision Zero bites hardest, because engineers can apply it directly. Trafikverket and the association of municipalities publish joint design rules for roads and streets (VGU). In 2008 Sweden introduced a speed-limit scale in steps of 10 km/h (30, 40, 50 … 120 km/h). This lets authorities match limits to crash-tolerance thresholds: for example 40 km/h on urban streets where pedestrians cross, and 80 km/h on rural roads without median barriers. Municipalities plan urban speed limits with a Vision Zero-based method that checks each street against what an unprotected road user can survive.

    The best-known engineering response is the 2+1 road: one lane in each direction plus a middle lane that alternates direction every few kilometers for overtaking, with a steel-cable barrier between opposing traffic. Section 7 treats it as a case study.

    3. Where the layers diverge, and why

    Vision Zero is an ethical framework, not a constraint on every investment

    The bill says that deaths are ethically unacceptable. It also says appraisal should find cost-effective ways to reduce them. It does not tell the national plan to minimize deaths, nor to reject any project that fails to reduce them. In practice the three layers split the work. Vision Zero sets the direction and the design norm. ASEK prices crashes so that projects can be ranked. Politicians choose the final plan. Quantified goals were missed: the goal for 2000 was at most 400 deaths, and 591 died. A reader who models Sweden as "maximize \(W\) subject to \(F\le\bar F\)" therefore gets the design standards right and the investment program wrong.

    Institutions: one agency for road and rail

    Trafikverket was formed in 2010 by merging the national road and rail administrations. One agency now owns the appraisal method, the four-step principle and the draft national plan for all modes. This makes cross-modal comparison easier than in countries where road and rail agencies compete for money. It does not take politics out of the final choice, as Eliasson and Lundberg showed.

    Funding rules: a congestion charge that pays for a motorway

    Legally, the Stockholm charge is a national tax. The Stockholm region argued that the money was really theirs. Regional and national politicians then brokered a ten-year infrastructure package worth around €10 billion, in which the charge revenues were earmarked for a new bypass around Stockholm (Eliasson, 2014). That bypass is Förbifart Stockholm, the large motorway tunnel west of the city. A later agreement raised the charges partly to finance a metro extension. The use of the revenue was decided by a political bargain, not by the four-step principle.

    Political power: climate goals and pump prices

    The 2017 framework commits Sweden to cutting domestic transport emissions by at least 70% by 2030. The 2024 cut in the biofuel obligation moved in the other direction, because fuel prices are politically sensitive, especially for rural households with no alternative to the car. A stated objective with a date does not bind a later government.

    History: a strong safety research tradition

    Vision Zero was formulated by safety researchers and officials, among them Claes Tingvall, whose story is told in Module 1. Sweden also had a long research tradition on speed and crashes: Göran Nilsson's power model, which links mean speed to deaths, was fitted on Swedish speed-limit changes. That tradition gave engineers numbers with which to turn an ethical statement into design rules.

    The same problem, here

    Take a problem every country on this site faces. A suburb 15 km from the city center is growing. Its one radial road into the city is jammed every morning. What would competent Swedish planners typically propose, and why? This is a stylized judgment, not a documented case.

    • The likely answer: first, measures that change demand or use the existing road better, because the four-step principle requires them to be considered: more frequent regional trains or buses, bus priority, perhaps pricing. If the road is still rebuilt, it is rebuilt to Vision Zero design rules: a median barrier, separated cycle paths, roundabouts instead of crossings. A widening of a state road can still enter the national plan.
    • Objectives: two co-equal goals (accessibility; safety, environment and health) and a transport climate target for 2030.
    • Constraints: Vision Zero acts as a limit on acceptable designs; the national plan's budget limits what is built.
    • Models: ASEK appraisal, with money values for time, crashes and CO₂, ranks the options.
    • Institutions: one agency for road and rail drafts the plan; politicians choose the final list, and benefit–cost ratios hardly predict their choice.

    In Sweden, the safety objective shapes what any answer looks like; whether the answer is road or rail is decided in the national plan, much as elsewhere. Compare the United States, Germany, Japan, Singapore, the Netherlands and France.

    4. Reading the choices in \(W\)

    Module 1 summarizes the Swedish reading as "safety as a hard constraint (Vision Zero); two co-equal transport-policy goals". The four layers refine this:

    • Safety constraints. A limit on the set of acceptable designs, plus a price \(p_{\text{crash}}\) in the ranking. Not a single binding constraint on the whole plan.
    • Two co-equal goals A C. The functional goal is Term A. The consideration goal combines the crash, air and CO₂ parts of Term C with Term B and the limits. Because the goals are co-equal, only safety, through Vision Zero, has anything like the status of a limit. Environment and accessibility are weighed against each other.
    • Term E E. A mature appraisal system (ASEK). Sweden has historically used a comparatively high carbon value, which gave CO₂ a large weight in Term C.
    • Prices. The congestion charges in Stockholm and Gothenburg change behavior. As in every country, the charge itself is a transfer: drivers pay it, the state receives it (Module 13).

    6. Successes and documented failures

    What works

    1. Safety as an ethical commitment. The burden of proof is on the design: show that the system is forgiving, rather than show that the deaths are "worth it". The idea spread to Norway, the Netherlands (as part of Sustainable Safety), Australia and New Zealand (Safe System), and to Vision Zero programs in many US cities.
    2. Human tolerance as the engineering norm. This is the root of today's "kinetic energy management": median barriers on fast rural roads, roundabouts instead of crossroads, 30–40 km/h where unprotected road users mix with cars.
    3. Congestion pricing that survived a referendum (section 7).
    4. Two co-equal goals and the four-step principle, which together make the comparison set wider than "which road to build".
    Sweden is not uniformly sustainable
    • Car dependence outside the cores. Even in Sweden's most transit-rich region, the outer suburban municipalities make 63% of trips by car (Region Stockholm, 2025). In sparsely populated Norrland the car is the only realistic mode for most trips. Term A for rural households depends almost entirely on the cost of driving, so fuel taxes and carbon policy hit the equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\) directly.
    • Climate policy has weakened. Transport is the largest source of Swedish domestic CO₂. In 2024 the government sharply lowered the biofuel reduction obligation (reduktionsplikt) for petrol and diesel to cut pump prices. The move was expected to raise emissions. Critics, including the government's own climate policy council, saw it as moving away from the 2030 transport target.
    • Förbifart Stockholm. The E4 Stockholm Bypass, a motorway largely in tunnel west of the city, is one of the largest road projects in Swedish history. Critics argue that it will induce car traffic and undercut the congestion tax and the region's climate goals. Supporters point to regional accessibility. It is a good exercise in Term A versus Term C with variable demand (Module 5).
    • Progress has slowed. Road deaths fell sharply after 1997, but since 2010 they have stayed between about 200 and 320 a year. Single-vehicle crashes, older road users, and pedestrian and cyclist injuries in towns are now the main problems. Many of those injuries are falls, which police statistics rarely capture. Vision Zero has also been criticized for focusing on deaths more than on serious injuries among unprotected road users.
    • Limit versus appraisal. Economists have pointed out that a zero target, taken literally, could justify measures with very low benefit–cost ratios, and that different measures imply very different values per life saved. Swedish practice resolves this informally (section 3). That compromise deserves explicit discussion.

    7. Case studies

    The Stockholm congestion tax

    Stockholm ran a seven-month congestion-charging trial in the first half of 2006. A referendum followed in September 2006, in which 53% of valid votes in the city were in favor. The charge was reintroduced permanently in August 2007. It is a time-differentiated charge for crossing a cordon around the inner city, collected automatically by number-plate recognition. Gothenburg introduced a similar tax in 2013. According to Eliasson's overview (KTH CTS Working Paper 2014:7):

    • traffic across the cordon fell by about 20% during the trial and stabilized at about 22% below 2005 levels during charged hours after reintroduction;
    • this produced substantial reductions in congestion and in the variability of travel times in and around the inner city;
    • public support, initially hostile, rose to more than two-thirds of the population, and the charges came to be accepted by all political parties.

    In the terms of Module 1, the charge enters the money cost \(c_{nj}\) of crossing the cordon. Some trips switch time, route, mode or destination, or are not made. The drivers who remain travel faster. That gain appears in Term A, because Term A is computed from everyone's travel times; it must not be added again in Term C (Module 1, "Where is congestion?"). Fewer car-kilometers also reduce emissions, noise and crashes, which are in Term C. The charge itself is a transfer: it lowers drivers' surplus in A and returns as revenue \(R\) in Term E. Welfare rises through the change in behavior and, if \(\mu>0\), through the revenue. Module 13 works through the numbers. Transportstyrelsen's congestion tax page (Swedish) gives current charge levels and times.

    Exercise SE.1 · A cordon charge in \(W\)

    Before charging, 100,000 car trips per peak period cross a cordon. A charge of 30 SEK reduces them by 20%. Assume a linear demand curve between these points, a value of time of 150 SEK/h, and that the remaining drivers save 6 minutes each because congestion falls. (a) For drivers who keep driving, compute the change in their surplus: the time they save minus the charge they pay. (b) For drivers who are priced off, use the rule of a half. (c) Compute the revenue. Explain why you must not add a separate "external congestion benefit": where is the time saved by the remaining drivers already counted? (d) Add a value of your choice for lower CO₂ and fewer crashes (Term C). (e) With \(\mu=0.2\) and annual collection costs equal to 15% of revenue, does the charge raise \(W\)? (f) Which of these numbers would the referendum voters have seen, and which decision about the revenue (section 3) was not part of the vote?

    2+1 roads with cable median barriers

    The 2+1 road is a road of normal rural width rebuilt with one lane in each direction plus a middle lane. The middle lane alternates between directions every few kilometers to allow safe overtaking. A steel-cable barrier separates opposing traffic. Introduced in the late 1990s on existing 13 m roads, it was much cheaper than building a motorway. It removed most head-on crashes, which were the main source of rural deaths on such roads. Evaluations by VTI and others have reported large reductions in deaths and serious injuries; international reviews often cite reductions of roughly half or more. The barriers have costs: frequent minor collisions with the cable, maintenance work in live traffic, risks for motorcyclists, and loss of the ability to turn left at every access. These belong in Terms C and E.

    The 2+1 road is the textbook example of the Vision Zero logic in Module 11. It does not try to make drivers perfect. It makes the most likely fatal error, drifting into oncoming traffic, survivable.

    Exercise SE.2 · Shadow price of a barrier

    A 20 km rural road carries 8,000 vehicles per day and has averaged 1.2 deaths and 4 serious injuries per year. Converting it to 2+1 costs SEK 200 million, and you may assume deaths and serious injuries fall by 50%. Use the current ASEK values (from the spreadsheet annex) for a death and a serious injury, a 40-year life and the ASEK discount rate. Compute the benefit–cost ratio of the safety benefits alone. If it is below 1, what is the implied value per death avoided if the road is built anyway? Compare it with the ASEK value. What does the gap tell you about whether Vision Zero is acting as a limit or a preference on this road?

    Urban speed management

    Swedish municipalities have widely adopted 40 km/h as the general urban limit, with 30 km/h near schools and in residential areas, using the 2008 speed-limit scale. The reasoning is again Vision Zero: an unprotected road user's chance of survival falls steeply between 30 and 50 km/h impact speed. Compare this with the French and Spanish choice of 30 km/h as the default (see France and Spain).

    9. Translated key passages

    The following are from the Vision Zero bill, Prop. 1996/97:137 (full text at riksdagen.se and lagen.nu; a longer English digest is on the Sources page). Government bills are official documents and may be quoted freely.

    The goal (summary of the bill)

    "Det långsiktiga målet för trafiksäkerheten föreslås vara att ingen skall dödas eller skadas allvarligt till följd av trafikolyckor inom vägtransportsystemet. För att nå detta mål föreslås att vägtransportsystemets utformning och funktion anpassas till de krav som följer av nollvisionen."

    "The long-term goal for road safety is proposed to be that no one shall be killed or seriously injured as a result of traffic crashes in the road transport system. To reach this goal, it is proposed that the design and function of the road transport system be adapted to the requirements that follow from Vision Zero."

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Appraisal finds cost-effective measures; it does not set the death toll (section 5.1)

    "Det är inte önskvärt att använda samhällsekonomiska analyser i detta syfte. Sådana analyser bör i stället främst användas som ett hjälpmedel för att identifiera kostnadseffektiva åtgärder inom trafiksäkerhetsområdet."

    "It is not desirable to use socio-economic analyses for this purpose [to find an optimal number of deaths]. Such analyses should instead be used primarily as an aid for identifying cost-effective measures in the field of road safety."

    Reading: this passage is the key to section 3. The bill does not abolish cost–benefit analysis. It changes its job, from deciding how many deaths are acceptable to finding the cheapest way to reduce them.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Human tolerance as the design norm

    "Människans fysiska förutsättningar att tåla yttre våld vid trafikolyckor bör vara normgivande vid utformningen av vägtransportsystemet."

    "People's physical capacity to withstand external violence in traffic crashes should set the norm for the design of the road transport system."

    Reading: this sentence turns Vision Zero into engineering. It defines the safety goal not as a count but as a design rule: every likely collision type must have impact energies below human tolerance.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Shared responsibility

    "Ansvaret för trafiksäkerheten bör vara delat mellan trafikanten och de s.k. systemutformarna." … "Trafikanterna har ansvar för att följa gällande trafikregler och visa hänsyn, omdöme och ansvar."

    "Responsibility for road safety should be shared between the road user and the so-called system designers." … "Road users are responsible for following the traffic rules in force and for showing consideration, judgment and responsibility."

    The bill adds that the system designers (road authorities, vehicle manufacturers, commercial transport operators) bear the ultimate responsibility for the design and function of the system.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Discussion questions
    1. Module 1 argues that every binding limit has an implied price. Estimate the implied value per death avoided of a Swedish measure with a low benefit–cost ratio (for example Exercise SE.2). Is it higher or lower than the ASEK value? What does that tell you?
    2. "Co-equal goals" cannot be added up. How would you rank two plans if one is better on accessibility and the other on safety, environment and health? Does Pareto comparison (Module 1) help?
    3. Stockholm's congestion tax was unpopular before the trial and popular after it. What does this imply for how cities should introduce pricing (Module 13)? Would it have been as popular if voters had known the revenue would fund a motorway?
    4. Planners' rankings follow the benefit–cost ratio; politicians' choices do not. Should appraisal results be binding? What would be lost if they were?
    5. Rural car dependence makes fuel and carbon taxes regressive across regions. Design a policy package that keeps the carbon budget and an equity floor for rural Sweden.
    6. Is Förbifart Stockholm compatible with the four-step principle? Argue both sides.
