    Further role models

    Other countries worth studying

    Eleven short profiles · Key terms: all · Read after Module 1

    The seven main role models do not have a monopoly on good ideas. This page collects eleven more countries and cities, each reduced to the one concept that is most worth learning, the term of \(W\) it illuminates, and a few verified resources. Where the central source is in Spanish, Italian or German, a short translated passage is included. As on the main country pages, the readings are qualitative interpretations meant to frame study, and each country has weaknesses as well as strengths.

    Spain: the walkable city and the 30 km/h default

    Concepts. Pontevedra, a Galician city of about 80,000, removed most car traffic from its historic centre after 1999 under mayor Miguel Anxo Fernández Lores. It extended traffic calming and pedestrian priority outward, with peripheral parking and an emphasis on walking distances rather than driving times. Barcelona's superilles (superblocks) group roughly 3×3 blocks of the Eixample grid. Through traffic goes around them, and the interior streets become low-speed shared space (Poblenou 2016, Sant Antoni 2018, and the "green axes" of the Superilla Barcelona programme, including Consell de Cent, opened in 2023). In 2021 Spain made 30 km/h the default limit on single-lane urban streets nationwide (Real Decreto 970/2020). Spain also has a national road-geometry standard (Norma 3.1-IC Trazado, 2016) and DGT rules and campaigns on how to drive multi-lane roundabouts, a frequent source of conflicts.

    Term of \(W\). Superblocks give land back (Term D) and cut noise and air pollution where people live (Term C), at the cost of longer car routes (a fall in the car part of Term A). The national 30 km/h rule acts on the crash part of C at almost no capital cost. Pontevedra shows the walking alternative in Term A becoming dominant once distances are short and the street is safe.

    Real Decreto 970/2020, new art. 50 of the Reglamento General de Circulación

    "a) 20 km/h en vías que dispongan de plataforma única de calzada y acera. b) 30 km/h en vías de un único carril por sentido de circulación. c) 50 km/h en vías de dos o más carriles por sentido de circulación."

    "(a) 20 km/h on roads with a single level surface shared by carriageway and footway; (b) 30 km/h on roads with a single lane in each direction; (c) 50 km/h on roads with two or more lanes in each direction." Lanes reserved for public transport do not count. The change to art. 50 took effect six months after publication (published November 2020, in force May 2021). The preamble cites the finding that the risk of a pedestrian dying when struck falls at least fivefold at 30 km/h compared with 50 km/h.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Official legal text.

    Italy: limited-traffic zones and Città 30

    Concepts. The Italian Highway Code lets municipalities create a zona a traffico limitato (ZTL), an area where access is restricted by time or by category of user and vehicle, usually enforced by camera gates. Almost every Italian historic centre has one. Milan went further: Area C (from January 2012, replacing the 2008 Ecopass pollution charge, after a 2011 referendum) charges most vehicles entering the 8.2 km² centre on weekdays. In the first months, entries fell by about a third. Bologna became the first large Italian city to make 30 km/h the general limit (introduced in 2023, with enforcement from early 2024), and the city reports falling crashes, injuries and deaths. For junction design, the ministerial decree of 19 April 2006 sets national functional and geometric standards for intersections, including roundabout types by size.

    Term of \(W\). A ZTL is a quantity instrument (a rule), Area C a price; both reduce congestion and pollution in Term C. Bologna's Città 30 is a constraint-style safety measure, and it shows the political cost of such measures: it faced national government opposition. The 2006 decree defines part of the design menu \(\theta\) for Module 9.

    DM 19 April 2006, section 4.5.1: roundabout types

    "Si considerano tre tipologie fondamentali di rotatorie in base al diametro della circonferenza esterna: rotatorie convenzionali con diametro esterno compreso tra 40 e 50 m; rotatorie compatte con diametro esterno compreso tra 25 e 40 m; mini rotatorie con diametro esterno compreso tra 14 e 25 m."

    "Three basic types of roundabout are distinguished by the diameter of the outer circle: conventional roundabouts with an outer diameter of 40 to 50 m; compact roundabouts of 25 to 40 m; and mini-roundabouts of 14 to 25 m." The decree adds that the central island may be partly traversable by heavy vehicles on mini-roundabouts of 18–25 m and fully traversable on those of 14–18 m, while compact roundabouts have non-mountable kerbs.

    United Kingdom: empirical capacity, appraisal and filtered streets

    Concepts. The UK's Transport Research Laboratory (TRL) built junction capacity models from observed behaviour at saturated sites rather than from gap-acceptance theory. Kimber's 1980 report LR942 found that roundabout entry capacity falls roughly linearly with circulating flow, with slope and intercept set by entry geometry (entry width, flare length, entry angle, inscribed diameter). These relationships underlie the ARCADY software and are still among the best-validated capacity models in the world. The UK's Transport Analysis Guidance (TAG) is a complete, free appraisal manual, from values of time to wider economic impacts. The Manual for Streets (2007) moved residential street design from highway geometry toward place-making. Low-traffic neighbourhoods (LTNs) use modal filters to remove through traffic from residential areas. Many were installed in 2020 and remain politically contested; research in London has found falls in motor traffic inside LTNs without consistent increases on boundary roads, but results vary by scheme. Do not confuse them with "LTN 1/20", the government's cycle-infrastructure design note.

    Term of \(W\). TRL models estimate the time component of Term A credibly. TAG is the closest real-world implementation of the full \(W\), including the marginal cost of public funds. LTNs trade some car access (A) for less severance, noise and crash risk on residential streets (C).

    • DfT: Transport Analysis Guidance (TAG) The UK appraisal manual; free and comprehensive.
    • TRL: The traffic capacity of roundabouts (LR942) The empirical roundabout model behind ARCADY.
    • DfT: Manual for Streets Street design for place as well as movement.
    • DfT: Cycle infrastructure design (LTN 1/20) National cycle design guidance, influenced by Dutch practice.

    Denmark: Copenhagen cycling and the Finger Plan

    Concepts. Copenhagen is, with the Dutch cities, the reference case for mass cycling in a northern climate. Close to half of residents' trips to work and education are made by bicycle, according to the city's regular Bicycle Account. The city built one-way kerb-separated cycle tracks on both sides of main streets (the "Copenhagen lane"), green-wave signals timed for cyclists, and, with neighbouring municipalities, a network of regional cycle superhighways. Denmark's Ministry of Transport has used health benefits explicitly in cycling appraisals. The Finger Plan of 1947 directed urban growth along five rail "fingers" radiating from Copenhagen, with green wedges between them. It has since become a binding national planning directive, including a station-proximity principle for large offices.

    Term of \(W\). Cycling at this scale makes Term B one of the largest benefit items. The Finger Plan is a long-run land-use policy (Module 5) that raises Term A for rail and reduces the land (D) and externalities (C) of car-based sprawl. The green wedges are an explicit land constraint \(L\le\bar L\).

    • Finger Plan (Wikipedia) History and current status of the plan.
    • Plan- og Landdistriktsstyrelsen (Danish) The national planning agency responsible for the Finger Plan directive.
    • Cycling Embassy of Denmark Industry and municipal network; English material on Danish cycling design.
    • City of Copenhagen (English) Entry point to the city's English publications, including the Bicycle Account.

    Austria: the €365 ticket and the housing–transit city

    Concepts. In 2012 Vienna cut the price of its annual public transport pass to €365, one euro a day, and kept it there for more than a decade. Annual pass holdings rose strongly, and the "365-euro ticket" became an export idea. The pass now costs €461 (digital) or €467 (card), with indexation to consumer prices planned from 2027. In October 2021 Austria introduced the KlimaTicket Ö, a single annual pass for almost all public transport in the country (€1,400 for 2026). Vienna also combines a very large municipal and subsidised housing sector (its Gemeindebau alone contains around 220,000 flats) with high-capacity transit, so that affordable housing is located where access is good. Austrian law has included a Begegnungszone (meeting zone) since 2013.

    Term of \(W\). Cheap passes lower \(c_{nj}\) for transit, which raises Term A and shifts some trips from cars (C). But they also lower \(R\) in Term E, and the multiplier \((1+\mu)\) makes this costly. Whether the trade is worth it depends on the elasticity of transit demand to price, which is usually small compared with service quality. Vienna's housing policy addresses the equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\) through land use rather than fares.

    Straßenverkehrsordnung (StVO) § 76c, Begegnungszonen (in force since 31 March 2013)

    "(2) In Begegnungszonen dürfen die Lenker von Fahrzeugen Fußgänger weder gefährden noch behindern … und dürfen nur mit einer Geschwindigkeit von höchstens 20 km/h fahren. … (3) In Begegnungszonen dürfen Fußgänger die gesamte Fahrbahn benützen. Sie dürfen den Fahrzeugverkehr jedoch nicht mutwillig behindern."

    "(2) In meeting zones, drivers of vehicles must neither endanger nor obstruct pedestrians … and may drive at no more than 20 km/h. … (3) In meeting zones, pedestrians may use the entire carriageway. They must not, however, wilfully obstruct vehicle traffic." Paragraph (1) allows the authority to declare such zones where this serves the safety or ease of traffic, especially pedestrian traffic. The rule is close to the French zone de rencontre and the Swiss Begegnungszone.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Official legal text.

    Switzerland: the clock-face timetable and the heavy vehicle fee

    Concepts. Switzerland introduced a national clock-face timetable (Taktfahrplan) in 1982: trains leave each station at the same minutes every hour. The Bahn 2000 programme, approved in a 1987 referendum, then built infrastructure to fit the timetable rather than the other way round. Main hubs were to be less than an hour apart so that trains meet at the full or half hour and passengers can transfer in all directions. The first stage was completed in December 2004, including the Mattstetten–Rothrist line, which cut Zürich–Bern from 69 to 56 minutes. Since 2001 the performance-related heavy vehicle fee (LSVA/RPLP) has charged lorries over 3.5 t per tonne-kilometre on all Swiss roads, differentiated by emission class, and much of the revenue funds rail. Switzerland introduced the Begegnungszone (20 km/h, pedestrian priority) in 2002.

    Term of \(W\). The Taktfahrplan raises Term A through reliability, short transfer waits and an easy-to-remember service, attributes in \(x\) that a speed-only appraisal misses. The rule of thumb associated with Bahn 2000 is "as fast as necessary, not as fast as possible". The LSVA is the textbook example of internalising Term C with a distance-based price and recycling \(R\) into Term E.

    • Rail 2000 (Wikipedia) Concise history, referendum, costs and travel-time results.
    • Federal Office of Transport (BAV/OFT) (German/French/Italian) Rail planning and the long-term rail expansion programmes.
    • Federal Office for Customs and Border Security (BAZG) (German/French/Italian) Collects the LSVA; tariffs and rules.
    • begegnungszonen.ch (German/French) Catalogue of Swiss meeting zones with examples and design notes.

    Colombia and Brazil: bus rapid transit, cable cars and car-free Sundays

    Concepts. Curitiba (Brazil) built the first full bus rapid transit (BRT) system from 1974 under Jaime Lerner, with trunk busways on structural axes and zoning that put density along them. Bogotá's TransMilenio (opened December 2000 under mayor Enrique Peñalosa) scaled BRT up to metro-like capacities on median busways with high-platform stations, off-board fare collection and overtaking lanes at stations. Medellín's Metrocable (Line K, 2004) used aerial cable cars, integrated with the metro, to serve steep informal hillside neighbourhoods that roads and buses served poorly. Bogotá's weekly ciclovía, dating from 1974, closes more than 100 km of main roads to cars on Sundays and holidays for cycling, walking and recreation.

    Term of \(W\). These projects are about Term A for low-income groups, which means the weights \(\omega_g\) and the equity floor. Medellín's cable cars cut travel times for some of the city's poorest residents, who walk or take buses rather than drive. BRT achieves high capacity at a fraction of rail cost (Term E). Weaknesses: TransMilenio's crowding and its failure to keep up with demand made it unpopular, which is a reminder that comfort attributes in \(x\) matter. The ciclovía mainly produces Term B and leisure value.

    • TransMilenio S.A. (Spanish) Operator site; system data and plans.
    • Metro de Medellín (Spanish) Integrated metro, tram and cable-car network.
    • ITDP: The BRT Standard Scoring system for what counts as "real" BRT, with Latin American benchmarks.
    • Curitiba Rede Integrada de Transporte (Wikipedia) History of the Curitiba system.

    South Korea: removing a freeway, reforming the buses

    Concepts. In 2003–2005 Seoul demolished the elevated Cheonggyecheon expressway, which carried about 169,000 vehicles per day, and restored the 5.8 km stream beneath it as a linear park, at a cost of about US$380 million. Traffic did not collapse, because the project was paired with a bus reform in July 2004: a colour-coded hierarchy of trunk, branch and circular routes, median bus lanes on main roads, a semi-public operating model in which the city sets routes and pays operators by service, and an integrated, distance-based fare with free transfers on the T-money smart card. Bus and subway ridership in Seoul rose in the following years.

    Term of \(W\). The freeway removal is a direct trade of road land (Term D) for park, with measured local cooling, cleaner air and higher nearby land values. It is also a natural experiment in "reduced demand", the mirror image of induced demand (Module 5). The bus reform raised Term A for transit users through reliability and integrated fares.

    • Landscape Architecture Foundation: Cheonggyecheon case study Quantified effects: temperature, air quality, ridership, land values.
    • Cheonggyecheon (Wikipedia) History and criticisms, including cost, ecology and displacement of street traders.
    • Seoul Metropolitan Government (English) Official English site, including transport policy pages.

    Australia: Safe System, Austroads and SIDRA

    Concepts. Australia adapted Vision Zero into the Safe System approach: people make mistakes, the human body has limited tolerance to crash forces, and the system (roads, speeds, vehicles, users) must be designed so that mistakes do not cause death or serious injury. It is the basis of national road-safety strategy. The 2021–2030 National Road Safety Strategy aims to at least halve deaths by 2030 on the way to zero by 2050. Austroads, the association of Australian and New Zealand road agencies, publishes the Guide to Road Design, Guide to Traffic Management and Safe System guidance, widely used beyond Australia. SIDRA INTERSECTION, developed by Rahmi Akçelik from work at the Australian Road Research Board, is a lane-based analytical model for signals, roundabouts and priority junctions, used in many countries.

    Term of \(W\). Safe System is the safety constraint \(F(\theta)\le\bar F\) expressed as a design method with speed thresholds by conflict type. SIDRA estimates delay and queues, the time component of Term A. Compare it with the empirical TRL approach (UK) in Module 9. Weakness: Australian cities remain among the most car-dependent in the OECD, so Safe System has done more for rural roads than for the land-use problem.

    • Austroads Guides to road design, traffic management and road safety. Some free downloads after registration.
    • SIDRA Solutions Documentation and technical papers for the SIDRA model.
    • Vision Zero and Safe System (Wikipedia) Overview of the family of approaches and where they have been adopted.

    India: modelling heterogeneous traffic

    Concepts. Indian urban traffic mixes cars, buses, two-wheelers, auto-rickshaws, bicycles, cycle-rickshaws, pedestrians and animals, often without lane discipline. Models calibrated on homogeneous Western traffic, which assume lane-based car-following and fixed passenger-car equivalents, fail here. Indian researchers have developed dynamic PCU values, area-occupancy measures, and simulation models in which vehicles of different widths occupy lateral space (for example the HETEROSIM line of work at IIT Madras). The Indian Highway Capacity Manual (Indo-HCM, 2017), led by CSIR–Central Road Research Institute, provides national capacity procedures. India's NPTEL programme offers complete transportation engineering courses for free.

    Term of \(W\). Every term of \(W\) depends on a model that predicts travel times and flows. India is the clearest reminder that a model must be validated on the traffic it is applied to (Module 10), and that pedestrians and two-wheelers, the majority of users, must be inside Term A and Term C, not outside the model.

    • NPTEL: Transportation Engineering I (IIT Bombay) Free video lectures covering traffic flow, demand modelling, capacity and junctions.
    • IIT Bombay: Transportation Systems Engineering lecture notes Free written notes with worked examples.
    • CSIR–Central Road Research Institute Publisher of Indo-HCM and Indian road research.

    China and Hong Kong: value capture, electric buses and traffic experiments

    Concepts. Hong Kong's MTR funds much of its rail expansion through "rail plus property": the government grants MTR development rights above and around new stations at pre-rail land values, and MTR builds or partners on housing and offices whose value the railway creates. This keeps the railway profitable without operating subsidy and puts very high density next to stations. Shenzhen converted its entire public bus fleet, 16,359 vehicles, to battery-electric by the end of 2017, helped by national and local subsidies and leasing arrangements that reduced operators' upfront costs. Chinese researchers have also contributed controlled traffic experiments, for example platoons of cars on test tracks, that show how oscillations grow along a car-following platoon. These experiments test competing theories of congestion, including Kerner's three-phase theory, against two-phase fundamental-diagram models.

    Term of \(W\). Rail plus property is value capture: the access gain in Term A shows up in land prices, and capturing it raises \(R\) in Term E. Shenzhen's e-buses cut local air pollution and noise in Term C, while CO₂ depends on the power mix. The traffic experiments improve the models behind the time component of Term A (Module 7). Caveat: Chinese cities have also built some of the largest urban road and expressway networks in the world, so China is not one coherent model.

    • WRI (2018): How did Shenzhen build the world's largest electric bus fleet? Fleet size, subsidies and leasing model.
    • Cervero & Murakami (2009): Rail and property development in Hong Kong, Urban Studies The standard academic analysis of rail plus property.
    • Jiang et al. (2014): Traffic experiment reveals the nature of car-following, PLOS ONE Open-access platoon experiment on oscillation growth.

    Discussion questions
    1. Pick two cities on this page that solved the same problem with different instrument families, for example Milan (price) and Bologna or a ZTL (rule). Compare their effects on Terms A, C and E.
    2. Vienna's €365 ticket and Switzerland's Taktfahrplan both raise transit use. Which is the better use of €100 million a year of public money? Use Term E with \(\mu\), and state your assumptions about elasticities.
    3. Cheonggyecheon and the Katy Freeway (Module 1) are mirror images. What does each say about the elasticity of demand with respect to road capacity?
    4. India and the UK both built empirical traffic models. What does each teach about transferring a model from one country to another?
    5. Rail plus property works in Hong Kong because the government owns the land. Which parts of the idea could work in a country with private land ownership?
