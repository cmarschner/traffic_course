    The value of climate action: the Quinet report (2019)

    France Stratégie · February 2019 · Terms: C constraints E · Modules 3, 14, 1

    France prices carbon in public appraisal with a valeur tutélaire, a value set by the state. The 2019 commission chaired by Alain Quinet raised it to 250 € per tonne of CO₂-equivalent in 2030 (2018 euros), rising to 775 € in 2050. More important than the number is the logic. The value is not an estimate of climate damage. It is the shadow price of a constraint: the carbon price needed to reach net-zero emissions in 2050. That is exactly the relationship between a carbon budget \(E_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\le \bar E_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) and the price \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) in Term C.

    Why it matters for W

    In \(W\), climate damage enters Term C as \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}E_{\mathrm{CO_2}}(\theta)\), and the carbon budget appears as the constraint \(E_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\le \bar E_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\). Module 1 notes that every binding constraint has a Lagrange multiplier. The Quinet report is a practical example of computing that multiplier. It asks: what price per tonne, applied consistently in all public and private decisions, would deliver net-zero emissions by 2050? The answer is used as \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\) in every French transport appraisal.

    This has three consequences for the course:

    • The price depends on the target, not on damage estimates. If the target tightens, the price rises. That is why the 2019 value is more than twice the 2008 value: France moved from "factor 4" (a 75 % cut) to net zero.
    • The price grows over time. A carbon value that grows at the discount rate (the Hotelling rule) keeps the present value of a tonne avoided constant over time. Long-lived transport infrastructure, whose emissions or savings fall mostly in later decades, is therefore not penalized by discounting.
    • It reorders transport projects. The report notes that counting greenhouse-gas emissions raises the value of rail and public-transport projects and lowers the value of motorway projects. In \(W\), that is a change in the ranking driven entirely by \(p_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\).

    Digest

    1. Mandate and method

    The commission updated two earlier French exercises: the Boiteux report on transport investment and the cost of nuisances (Commissariat général du Plan, 2001) and the first Quinet report on the shadow price of carbon (Centre d'analyse stratégique, 2008). About twenty experts from universities, research centers, international organizations, business, unions, NGOs and the administration took part. Five modeling teams supplied simulations, and open workshops were held with sector representatives.

    The commission distinguishes two logics. A cost–benefit logic, going back to Pigou and used by Nordhaus and the Stern review, sets the carbon value equal to the marginal damage of a tonne of CO₂e. A cost-effectiveness logic sets it at the value that, applied in all decisions, achieves a given target. The commission chose the second. Its reason: the target (net zero in 2050) reflects collective preferences, and the approach avoids the large uncertainty in damage estimates.

    2. Why net zero in 2050

    Climate change is a stock externality, traditionally handled through carbon budgets (cumulative emission ceilings). Because global budgets are being exhausted quickly, the Paris Agreement (2015) and the IPCC special report on 1.5 °C (2018) moved to a flow target: net-zero greenhouse-gas emissions. France adopted net zero by 2050 in its Climate Plan of July 2017, replacing the earlier "factor 4" target. The commission notes that French emissions fell about 15 % between 1990 and the late 2010s while GDP grew by nearly 50 %, and argues that decarbonization must come from lower emissions per unit of GDP, not from lower output.

    3. Three ingredients

    1. Economic theory of effort over time. Managing a carbon budget at least cost implies a carbon value that grows at the public discount rate (the Hotelling rule), so that its discounted value is constant. The commission treats Hotelling as the long-run reference but allows faster growth in the early years to smooth the jump from the old value.
    2. Model simulations. Technology-rich models give marginal abatement costs that rise as cheaper options are exhausted; macroeconomic models describe the investment and behavior changes needed. The commission judges the models reliable to about 2030 (perhaps 2040), and less so as emissions approach zero and the required changes stop being marginal.
    3. Technology foresight. Studies by the International Energy Agency and for the French low-carbon strategy (SNBC) give the potential, speed and cost of key technologies. Reaching full decarbonization requires a portfolio of technologies for residual emissions, even if they are still expensive.

    4. The trajectory

    Year20182020203020402050
    Value of climate action (€2018 per tCO₂e)5487250500775

    The 2030 anchor is set from the models. The commission considers 2030 decisive for anchoring expectations and starting a wave of low-carbon investment, and close enough for reasonably solid modeling. The 2030 value of 250 € compares with the 2008 commission's 100 €2008 for 2030 (about 110 € in 2018 money). The increase reflects lost time globally, the higher ambition of net zero, high abatement costs in agriculture, heavy industry (cement, chemicals, steel) and long-distance freight, and weak international cooperation. Between 2030 and 2050 the growth rate falls progressively to a Hotelling rule with a 4.5 % public discount rate. The 2050 value is in line with the expected costs of the key decarbonization technologies; the report gives a prudent range of 600–900 € for 2050.

    Sensitivity analysis suggests that values after 2030 could be revised down, to around 450 € in 2050, if stronger international cooperation speeds up green innovation. The commission recommends revising the trajectory every five to ten years.

    5. How the value should be used

    The report frames four questions for public policy: Is the country on track? Is it reaching the target at least cost? Are actions taken in order of merit (cheapest abatement first)? Do private actors act spontaneously, or is public intervention needed? The carbon value answers the second and third. Every action whose socio-economic abatement cost is below the value should be undertaken, and if it is not, the obstacles should be identified.

    Two indicators are recommended: the socio-economic abatement cost of an action (full cost per tonne avoided, to be compared with the value; the report asks for standardized calculation rules), and the socio-economic return of public investment projects, in which avoided emissions are valued at the carbon value. The commission also asks for co-benefits to be counted (local air quality and health, biodiversity, reduced exposure to oil shocks), for construction-phase emissions to be included, and for the discounting of climate costs and benefits to be reviewed.

    The value is not tied to one instrument. Carbon taxes, subsidies, regulation, credit and risk-sharing each have their domain and drawbacks: carbon pricing raises distributional and competitiveness concerns, regulation can be costly and slow innovation, subsidies cost taxpayers and create windfall effects. The commission recommends first aligning land, urban-planning and transport policies with the climate goal.

    6. Transport-specific findings

    Transport is described as the traditional domain of French socio-economic appraisal. On the basis of evaluations available to the commission, the climate gain per euro of project cost varies widely: it is particularly high for some rail and public-transport projects and, as the report puts it, "without surprise", counting greenhouse-gas emissions reduces the value of motorway projects. Among the low-cost abatement options listed in the key messages is the optimization of public-transport systems. Long-distance road freight is listed among the sectors with high abatement costs.

    Worked example · what the price does to a road project

    A bypass induces 40 million extra vehicle-km per year at 150 g CO₂/km, that is \(6{,}000\) t/yr. At the 2030 value of 250 €/t, the annual climate cost is \(1.5\) M€. At the 2008 value (about 110 € in 2018 money) it would have been \(0.66\) M€. Once the value follows the Hotelling rule (growing at the 4.5 % discount rate), each later year's tonne counts about as much in present value as a tonne in the first year, so a 40-year appraisal period accumulates roughly 40 times the first-year climate cost in present value. With fixed-demand modeling (no induced vehicle-km) the term would be zero. This is why demand modeling (Module 4) and carbon pricing (Module 3) must be done together.

    Key passages, side by side

    Each original excerpt is under about 40 words and is quoted from the full report (February 2019) unless noted.

    Definition

    La valeur de l'action pour le climat représente précisément la valeur monétaire que la collectivité donne aux actions permettant d'éviter l'émission d'une tonne équivalent CO2 (CO2e).

    The value of climate action represents precisely the monetary value that society gives to actions that avoid the emission of one tonne of CO₂-equivalent (CO₂e).

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Cost-effectiveness rather than damage

    Par rapport à une démarche de coûts-avantages, cette logique permet de s'affranchir des incertitudes sur l'évaluation des dommages, en se fondant sur un objectif reflétant les préférences collectives.

    Compared with a cost–benefit approach, this logic makes it possible to avoid the uncertainties in valuing damage, by relying on a target that reflects collective preferences.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    The 2030 anchor (key messages, March 2019)

    sur la base des travaux de modélisation réalisés, la commission propose, en partant de 54 € en 2018, de retenir une valeur cible de l'action pour le climat de 250 €2018 en 2030.

    On the basis of the modeling work carried out, the commission proposes, starting from 54 € in 2018, to adopt a target value of climate action of 250 €2018 in 2030.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Lower case as in the source PDF.

    After 2030: the Hotelling rule

    Entre 2030 et 2050, la croissance de la valeur tutélaire se réduit sensiblement pour se caler progressivement sur une règle de Hotelling pour un taux d'actualisation public de 4,5 %.

    Between 2030 and 2050, the growth of the shadow value slows markedly and gradually aligns with a Hotelling rule for a public discount rate of 4.5 %.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    What a higher value implies

    Une valeur plus élevée étend le champ des actions rentables pour la collectivité : à chaque instant, toutes les actions – publiques ou privées – qui coûtent moins cher que la valeur tutélaire du carbone […] devraient être entreprises.

    A higher value widens the range of actions that are worthwhile for society: at every moment, all actions – public or private – that cost less than the shadow value of carbon […] should be undertaken.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Effect on transport projects

    Ainsi, ces gains sont particulièrement élevés pour certains projets de transports ferroviaires et collectifs ou de recherche. À l'inverse, et sans surprise, la prise en compte des émissions de GES réduit la valeur des projets autoroutiers […]

    These gains are thus particularly high for some rail and public-transport projects, or research projects. Conversely, and unsurprisingly, taking greenhouse-gas emissions into account reduces the value of motorway projects […]

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).
