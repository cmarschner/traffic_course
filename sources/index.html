    Sources & translations

    Primary sources in their own languages

    9 digests · 7 languages · last checked 31 October 2026

    Much of the best transport planning practice is written in Swedish, German, French, Dutch, Japanese, Spanish and Italian, and is rarely read in the original outside its home country. Secondary English accounts often flatten what the documents actually say. This section gives English readers access to a small set of documents that the course relies on: what they say, in structured English digests, and how they say it, in side-by-side passages of original text and translation.

    Translation policy

    How these pages are made
    • From the real text. Every digest was written from the original document, retrieved from the official publisher or an official legal database. We do not translate from memory or from secondary summaries. The retrieval source and date are given on each page.
    • Machine-assisted, reviewed by the course authors. Translations were produced with machine assistance and then reviewed and corrected by the course authors for meaning and technical terminology. They are not certified or official translations. Where a term has no settled English equivalent, we give the original in brackets.
    • Original always linked. Each page links the original document. Where the original and our translation differ, the original prevails.
    • Official legal texts: translated in full or in large part. Statutes, regulations, decrees and parliamentary bills are not protected by copyright in the countries concerned (for example Sweden, Germany, France, Spain and Japan). For these we translate the operative provisions in full.
    • Copyrighted guidance and reports: digests with short quotations. For manuals, reports and municipal publications that are under copyright or whose reuse terms we could not confirm, we write an English digest in our own words and quote only short passages, each under about 40 words, with attribution.
    • Facts, not endorsements. Numbers are reported as published in the source. Where a source advocates a policy, or where a figure is the publisher's own claim, the page says so.

    Conventions

    • Original passages appear in a translation box: the original in italics, marked with its language, followed by the English translation and the note "Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted)".
    • Omissions are marked […]; our insertions are in [square brackets].
    • Obvious errors in digitized originals (OCR artefacts) are corrected silently in quotations and noted on the page.
    • Each digest states which term of the course's objective function \(W(\theta)\) it informs, using the labels A to E and constraints, as defined in Module 1.
    • British spelling is used throughout, as in the rest of the course.

    How each digest is organized

    1. Bibliographic record: original and English title, publisher, date, legal status, link to the original, reuse status and currency (whether the document has been superseded).
    2. Why it matters for W: which term of the objective function the document informs, and what bias or choice it embodies.
    3. Digest: a structured English account of the document's content, in the document's own order, with numbers and formulas where the source gives them.
    4. Key passages, side by side: original text and English translation.
    5. Where the course uses it: links to modules and country pages.

    Reading across the digests

    The nine documents were chosen because they sit at different points of the same debate. Some suggested comparisons:

    • Price or constraint? The Swedish Vision Zero bill refuses to treat road deaths as something to be priced and traded. The French Quinet report derives a carbon price from a constraint (net zero by 2050). Both are cost-effectiveness logics, applied to different terms of \(W\).
    • Narrow and broad appraisal. The Japanese road CBA manual counts three benefits. The Dutch MKBA guidance asks for every effect, priced or not, to be shown. Both rest on willingness to pay.
    • Speed as a legal default. Spain changed the national default (RD 970/2020); Germany widened the grounds on which authorities may deviate from it (StVG/StVO 2024); France defined zone types with a duty of consistent design (R110-2); Bologna used municipal orders and saw them annulled and re-adopted (Città 30).
    • Moving destinations, not vehicles. Japan's Location Normalisation Plan works on \(O_j\) in Term A, the opportunity side of accessibility, rather than on travel time.

    Sources we could not include (yet)

    Some documents named in the course plan could not be retrieved in a form we could translate from when this section was prepared (31 October 2026). We do not translate from memory, so they are listed here without digests:

    • Japan Society of Traffic Engineers (JSTE), verification manual for traffic simulation models (交通シミュレーションモデルの検証, the "clearing house" material on the JSTE simulation pages). The JSTE simulation pages returned a maintenance notice. Verification and calibration of simulation models are covered in English in Module 10.
    • SWOV, DV3 – Visie Duurzaam Veilig Wegverkeer 2018–2030 (Sustainable Safety, 3rd edition). The SWOV server refused automated retrieval. SWOV publishes an English edition, Sustainable Safety 3rd edition – The advanced vision for 2018–2030; see Module 11.
    • Légifrance blocks automated access; for the French Highway Code we used the consolidated text generated from the Légifrance API by codes.droit.org and checked the article identifier on Légifrance.

    Candidates for future digests include Germany's BVWP 2030 appraisal method handbook, the Cerema guidance on 30 km/h cities, Spain's Ley 7/2021 on low-emission zones and Italy's 2006 ministerial decree on junction design.
