    Japan's Location Normalization Plan: "compact plus network" in statute

    Act on Special Measures concerning Urban Renaissance, arts. 81, 88, 108 · Terms: A D E constraints · Modules 5, 6

    Japan's cities are shrinking and aging. Since 2014, municipalities may adopt a Location Normalization Plan (立地適正化計画, literally "location optimization plan") that designates where housing and everyday urban functions (hospitals, care facilities, shops) should concentrate, and ties those areas to public transport. Planning in this sense works on the opportunity term \(\ln O_j\) in Term A: instead of making travel faster, it moves the destinations closer to people and to transit. This page translates the core article in full and the notification rules that give it teeth.

    Why it matters for W

    The course's utility function is \(V_{nj}=\beta_t t_{nj}+\beta_c c_{nj}+\boldsymbol{\beta}_x^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{nj}+\ln O_j\). Most transport policy changes \(t\) and \(c\). A Location Normalization Plan changes \(O_j\) and the location of the people \(n\): it steers housing into areas near rail stations and bus corridors, and steers hospitals, care homes and supermarkets into designated centers. The logsum rises because opportunities are close and reachable without a car, not because anyone travels faster. This is the land-use half of "compact plus network" (コンパクト・プラス・ネットワーク), the slogan under which MLIT promotes these plans together with public-transport network plans.

    The plan also touches the other terms. Concentration lowers the per-capita cost of infrastructure and services (Term E), which is the fiscal motive for shrinking cities. Keeping housing out of disaster-prone land acts like a constraint. And because the instruments are soft — notification and recommendation, not prohibition — the plan illustrates a policy that shifts \(O_j\) slowly, over decades. That timescale is the one in the last row of the table in Module 1, section 6.

    Digest

    1. The Act and its purpose

    The Act was passed in 2002 to respond, in its own words, to the failure of Japanese cities to keep up with rapid informatisation, internationalisation, falling birth rates and aging. Its purpose clause (Article 1) lists "higher urban functions and better urban living environments" (together, "urban renaissance") and urban disaster prevention, to be achieved by special measures including, since 2014, "special city-planning measures for optimizing the location of housing and urban-function facilities based on Location Normalization Plans".

    2. What a plan contains (Article 81)

    A municipality may prepare a plan, alone or jointly with neighbors, for land within a city planning area, based on the national Basic Policy for Urban Renaissance. The plan must contain, roughly:

    1. a basic policy on the location of housing and urban-function facilities;
    2. a residence inducement area and the measures the municipality will take to attract residents there, such as better living environments and securing public transport;
    3. one or more urban function inducement areas, the induced facilities to be attracted to each (for example a hospital, a day-care center, a supermarket) and the supporting measures (land, subsidies);
    4. projects needed to locate induced facilities in those areas;
    5. a disaster-prevention guideline for housing and facilities in the inducement areas;
    6. related matters, and anything else needed to optimize location.

    Optional contents include zones for rationalising parking (to improve pedestrian convenience and safety, with prior consultation of the prefectural public safety commission), consolidated parking facilities, renewal of old urban facilities, joint management of open spaces, land readjustment in flood-prone areas, the relocation of housing and facilities out of hazard areas, guidelines for under-used land, and the management of vacant lots outside the residence area.

    The residence inducement area must be set with regard to the present and future population, land use and transport so that a good living environment is secured and public investment and administration are efficient. It may not include urbanization control areas or disaster hazard areas where residential building is prohibited. The urban function areas and induced facilities must be set so that, by attracting facilities to the right places, the location of housing is effectively optimized. Plans must be consistent with the municipal master plan and city-planning policies, based on the basic survey for city planning, and adopted after public hearings and consultation of the city-planning council. They must be published.

    3. The notification system (Articles 88 and 108)

    The plan does not forbid development elsewhere. Instead:

    • Anyone intending residential development or construction above thresholds set by Cabinet Order outside the residence inducement area must notify the mayor at least 30 days before starting (Article 88(1)).
    • Anyone intending to build an induced facility (as listed in the plan) outside the corresponding urban function inducement area must likewise notify the mayor 30 days in advance (Article 108(1)).
    • If the project would hinder the inducement, the mayor may issue a recommendation and should try to help the developer acquire land inside the area (Articles 88(3)–(4), 108(3)–(4)).
    • For housing developers in designated hazard zones (landslide, flood, sediment-disaster zones and similar) who ignore a recommendation, the mayor may publish that fact (Article 88(5)).

    The legal force is therefore informational and reputational. The main levers are the subsidies, tax measures and public facility siting that accompany the plan, which are set in other instruments.

    4. Practice and assessment

    Several hundred municipalities have published plans. The plans are typically paired with a Regional Public Transport Plan under separate legislation, so that inducement areas lie along services the municipality commits to maintain. Commentators have noted two weaknesses: residence inducement areas are often drawn very widely (sometimes covering most of the existing urbanization-promotion area), which dilutes the signal; and the notification system cannot stop development in the absence of zoning changes. These are qualitative judgments; the evidence on population shifts is still accumulating.

    Linking to W: a stylised calculation

    Take a resident \(n\) in an outer suburb with two destinations for daily shopping and health care: a center 25 minutes away by bus and a car-oriented site 15 minutes away by car. If the plan relocates a clinic and supermarket to a center 8 minutes' walk away, the resident gains a new alternative with high \(O_j\) and small \(t_{nj}\). Even if they keep driving, the logsum rises (Module 1, exercise 1.2). For a resident who cannot drive — a large and growing group in aging Japanese towns — the gain is much larger, which is why the plan also speaks to the equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\).

    Article 88(3)–(4) · recommendation and help with land, full text

    ３　市町村長は、第一項又は前項の規定による届出があった場合において、当該届出に係る行為が居住誘導区域内における住宅等の立地の誘導を図る上で支障があると認めるときは、当該届出をした者に対して、当該届出に係る事項に関し、住宅等の立地を適正なものとするために必要な勧告をすることができる。
    ４　市町村長は、前項の規定による勧告をした場合において、必要があると認めるときは、その勧告を受けた者に対し、居住誘導区域内の土地の取得についてのあっせんその他の必要な措置を講ずるよう努めなければならない。

    (3) Where a notification under paragraph (1) or the preceding paragraph has been made and the mayor of the municipality finds that the notified activity would hinder the inducement of housing and similar buildings into the residence inducement area, the mayor may make to the notifying person the recommendations necessary, concerning the notified matters, to make the location of the housing appropriate.
