    Bologna Città 30: the first large Italian 30 km/h city and its first two years

    Comune di Bologna · 2024–2026 · Terms: C B A constraints · Modules 11, 12, 6

    In January 2024 Bologna applied a 30 km/h limit to most of its streets, keeping 50 km/h only on a network of main roads. The city presents Città 30 as more than a speed limit: a program of street redesign, school streets, pedestrian squares and cycle routes with the stated goal of zero road deaths. A regional administrative court annulled the speed orders in January 2026; the city re-adopted them through a new detailed plan from 20 April 2026. This digest summarizes the city's program pages and its monitoring report for 2025, and flags what the numbers can and cannot show.

    Why it matters for W

    Bologna is a natural experiment in moving a whole city from the speed-based reading of Term A toward the safety constraint and Term C, with Term B (health from cycling and walking) as a co-benefit. It also shows the practical limits of the constraint approach in a legal system where the default urban limit is 50 km/h and each lower limit must be justified street by street. The city's two-year report gives before–after numbers for every term of \(W\) except land: crashes, deaths and injuries (C, safety constraint), cycling and bike-share use (B, and A through new options), motor traffic volumes (A and C), and NO₂ (C).

    For students, the report is equally useful as a case in evaluation method. It compares two post-policy years with two pre-policy years, excludes the COVID-19 years, and responds to the objection that tram construction caused the reductions. A careful reader will want a control group, a longer pre-period and an analysis of exposure, which Module 11 discusses.

    Digest

    1. What Città 30 is

    The city's program site describes Bologna as the first large Italian city to become a "Città 30". It insists that the policy is more than a speed limit: the city is to become quieter and more spacious, with safe and well-kept streets, new green areas, pedestrian squares and cycle tracks, calm crossings for older and disabled people and protected spaces for children in front of schools, and "more fluid" traffic for all modes. Its stated aim is to put people's health at the center and to bring road deaths to zero.

    The city argues that on Bologna's streets more than 80 % of space is taken up by cars, moving or parked, and that Città 30 will redistribute it. It describes average motor-vehicle speed in the city as around 20 km/h and states that 83 % of trips within the city are shorter than 5 km.

    2. What changes on the street

    From 20 April 2026, the city restored the 30 km/h limit — first in force from January 2024 and annulled on 20 January 2026 by a judgment of the regional administrative court (TAR) — on 258 km of streets, grouped into 47 new 30 km/h zones across all districts, in addition to earlier zones. Only some through roads stay at 50 km/h, chosen for their characteristics (many lanes, a central median), their function (mainly motor through-traffic) or the presence of separate infrastructure for vulnerable users. Examples given by the city are the ring boulevards (viali di circonvallazione, except in front of the station) and several radial axes. Outside the Città 30 area, existing limits remain (for example 70 or 50 km/h on the urban expressway).

    Signage includes more than 500 signs and about 300 road markings with the 30 symbol. Enforcement on 30 km/h streets is by police patrols; fixed speed cameras are only on 50 km/h roads. The city states its aim as "zero deaths and zero fines".

    A multi-year investment program, which the city reports has grown from an initial 24 million to over 35 million euros, funds safety works on streets, junctions and crossings, new pedestrian and school squares, footways, cycle tracks and cycle parking. According to the 2025 report, about 18.9 million euros of works had been completed by early 2026, 6.5 million were under way, 7.7 million in design and 2.1 million still to be programmed. A "phase two" concentrates on 100 speed-moderation and safety interventions, especially at pedestrian and cycle crossings. The construction of Bologna's tram lines is also used to rebuild streets on Città 30 principles.

    3. The two-year results reported by the city

    The 2025 report compares 2024–2025 (the first two years of Città 30) with 2022–2023. On the city's streets, excluding motorways and the ring road (tangenziale):

    Indicator2022–20232024–2025Change
    Deaths3922−43.6 %
    Injured4,8484,500−7.2 %
    Crashes5,6534,944−12.5 %

    The city values the reduction in social crash costs at almost 66 million euros, using Ministry of Transport parameters updated to 2025. For 2025 alone, compared with the 2022–2023 average: crashes −13 % (2,457 against 2,827), deaths −38 % (12 against an average of 19.5), injured slightly under −5 %, "red-code" (life-threatening) emergency calls −22 % compared with 2023, motor traffic on the cordon count stations −9 % (about 205,000 against 226,000 vehicles per average weekday), cycle counts +19 %, bike-share trips +100 % (3.69 million in 2025), and annual mean NO₂ at the urban traffic station −24 % (31 against 41 µg/m³).

    The report also discloses less favorable facts. Deaths in 2025 (12) were higher than in 2024 (10). Pedestrian deaths rose from zero in 2024 to seven in 2025. Of the twelve fatal crashes in 2025, nine occurred on roads that had kept the 50 km/h limit, which the city reads as support for lower speeds. Excessive speed was the most frequent violation recorded by police in crashes (39.6 %), followed by failure to give way (19.6 %). Only three fines were issued for exceeding 30 km/h in 2025, which the city explains by the legal requirements for speed checks (signed warning 80 m before the patrol, and suitable streets).

    4. The city's arguments in its FAQ

    The FAQ restates the survival argument (about 9 in 10 pedestrians survive a collision at 30 km/h, about 5 in 10 at 40 km/h, and about 1 or 2 in 10 at 50 km/h), cites a 2024 study from the National Technical University of Athens of more than forty European 30 km/h cities (average reductions of 23 % in crashes, 37 % in deaths and 38 % in injuries), and argues that 30 km/h applies at night as well because night traffic is faster and noise matters more. It also claims that 30 km/h is close to the speed that maximizes urban road capacity. That last claim depends on assumptions about headways and junction control and should be examined against Module 7; we report it as the city's claim, not as established fact.

    5. How to read the evidence

    • Small numbers. With about 20 deaths a year, a change from 39 to 22 over two years is large in percentage terms but subject to considerable random variation and regression to the mean.
    • Exposure. Cycling rose by about 19 % and motor traffic fell by about 9 %. Crash counts therefore mix risk per kilometer with exposure; the city itself notes that cyclists involved in crashes rose about 20 %, in line with cycle volumes.
    • Confounders. Tram construction sites and national trends could explain part of the change. The city answers that 2024, with fewer construction sites, showed better results than 2025, and that the reduction was city-wide. A comparison with the thirteen other large Italian cities, using ISTAT data for 2023–2024, is cited as supporting evidence.
    • Legal fragility. The TAR judgment of January 2026 shows that a constraint adopted by municipal order can be undone by courts when national law treats 50 km/h as the default. Compare Spain, where the default itself was changed (see the RD 970/2020 digest), and Germany's 2024 reform (see the StVG/StVO digest).

    salvare 17 vite umane sulle strade di Bologna (da 39 a 22 morti nel confronto tra i due bienni, pari al -43,6%)

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). "Lives saved" is the city's causal interpretation of a before–after difference.
