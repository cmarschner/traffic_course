    The Vision Zero bill: Nollvisionen och det trafiksäkra samhället (1997)

    Government bill (proposition) 1996/97:137 · Ministry of Transport and Communications · Terms: constraints C · Modules 11, 1, 14

    This is the document in which a government first wrote down that it would not accept an "optimal" number of road deaths. The bill asked the Riksdag to adopt a long-term goal that nobody should be killed or seriously injured in road traffic, and to adapt the design of the road system to that goal. It is the origin of the safety constraint \(F(\theta)\le\bar F\) in the course's objective function.

    Why it matters for W

    The course's welfare function treats safety in two possible ways. One is a price: crash harm enters Term C as \(p_{\text{crash}}E_{\text{crash}}(\theta)\), valued with a value of a statistical life. The other is a constraint, \(F(\theta)\le\bar F\), with \(\bar F\to 0\) in the long run. This bill is the clearest primary source for the second approach. Three features make it central to the course:

    • It rejects the optimum-number-of-deaths reading of cost–benefit analysis. Some consultees argued that welfare economics implies an optimal number of casualties. The Government replied that it could not share that conclusion and that cost–benefit analysis should instead be used to find cost-effective safety measures. In the language of Module 1, the safety target is a constraint, and appraisal is used to meet it at least cost. That is a cost-effectiveness logic, not a cost–benefit one.
    • It moves responsibility from the road user to the system designer. The bill argues that the human body's tolerance to kinetic energy, not driver behavior, should set the design standard. That is the conceptual root of Safe System design in Module 11 and Module 12.
    • It ties speed to infrastructure quality. Speed limits are to follow from what the road and vehicle can make survivable. That links Term A (travel time) to the safety constraint through the design of the road itself.

    The bill is also an honest record of disagreement. The consultation responses it summarizes include critics who called the goal unrealistic or the design principle absurd. That debate is still the right one to have when you choose between a price and a constraint.

    Digest

    1. The proposal

    The bill contains a single proposal for a Riksdag decision: that the long-term road-safety goal be that nobody is killed or seriously injured as a result of traffic crashes in the road transport system ("Vision Zero"), and that the design and function of the road transport system be adapted to the requirements that follow from it. Everything else in the bill is either the Government's assessment (bedömning), which guides agencies but is not a decision of the Riksdag, or a description of the problem.

    2. The previous goals and the problem

    The Riksdag's earlier road-safety goals dated from 1982 and were reconfirmed in 1988 and 1993. They asked for continuing reductions in the number killed and injured, in the risk for every road-user group, and a faster reduction for unprotected road users, with special attention to children. The Swedish Road Administration, the National Police Board and the Swedish Association of Local Authorities had quantified the first goal as at most 400 killed and 3,700 seriously injured in the year 2000.

    The bill frames road crashes as a public-health problem, citing the WHO definition of health. Over the preceding five years, on average just over 600 people had been killed and between 60,000 and 80,000 injured per year. Between 12,000 and 15,000 were admitted to hospital each year. The bill reports a Lund University estimate that crashes in 1995 cost society 14.8 billion kronor, about 1 % of GDP. A table of fatality rates per billion person-kilometers in the 1990s makes the comparison with other modes explicit: about 82 for mopeds, 77 for motorcycles, 45 for pedestrians, 21 for cyclists, 5.8 for car drivers, 3.6 for car passengers, 0.1 for buses on rural roads and 0.07 for SJ trains.

    The bill explains the difference partly by design philosophy. Rail and aviation were developed on the premise that no crash may cause death or serious injury, with professional operators and supporting technology. Road transport had instead tried to improve individual behavior. The bill also notes the road system's environmental health effects: around 700 cancer cases a year attributed to air pollution, and roughly a quarter of a million people exposed to noise above 65 dBA.

    A survey commissioned by the Road Administration found that few people accepted road deaths, but most placed the responsibility on individual road users and many opposed general measures such as lower speeds. Women were markedly more positive than men toward lower speed limits.

    3. A new long-term goal (section 5.1)

    The Government argues that the existing goal, a continuous reduction in casualties, already implies zero in its ultimate consequence. Vision Zero makes this explicit and narrows the focus: it is about death and serious injury, not about eliminating all property-damage or slight-injury crashes. The bill notes that the definition of "serious injury" needs to be clarified (taken up in section 8.3).

    Consultees were mostly supportive. Some saw a scientific and pedagogical basis for safety work, drawing an analogy with occupational safety, where serious injuries fell without loss of efficiency. Others asked what the vision would cost, called it unrealistic, or wanted cost–benefit analysis to arbitrate between road safety and, for example, health care. The Government's answer is the central passage translated below: it could not accept, on ethical grounds, that people are killed or seriously injured when moving within the road transport system, nor that road risk should be much higher than in other modes. Cost–benefit analysis should be used to identify cost-effective measures, not to define an acceptable death toll. Costs and the relationship to other transport-policy goals were deferred to the 1998 transport-policy bill.

    4. Design requirements (section 5.2)

    The Government's assessment is that human physical tolerance to external violence in crashes should set the norm for designing the road transport system. Earlier policy had tried to adapt people to the system rather than the system to people. Because the system exposes road users to forces far beyond what the body can withstand, a single wrong decision can be fatal for several people. The consequences of mistakes must be limited so that they do not lead to long-term health loss. Tolerance thresholds should be established for different road-user groups, age groups and crash types, and roads, vehicles and transport services designed so that they are not exceeded. Support systems such as rules, training, information, enforcement, rescue, care and rehabilitation should be designed with the same aim. Safety, like the environment, should be built into every organization that shapes the system.

    Not everyone agreed. The Royal Institute of Technology (KTH) argued that the principle led to absurdities such as median barriers on every road with a limit above 70 km/h. In hindsight the example is instructive: Sweden later built thousands of kilometers of 2+1 roads with median cable barriers.

    5. Shared responsibility (section 5.3)

    Until then, almost all legal responsibility lay with the road user. The bill quotes section 5 of the Road Traffic Ordinance, which required road users to take the care that circumstances demand. No comparable duty applied to those who design the system. The Government proposes a shared responsibility between road users and "system designers": public and private bodies responsible for roads, vehicles and transport services, and for rules, training, enforcement, rescue, care and rehabilitation. System designers carry the ultimate responsibility for the safety level of the whole system. If road users fail to take their share, because of lack of knowledge, acceptance or ability, or if injuries occur for other reasons, system designers must take further measures. An inquiry into how public and business responsibility could be specified was announced.

    6. Orientation of safety work and of measures (sections 5.4–5.6)

    Agencies and municipalities should start from citizens' needs and demands, encourage decision-makers to take responsibility, integrate safety into all activities that shape the system, stimulate demand for safe products and services (including in public procurement of transport), and make it easier for road users to follow rules. On measures, the bill lists four stages of prevention: reduce exposure to crash risk, reduce crash risk, reduce injury risk in crashes, and reduce the risk of long-term health loss after injury. It notes that it is often more efficient to prevent injury than to prevent the crash itself, citing single-vehicle crashes, which have many causes but few injury mechanisms. The needs of the most vulnerable road users should govern the design of streets and roads, inside and outside built-up areas. Speed is named as the most important regulating factor, and speed limits should follow from the technical standard of road and vehicle together with human tolerance: better roads and safer vehicles can justify higher speeds, provided environmental goals are not undermined.

    7. Urban speeds, cycle helmets, planning (chapter 7)

    For built-up areas, the Government's assessment is that actual speeds should in the long run be no more than 30 km/h at times and places where pedestrians and cyclists mix with motor traffic in a planned way, and that municipalities should be able to decide 30 km/h limits as the first instance. Consultees were broadly supportive in principle but split on implementation. The National Police Board warned that drivers would see a general 30 km/h limit as unnatural and that rules that cannot be enforced should be avoided. Several municipalities said streets would have to be rebuilt for the limit to be credible. Public-transport bodies feared that lower speeds would reduce bus ridership. The chapter also covers cycle-helmet use and land-use planning.

    8. Other chapters

    Chapter 6 deals with safer car traffic: driver training, quality assurance of road transport, business travel and commuting, public technology procurement and road informatics. Chapter 8 deals with rescue services and emergency care, consumer information, and a new health measure. The last point (8.3) argues that police crash statistics under-report injuries, especially among unprotected road users, and that a lay officer's "serious/slight" classification describes actual health loss poorly. The Government asks for a measure based on medical diagnosis and prognosis that captures the risk of death and of permanent disability. Sweden later adopted such a definition based on permanent medical impairment.

    Key passages, side by side

    Section 1 · The proposal for a Riksdag decision

    Regeringen föreslår att riksdagen godkänner att det långsiktiga målet för trafiksäkerheten skall vara att ingen skall dödas eller skadas allvarligt till följd av trafikolyckor inom vägtransportsystemet (nollvisionen), samt att vägtransportsystemets utformning och funktion anpassas till de krav som följer av detta.

    The Government proposes that the Riksdag approve that the long-term goal for road safety shall be that nobody shall be killed or seriously injured as a result of traffic crashes within the road transport system (Vision Zero), and that the design and function of the road transport system be adapted to the requirements that follow from this.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Section 5.1 · No "optimal" number of deaths

    Regeringen anser att det från en etisk utgångspunkt inte kan accepteras att människor dödas eller skadas allvarligt i samband med förflyttningar inom vägtransportsystemet. Det är heller inte acceptabelt att risknivåerna inom vägtrafiken är väsentligt högre än inom andra trafikslag. Regeringen kan därför inte dela den slutsats som kan dras av det resonemang som förekommer bland remissvaren, nämligen att det utifrån ett samhällsekonomiskt synsätt finns ett optimalt antal dödade och skadade i vägtrafiken. Det är inte önskvärt att använda samhällsekonomiska analyser i detta syfte. Sådana analyser bör i stället främst användas som ett hjälpmedel för att identifiera kostnadseffektiva åtgärder inom trafiksäkerhetsområdet.

    The Government considers that, from an ethical standpoint, it cannot be accepted that people are killed or seriously injured while moving within the road transport system. Nor is it acceptable that risk levels in road traffic are substantially higher than in other modes of transport. The Government therefore cannot share the conclusion that may be drawn from the reasoning found among the consultation responses, namely that from a socio-economic point of view there is an optimal number of people killed and injured in road traffic. It is not desirable to use socio-economic analyses for this purpose. Such analyses should instead be used primarily as an aid for identifying cost-effective measures in the field of road safety.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Section 5.2 · Human tolerance as the design norm

    Ett av de centrala kraven på framtidens vägtransportsystem är att konsekvenserna av de misstag och felbeteenden som kan komma att ske i trafiken begränsas så att de inte leder till långvariga hälsoförluster. Människans fysiska förutsättningar att tåla yttre våld vid trafikolyckor bör därför vara normgivande vid utformningen av vägtransportsystemets olika delar.

    One of the central requirements for the road transport system of the future is that the consequences of the mistakes and errors that may occur in traffic are limited so that they do not lead to long-term loss of health. Human physical capacity to withstand external violence in traffic crashes should therefore set the norm for the design of the various parts of the road transport system.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Section 5.3 · Shared responsibility

    Systemutformarna bör alltid ha det yttersta ansvaret för vägtransportsystemets utformning, skötsel och användning och härigenom ansvara för hela systemets säkerhetsnivå. Trafikanterna har liksom tidigare ansvar för att visa hänsyn, omdöme och ansvar i trafiken samt att följa trafikreglerna. Om trafikanterna inte tar sin del av ansvaret – på grund av t.ex. bristande kunskap, acceptans eller förmåga – eller om personskador uppstår eller riskerar att uppstå av andra orsaker, måste systemutformarna vidta ytterligare åtgärder i den mån detta krävs för att motverka att människor dödas eller skadas allvarligt.

    The system designers should always bear the ultimate responsibility for the design, maintenance and use of the road transport system, and thereby be responsible for the safety level of the whole system. Road users remain, as before, responsible for showing consideration, judgment and responsibility in traffic and for obeying the traffic rules. If road users do not take their share of the responsibility – for example because of lack of knowledge, acceptance or ability – or if injuries occur or risk occurring for other reasons, the system designers must take further measures to the extent required to prevent people from being killed or seriously injured.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Section 5.5 · Speed limits follow from road and vehicle standard

    Regeringen anser att hastighetsgränserna inom vägtransportsystemet bör bestämmas utifrån vägens och fordonens tekniska standard med beaktande av människans fysiska förutsättningar att tåla yttre våld. Ju bättre vägutformning och säkrare fordon desto högre hastigheter bör kunna accepteras, förutsatt att detta inte motverkar de miljöpolitiska målen.

    The Government considers that speed limits in the road transport system should be set on the basis of the technical standard of roads and vehicles, taking into account the human body's physical capacity to withstand external violence. The better the road design and the safer the vehicles, the higher the speeds that can be accepted, provided this does not run counter to environmental-policy goals.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Section 7.1 · 30 km/h where people mix with cars

    Inriktningen bör vara att den faktiska hastigheten inom tättbebyggt område på sikt bör uppgå till högst 30 kilometer i timmen vid tider och platser där gående och cyklister på ett planlagt sätt blandas med biltrafik. Som en del i denna inriktning bör kommunerna få utökade befogenheter att som första instans besluta en hastighetsgräns om 30 kilometer i timmen inom tättbebyggt område.

    The direction should be that the actual speed in built-up areas should, in the long run, be at most 30 kilometers per hour at times and places where pedestrians and cyclists are mixed with car traffic in a planned way. As part of this direction, municipalities should be given extended powers to decide, as the first instance, a speed limit of 30 kilometers per hour within built-up areas.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Note "actual speed" (faktiska hastigheten): the goal is about driven speeds, not just posted limits.

    Reading the bill critically

    The bill is a political document, and it leaves the hard questions open. It does not say how fast \(\bar F\) should fall, what the measures will cost, or how Vision Zero should be traded against accessibility and environmental goals. It explicitly defers those questions to the 1998 transport-policy bill. In the vocabulary of Module 1, it states the constraint and the design principle but not the shadow price. Students should compare it with how the Swedish Transport Administration's appraisal practice (ASEK values) still monetizes casualties for project ranking.
