    The French street hierarchy in law: Code de la route, article R110-2

    Regulatory code · current version since 25 April 2022 · Terms: A C constraints · Modules 12, 9, 11

    French law defines a graded family of street regimes: the pedestrian area (aire piétonne), the shared-space "meeting zone" (zone de rencontre, 20 km/h, pedestrian priority) and the 30 km/h zone (zone 30). Each definition ties a speed to a priority rule and to a duty to lay out the street "consistently" with that speed. This page translates the definitions in full, together with the articles that bring them into force and the roundabout priority rule.

    Why it matters for W

    Most of the course treats speed and space as decision variables in \(\theta\). French law shows how a state can turn them into a small menu of legally defined street types, each a bundle of a maximum speed, a priority rule, rules on two-way cycling, and a design obligation:

    Two features matter for the course. First, the definitions are outcome-oriented. The zone must be "laid out consistently with the applicable speed limit" (aménagé de façon cohérente avec la limitation de vitesse applicable), and R411-3-1 and R411-4 make the rules applicable only once the mayor or other police authority has found that the layout is consistent. A sign alone is not supposed to be enough. That is the French preference for passive, self-enforcing design discussed in Module 1, section 6. Second, two-way cycling on one-way streets becomes the default in both zones. Every one-way street in a zone 30 adds cycling alternatives, which the logsum in Term A values even for people who rarely use them.

    Digest

    1. What article R110-2 is

    R110-2 is the definitions article of the regulatory part of the French Highway Code. It defines fifteen terms that are used throughout the code: built-up area (agglomération), pedestrian area, stopping, cycle lane, hard shoulder, motorway link road, roundabout, carriageway, intersection, cycle track, parking, traffic lane, greenway (voie verte), meeting zone and 30 zone. Several of the definitions carry substantive rules: maximum speeds, priority, and who may use a space.

    2. The three calm-traffic regimes

    Pedestrian area. A section or set of sections of road inside a built-up area, excluding roads designated as major traffic routes (routes à grande circulation), assigned to pedestrians either temporarily or permanently. Only vehicles needed to serve the zone may drive there, at walking pace, and pedestrians have priority over them. Cyclists may ride under conditions set elsewhere in the code. The police authority defines the perimeter and the rules inside it (R411-3).

    Meeting zone (zone de rencontre). A section or set of sections inside a built-up area open to all users. Pedestrians may walk on the carriageway (but not stand still on it) and have priority over vehicles. Vehicles are limited to 20 km/h. All carriageways are two-way for cyclists and for users of light motorized personal vehicles unless the police authority decides otherwise. Entries and exits are signed, and the whole zone must be laid out consistently with the 20 km/h limit. The French meeting zone follows Swiss and Belgian precedents; it plays the role of the Dutch woonerf and the German verkehrsberuhigter Bereich, but at 20 km/h rather than walking pace.

    30 zone. A section or set of sections open to all users, where vehicles are limited to 30 km/h, with the same two-way cycling default, signed entries and exits, and consistent layout. Unlike the meeting zone, the 30 zone is not restricted to built-up areas by its definition, and it gives pedestrians no special right to use the carriageway.

    3. How a zone comes into force

    For meeting zones (R411-3-1) and 30 zones (R411-4), the procedure has two steps. First, the authority holding traffic police powers (usually the mayor) fixes the perimeter and the layout by order (arrêté), after consulting the road managers concerned and, on a major traffic route, with the binding opinion of the prefect. Second, a further order makes the traffic rules applicable, "noting the consistent layout of the zones and the installation of the corresponding signs". The legal sequence therefore runs from design to rule, not the other way round.

    4. Speed in built-up areas and the "ville 30"

    The default urban limit is 50 km/h (R413-3). It may be raised to 70 km/h on sections where access points and pedestrian crossings are few and protected, by order of the police authority. Since the 2010s many French cities, Paris among them, have extended 30 km/h to most of their streets (the ville 30). They use the limit-setting powers of the mayor rather than creating one giant 30 zone, which is why R110-2's zone definitions and the general speed limits must be read together. Cerema, the national technical agency, publishes guidance on designing such cities.

    5. Roundabouts

    R110-2 defines a carrefour à sens giratoire as a junction with a physically uncrossable central island, ringed by a one-way carriageway (counter-clockwise, "mis à sens unique par la droite"), which several roads join, and announced by specific signs. The definition also allows roundabouts with a physically crossable (mountable) central island, which drivers may drive over when the size of their vehicle makes it necessary; this is the legal basis of the mini-roundabout. R415-10 gives priority to traffic already on the ring. France adopted this "priority to the ring" rule in the 1980s; under the older general priority-to-the-right rule, entering traffic had priority and large circles tended to lock up at high flows. The definition matters for Module 9: the capacity models used in France (such as Cerema's GIRABASE) assume this priority rule.

    6. Limits of the legal approach

    The law names the regimes but does not say where they should be used. The choice between a meeting zone, a 30 zone and an ordinary street is a planning decision, informed by Cerema guidance and local mobility plans. The "consistent layout" requirement is not specified numerically, so how strictly it is applied varies between authorities. These definitions are best understood as a legal toolbox for passive control, not as a planning method.

    R110-2 · aire piétonne (pedestrian area)

    aire piétonne : section ou ensemble de sections de voies en agglomération, hors routes à grande circulation, constituant une zone affectée à la circulation des piétons de façon temporaire ou permanente. Dans cette zone, sous réserve des dispositions des articles R. 412-43-1 et R. 431-9, seuls les véhicules nécessaires à la desserte interne de la zone sont autorisés à circuler à l'allure du pas et les piétons sont prioritaires sur ceux-ci. Les entrées et sorties de cette zone sont annoncées par une signalisation.

    pedestrian area: a section or set of sections of road within a built-up area, excluding major traffic routes, forming a zone assigned to pedestrian traffic on a temporary or permanent basis. In this zone, subject to the provisions of articles R. 412-43-1 and R. 431-9, only vehicles needed to serve the interior of the zone are authorized to move, at walking pace, and pedestrians have priority over them. The entries to and exits from this zone are announced by signs.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). R. 431-9 concerns cyclists; R. 412-43-1 concerns motorized personal mobility devices (e-scooters and similar).

    R110-2 · carrefour à sens giratoire (roundabout)

    carrefour à sens giratoire : place ou carrefour comportant un terre-plein central matériellement infranchissable, ceinturé par une chaussée mise à sens unique par la droite sur laquelle débouchent différentes routes et annoncé par une signalisation spécifique. Toutefois, les carrefours à sens giratoire peuvent comporter un terre-plein central matériellement franchissable, qui peut être chevauché par les conducteurs lorsque l'encombrement de leur véhicule rend cette manoeuvre indispensable ;

    roundabout: a square or junction with a physically uncrossable central island, surrounded by a carriageway made one-way, keeping to the right, onto which several roads lead, and announced by specific signs. However, roundabouts may have a physically crossable central island, which drivers may drive over when the size of their vehicle makes this maneuver indispensable;

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). "One-way, keeping to the right" means anticlockwise circulation in right-hand traffic.

    R110-2 · zone de rencontre (meeting zone)

    zone de rencontre : section ou ensemble de sections de voies en agglomération constituant une zone affectée à la circulation de tous les usagers. Dans cette zone, les piétons sont autorisés à circuler sur la chaussée sans y stationner et bénéficient de la priorité sur les véhicules. La vitesse des véhicules y est limitée à 20 km/h. Toutes les chaussées sont à double sens pour les cyclistes, les conducteurs de cyclomobiles légers et les conducteurs d'engins de déplacement personnel motorisés, sauf dispositions différentes prises par l'autorité investie du pouvoir de police. Les entrées et sorties de cette zone sont annoncées par une signalisation et l'ensemble de la zone est aménagé de façon cohérente avec la limitation de vitesse applicable.

    meeting zone: a section or set of sections of road within a built-up area forming a zone assigned to traffic by all users. In this zone, pedestrians are permitted to move on the carriageway without stopping there and have priority over vehicles. The speed of vehicles is limited to 20 km/h. All carriageways are two-way for cyclists, riders of light cyclomobiles and riders of motorized personal mobility devices, unless the authority vested with police powers decides otherwise. The entries to and exits from this zone are announced by signs, and the whole zone is laid out consistently with the applicable speed limit.

    R415-10 · priority at roundabouts

    Tout conducteur abordant un carrefour à sens giratoire est tenu, quel que soit le classement de la route qu'il s'apprête à quitter, de céder le passage aux usagers circulant sur la chaussée qui ceinture le carrefour à sens giratoire.
