    The Dutch general guidance for social cost–benefit analysis (MKBA), 2013

    CPB Netherlands Bureau for Economic Policy Analysis and PBL Netherlands Environmental Assessment Agency · 2013 · Terms: all of \(W\), especially A E and the weights \(\omega_g\) · Modules 14, 2

    The Netherlands is one of the few countries where a single, short, government-endorsed text says how every social cost–benefit analysis (maatschappelijke kosten-batenanalyse, MKBA) should be done, whatever the policy field. Its welfare-economic foundations are the same as those of the course's objective function: willingness to pay, a broad concept of welfare including unpriced effects, and a strict separation between efficiency and distribution. Reading it alongside Module 14 shows which parts of \(W\) a standard appraisal already contains and which it deliberately leaves out.

    Why it matters for W

    The MKBA guidance and the course's \(W\) share a foundation. Both measure welfare change as willingness to pay, both include effects without market prices (nature, safety, health, the living environment), and both compare a policy alternative with a carefully defined reference case. Three points of contact are especially useful:

    • Distribution is shown, not weighted. The guidance says MKBA places no value on who gains or loses, but can show distributional effects. In \(W\) that is the choice \(\omega_g=1\) for all groups, plus a separate table of effects by group. The course's equity weights and equity floor \(A_g\ge A_{\min}\) are a deliberate departure from this convention.
    • The reference case is the most likely future without the policy. The effect of a plan is "policy alternative minus reference alternative". In \(W\), this is \(W(\theta)-W(\theta_0)\), with \(\theta_0\) including existing policy and committed measures. The guidance warns against putting too much intended policy into \(\theta_0\).
    • Indirect effects count only where there is market failure. Effects that spread to other markets add to welfare only if those markets are distorted; otherwise they are transfers of the direct effect and would be double-counted. This is the same reasoning as the rule of a half and the double-counting warning in Term C (Module 2, Module 1).

    Digest

    1. Purpose and scope

    The guidance presents MKBA as an information instrument for ex-ante policy choices. It gives an overview of a measure's effects, risks and uncertainties, and the resulting advantages and disadvantages for society as a whole. By quantifying and valuing as many effects as possible in euros, it shows the effect of a measure on social welfare as the balance of benefits minus costs. That balance includes effects for which no market prices exist.

    The authors stress that MKBA is useful beyond a go/no-go verdict. Its questions ("What is the problem? How does it develop if we do nothing? What are the possible solutions?") help to make policy debates more businesslike. MKBA is not appropriate where economic efficiency is irrelevant, such as questions of human dignity. Where effects cannot be measured, the lack of evidence is not a defect of the instrument but a reason for more empirical research. Using MKBA merely as a "way of thinking" does not produce an MKBA and should not be called one.

    The 2013 guidance replaced the transport-specific OEI guide of 2000 as the general framework for all policy fields. It sets the minimum requirements every MKBA must meet; sector-specific work guides add detail.

    2. The role of MKBA in decision-making

    Decision-making runs from problem identification through goals and measures to assessment and political choice. MKBA is usually applied at the assessment stage but can help earlier. Three lessons are drawn from Dutch experience: the problem analysis must match the measures being assessed; stakeholders should be involved early because MKBA is often perceived as a black box; and quality assurance is needed, because guidance alone does not produce good MKBAs.

    3. Welfare-economic foundations

    Welfare changes are expressed as people's willingness to pay for a measure's effects. If the costs are lower than the willingness to pay for the effects, the measure raises welfare. The concept of welfare is broad and includes things people value that are not traded on markets, such as the quality of the living environment, health and safety. "Markets" is interpreted widely to include missing markets. Distribution of welfare is treated as primarily a political matter: if distributional effects are important, an MKBA shows them (for example as purchasing-power changes by income group) without attaching a value judgment.

    4. The eight steps

    1. Problem analysis — what bottleneck or opportunity exists, which policy goal follows, which solutions are promising.
    2. Reference alternative (nulalternatief) — the most likely development without the policy, including existing policy and committed measures. Effect = policy alternative − reference alternative.
    3. Policy alternatives — the smallest coherent set of measures that is technically and legally feasible and plausibly linked to the problem. Packages should be unbundled so that unprofitable parts do not "free-ride" on profitable ones.
    4. Effects and benefits — identify, quantify and value effects.
    5. Costs — resources used, one-off or recurring, only those additional to the reference alternative.
    6. Variants and risk analysis — identify the main uncertainties and risks and analyze their consequences.
    7. Overview of costs and benefits — discount everything to a common base year, compute the balance, and show all effects including those not quantified or monetized.
    8. Presentation — relevant, accessible and clear; transparent and reproducible; with an interpretation of what decision-makers can learn.

    5. Measuring and valuing effects

    Accepted methods for estimating effects are behavioral models, business cases, empirically estimated price elasticities, experiments and key ratios. Methods must be scientifically verified and regularly revalidated, aimed at the effect in question, more detailed the more important the effect, and accompanied by an analysis of knowledge uncertainty. Where off-the-shelf models do not fit the question (the guidance mentions labor-market and education policy), a tailored randomised or natural experiment is preferable.

    Valuation should follow observed market prices where possible. For unpriced effects, revealed-preference methods (hedonic pricing, travel-cost method) use observed behavior in related markets but may capture only part of the effect. Stated-preference methods ask people directly and can cover almost anything, including existence values, but stated answers may differ from behavior.

    6. Discounting and risk

    The guidance follows the cabinet decision of 2011: a real risk-free discount rate of 2.5 % plus a general macroeconomic risk premium of 3 %, giving 5.5 % for effects that are correlated with the business cycle. Project-specific risk premiums are preferred in principle but rarely feasible. Following the advice of the working group on the long-term discount rate, a lower premium of 1.5 % instead of 3 % applies to project effects that meet specific conditions, the first being that they are negative external effects caused or removed by the project. The guidance illustrates the stakes: a welfare gain of 100 euros in 25 years is worth 54 euros today at 2.5 % but 26 euros at 5.5 %; after a hundred years it is worth 8 euros at 2.5 % and less than half a euro at 5.5 %. It recommends analyzing flexible alternatives (postponement, phasing) to deal with uncertainty, an option-value logic.

    7. Direct, indirect and external effects

    Direct effects occur in the market where the policy intervenes (for a road project, the transport market). Indirect effects are the knock-on effects in other markets (labor, housing, business). The distinction has no welfare-economic significance in itself but helps to delimit markets and avoid double counting. Indirect effects matter for welfare only if they are substantial and occur in markets with market failure; then they are "additional indirect effects". In transport appraisals, external effects such as emissions, noise and safety are the typical unpriced direct effects that need shadow prices.

    MKBA and W compared
    Element of \(W\)Treatment in the MKBA guidance
    Term A (logsum consumer surplus)Willingness to pay for direct effects in the transport market; behavioral models required. In transport practice typically approximated with the rule of a half.
    Term B (health)Included as an unpriced effect to be valued where evidence allows.
    Term C (externalities)Explicitly included; valued with shadow prices from sector guidance.
    Term D (land)Included through resource cost of land and through indirect effects on land and housing markets where they add welfare.
    Term E (net public cost, \(\mu\))Costs relative to the reference alternative; the general guidance does not prescribe a marginal cost of public funds.
    Weights \(\omega_g\), equity floorNot used. Distribution shown separately, without valuation.
    Hard constraintsNot part of MKBA; policy targets enter through the definition of alternatives.

    Key passages, side by side

    Each original excerpt is under about 40 words.

    Distribution is shown, not valued

    De MKBA heeft geen waardering voor de mate waarin verschillende groepen voor- of nadeel ondervinden van een maatregel, maar kan deze verdelingseffecten wel in beeld brengen.

    MKBA places no value on the extent to which different groups benefit or lose from a measure, but it can show these distributional effects.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Willingness to pay as the measure of welfare

    Welvaartsveranderingen van beleidsmaatregelen komen tot uitdrukking in de betalingsbereidheid van mensen voor de effecten ervan.

    The welfare changes caused by policy measures are expressed in people's willingness to pay for their effects.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    The reference alternative

    Het nulalternatief […] is de meest waarschijnlijk te achten ontwikkeling die zal plaatsvinden op het voor de MKBA relevante terrein in het geval dat de te beoordelen maatregel niet wordt uitgevoerd.

    The reference alternative […] is the development that is to be considered most likely to occur in the field relevant to the MKBA if the measure under assessment is not carried out.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    No free-riding inside packages

    Definieer de beleidsalternatieven niet zo ruim dat onrendabele deelmaatregelen meeliften op de rendabele delen van het beleidsalternatief.

    Do not define policy alternatives so broadly that unprofitable component measures ride along on the profitable parts of the policy alternative.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    When indirect effects count

    Relevante indirecte effecten omvatten in ieder geval de doorwerking op markten met significante gevolgen voor de welvaart. Dit is het geval als een indirecte effect substantieel is en optreedt op een markt waar sprake is van marktfalen.

    Relevant indirect effects include in any case knock-on effects in markets with significant consequences for welfare. This is the case when an indirect effect is substantial and occurs in a market where there is market failure.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).

    Discount rate (2011 cabinet decision, as cited in 2013)

    In het meest recente kabinetsbesluit (uit 2011) wordt de reële risicovrije discontovoet vast gesteld op 2,5% en de algemene risico-opslag (de macro risicopremie) op 3%.

    In the most recent cabinet decision (from 2011), the real risk-free discount rate is set at 2.5 % and the general risk premium (the macro risk premium) at 3 %.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted). Superseded by later cabinet decisions; see "Currency" above.

    A way of thinking is not an MKBA

    Het gebruik van de MKBA als denkkader leidt echter niet tot een MKBA en mag zo ook niet worden genoemd.

    Using MKBA as a framework for thinking does not, however, produce an MKBA and may not be called one.

    Translated by the course authors (machine-assisted).
