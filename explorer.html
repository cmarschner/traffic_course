Things to try

  1. Start with Classic traffic engineering. Widening wins. Switch the horizon to long run without changing anything else: induced demand erodes most of its time savings.
  2. Choose Sweden. Widening is ruled out by the Vision Zero constraint, not by its price. Untick the constraint and see whether the crash price alone would have excluded it.
  3. Choose Singapore, then drag the land value \(r\) down to zero. Which plan does a land-abundant city prefer?
  4. In any long-run preset, set the carbon value to €1,000/t. At corridor scale CO₂ rarely decides the ranking. Crashes, health and land usually matter more. Why, and what does that imply for climate policy at network scale?
  5. Pricing (D) has a large revenue term. Why does that revenue not count as a benefit in itself? Look at Terms A and E together and see Module 13.

The model behind the explorer

Illustrative numbers

The physical effects of each plan are invented but plausible teaching values for a single corridor. The presets caricature each tradition's emphasis as described in Module 1. They are not official national parameter values. For the real values and how they are derived, see Module 2 (time), Module 3 (externalities) and Module 14 (national appraisal frameworks).

For each plan the explorer computes, in M€ per year:

  • A = VTTS × net user benefit in person-hour equivalents − tolls and fares paid. The short-run figure assumes fixed demand. The long-run figure includes induced traffic, rerouting and mode shift, with benefits to new users counted by the rule of half (Module 2).
  • B = health value × change in walking and cycling km.
  • C = −(carbon value × CO₂ at 170 g/vehicle-km + air/noise value × vehicle-km + KSI value × change in KSI).
  • D = −\(r\) × change in land used for roads.
  • E = −(1 + \(\mu\)) × (annualized capital + operating cost − revenue).
