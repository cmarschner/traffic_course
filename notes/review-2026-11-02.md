## Verdict in one line

The premise is worthwhile, but the originality must be framed as a **synthesis and a way of
organizing the field**, not as a new theory or a correction of a profession that ignores welfare.

## Correctness

- [ ] **Congestion is counted twice.** Term A already uses everyone's realized (equilibrium)
  travel times, so the delay each driver imposes on others is already inside the others'
  logsums. Adding the marginal external delay q·t'(q) in Term C counts it again.
  q·t'(q) is the right *toll* (Pigou/Walters/Vickrey), not an extra loss to subtract.
  Affected places in `modules/m01-objective.html`: Term C table row "congestion",
  the "Congestion is strongly convex" paragraph ("Only this external part belongs in C"),
  the congestion shape chart caption, the "eight objectives" table row 1
  ("external congestion in C"), Exercise 1.3. Check Module 3 and Module 13 for the same.
  The same logic applies to transit crowding and possibly to severance (if pedestrians'
  delays are already in A).
- [ ] **Land: D versus acquisition cost in K.** If K includes buying land for a new road,
  and D charges r·L for the same land, it is counted twice. State the rule: land already
  in use → D (opportunity cost); new land → either in K or in D, not both.
- [ ] **Taxes and tolls as transfers:** check that every module treats them consistently
  (Module 1 "Why revenue is not a benefit" is right; check Modules 2, 13, 14).

## Framing and positioning

- [ ] **Do not straw-man the profession.** Module 1 says "the objective most
  traffic-engineering software minimizes is total vehicle travel time". True for many
  models, but appraisal (e.g. UK TAG, Australian ATAP, German BVWP) already values safety,
  environment, accessibility, distribution. Distinguish three levels:
  1. **model objective** (what the model computes or optimizes),
  2. **appraisal objective** (how outcomes are valued to compare projects),
  3. **societal / political objective** (what the city wants to become).
  The interesting problem is that the three are often inconsistent: ambitious climate and
  access goals, but car-capacity design standards and forecasting practice.
- [ ] **Acknowledge predecessors** in the introduction and/or About page:
  - David Banister, "The Sustainable Mobility Paradigm", *Transport Policy* (2008)
  - Karel Martens, *Transport Justice* (2017): sufficiency of access ↔ our A ≥ A_min
  - ITF, *Sustainable Accessibility for All* (2024)
  - EU Sustainable Urban Mobility Plans (SUMP guidelines, 2013, updated 2019)
- [ ] **What is already taught**, as counterexamples worth citing: MIT Transportation Systems
  Analysis (Ben-Akiva; discrete choice, pricing, evaluation), TU Delft Transport & Planning
  (incl. ethics, societal cost, safety), ETH Zürich Transport Planning Methods (four-step,
  agent-based, CBA), TU Dresden Cost-Benefit Analysis in Transport. The defensible claim:
  these subjects are **split across courses, specializations and institutions**; an engineer
  may know shockwaves but not transport justice, a planner the reverse.
- [ ] **Suggested central question** for the course:
  "Given the same transport problem, why do competent planners in different countries produce
  such different solutions? How much is explained by different objectives, constraints,
  models and institutional incentives, and how can we make those choices explicit enough
  to compare and improve them?"
- Suggested self-description: "an integrated, objective-first introduction to transport
  systems, combining welfare economics, engineering, comparative international practice and
  computational experimentation."

## Countries

- [ ] Countries are examples of **institutional choices**, not seven coherent philosophies.
  Dutch highway appraisal is not cycling planning; US cities vary hugely; Vision Zero is not a
  binding mathematical constraint on every Swedish investment. For each country, separate
  **stated objectives, appraisal procedures, design standards and observed outcomes**.
- [ ] Ask: why do countries claiming similar objectives build such different things?
  (institutions, incentives, historical commitments, political power)

## Strengths to build on

- Houston–Utrecht opening works because it starts from places and history, and is fair to
  Houston.
- **Simulation → welfare → optimizer → public decision** chain, with Goodhart and open tools:
  the reviewer's "potentially strongest distinctive angle". Expand it, not only as motivation
  for Module 10.
- **Sources & translations** (nine digests, seven languages): a concrete service; could make
  the course a reference people return to. Grow it.
